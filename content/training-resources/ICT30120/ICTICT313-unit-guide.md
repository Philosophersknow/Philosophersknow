## What This Unit Is About

The IT professional operates at the intersection of law and technology. Every day, IT workers handle personal data, access systems on behalf of others, manage software that is owned by third parties, and make decisions with ethical implications. ICTICT313 asks you to understand the legal and ethical framework that governs this work — and to apply it in practice.

This unit is not about memorising legislation. It is about understanding why these obligations exist and being able to identify when they apply in real-world ICT scenarios.

## Intellectual Property in ICT

### What Is Intellectual Property (IP)?

Intellectual property refers to creations of the mind — inventions, artistic works, designs, symbols, and names — that are protected by law. In ICT, the most relevant IP protection is **copyright**.

### Copyright and Software

Software (code) is protected by copyright under the **Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)**. Copyright protection is automatic — it does not need to be registered. The copyright owner has the exclusive right to:
- Copy the software
- Distribute copies
- Create derivative works (modifications, adaptations)
- License others to do these things

**Using software without a valid licence is copyright infringement** — commonly called piracy. This exposes both the individual and the organisation to civil liability and, in serious cases, criminal prosecution.

### Types of Software Licence

| Licence Type | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Proprietary / Commercial** | The copyright owner sells or licenses the software; terms restrict copying, redistribution, and modification |
| **Free (as in freedom) / Open-source** | The source code is available; can be used, modified, and distributed under the terms of the specific open-source licence |
| **Freeware** | Software that can be used at no cost but is NOT open-source — copyright restrictions may still apply to modification and redistribution |
| **Shareware** | Free to try; payment required for continued use |
| **SaaS (Software as a Service)** | Software accessed via subscription over the internet — licenced per user or per organisation, not installed locally |
| **Site licence** | An organisation purchases a licence to use software across all their devices or for all their users |
| **Per-seat / per-user licence** | Each individual user requires a separate licence |

### Open-Source Licensing — Key Types

| Licence | Key Obligation |
|---|---|
| **GPL (GNU General Public Licence)** | "Copyleft" — if you modify and distribute GPL software, your derivative work must also be released under GPL (source code must be made available) |
| **MIT Licence** | Permissive — can use, modify, and distribute freely with minimal restrictions (must include copyright notice) |
| **Apache Licence 2.0** | Permissive — similar to MIT; additionally provides an express grant of patent rights |
| **Creative Commons (for content)** | Various levels of restriction (Attribution, ShareAlike, NonCommercial, NoDerivatives) — applies to creative works, not typically software code |

**Why this matters for IT workers:** When using open-source libraries or components in a project, you must know the licence terms. A GPL component included in a commercial product may require the whole product's source code to be made available — which may be commercially unacceptable and legally required.

### Trademarks and Patents
- **Trademark:** Protects brand names, logos, and identifiers that distinguish products/services. Registered trademarks (®) provide stronger protection. Unregistered marks (™) have some common law protection.
- **Patent:** Protects inventions, including software patents in some jurisdictions. Patent law in Australia is less generous than in the US for software — but awareness is important when developing software products.

## Privacy Law and ICT

### Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)

The Privacy Act regulates how organisations handle **personal information** — information that identifies or could identify an individual.

**Who does it apply to?** The Act applies to:
- Australian Government agencies
- Organisations with an annual turnover of more than $3 million
- Some smaller organisations (health service providers, credit reporting bodies, etc.)

**The 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) — Summary for ICT Workers:**

| APP | Key Obligation |
|---|---|
| APP 1 | Manage personal information openly and transparently; have a privacy policy |
| APP 2 | Allow individuals to remain anonymous or use a pseudonym where possible |
| APP 3 | Collect only information reasonably necessary for the purpose |
| APP 4 | Deal carefully with unsolicited information; destroy it if not needed |
| APP 5 | At or before collection, notify individuals of how information will be used |
| APP 6 | Only use information for the purpose for which it was collected |
| APP 7 | Restrictions on use of personal information for direct marketing |
| APP 8 | Restrictions on cross-border disclosure of personal information |
| APP 9 | Adoption of government-related identifiers restricted |
| APP 10 | Keep personal information accurate, up-to-date, and complete |
| APP 11 | Take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, and unauthorised access |
| APP 12 | Give individuals access to their own personal information on request |
| APP 13 | Correct personal information that is inaccurate, incomplete, or misleading on request |

**For ICT workers, the most directly relevant APPs are 3 (minimum necessary), 6 (use only for collected purpose), and 11 (security).**

### Notifiable Data Breaches Scheme (NDB)

Under the Privacy Act, entities covered by the Act must notify the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and affected individuals when:
- There is a data breach
- It is likely to result in serious harm to the individuals whose information was involved

**As an IT worker, if you discover or suspect a data breach:**
1. Report it to your supervisor/security team immediately
2. Do not attempt to conceal or minimise it
3. Follow your organisation's incident response plan
4. Understand that delayed disclosure can significantly worsen the consequences

### State Privacy Legislation
Some states have their own privacy legislation covering state government agencies and sometimes private organisations. Understand which legislation applies to your employer.

## Cybersecurity Ethics and Law

### Cybercrime Act 2001 (Cth)

The Cybercrime Act makes it a criminal offence to:
- Access a computer or data without authorisation
- Modify data on a computer without authorisation
- Impair access to data or a computer
- Distribute malware

**Critical point for IT workers:** Legitimate access to a system does not authorise access to all data on that system. If your IT role gives you administrator access to a server, you are authorised to access what your role requires — not everything on the server. Accessing personal files, emails, or data of employees beyond what your role requires is potentially illegal.

### Whistleblowing and Disclosure

IT workers sometimes discover wrongdoing within their organisations — data being collected illegally, systems being used for fraud, privacy breaches being covered up. The **Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth)** (and equivalent state legislation) provides protections for people who disclose wrongdoing in good faith. Understanding these protections is relevant if you face a situation where disclosure is warranted.

## Ethical Practice in ICT

### Professional Ethics vs Legal Obligations

Being ethical in ICT goes beyond legal compliance. Professional ethics include:

- **Confidentiality:** Information encountered through your IT role is confidential — client data, network configurations, security vulnerabilities, internal communications
- **Honesty:** If you make a mistake or discover a problem, report it honestly — attempting to conceal errors creates greater harm
- **Competence:** Do not represent yourself as having skills you do not have; operate within your competence and escalate when a problem exceeds your capability
- **Non-maleficence:** Do not use your technical knowledge or access to harm others
- **Fairness:** Apply policies consistently; do not use system access to advantage some users over others

### Codes of Ethics in ICT
Several professional bodies publish codes of ethics for ICT practitioners:
- **Australian Computer Society (ACS) Code of Ethics:** Covers public interest, quality, honesty, competence, professional development, and professionalism
- **ISACA Code of Professional Ethics:** Relevant for cybersecurity and IT governance professionals
- **IEEE Code of Ethics:** International standard for computing and engineering professionals

## Social Media and ICT Ethics

Social media raises specific ethical issues for IT workers:

- **Company information:** Do not post confidential company information, client information, or system details on social media
- **Impersonation:** Do not represent yourself as acting officially for your employer unless authorised to do so
- **Harassment:** Do not use IT systems or professional access to harass others
- **Monitoring:** In many organisations, IT staff can see network traffic and communications — this access must be used only for legitimate purposes and not disclosed inappropriately

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are a junior IT support officer at a medical clinic. You are performing routine maintenance on the clinic's server and discover that a folder on a shared drive contains what appears to be the personal medical records of thousands of patients. The folder is accessible to all staff — including reception, billing, and administrative staff — without any access restriction. What should you concern yourself with and what do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This situation raises serious privacy and data security concerns under the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and the Health Records Act (or equivalent state legislation). Medical records are classified as **sensitive information** — the most protected category under the Privacy Act — and their unauthorised disclosure carries significant legal consequences.

**The problem:**
Patient medical records being accessible to all staff (regardless of whether those staff need access to them for their role) breaches:
- **APP 11** — the organisation has not taken reasonable steps to protect personal information from unauthorised access. Any staff member with access to the shared drive can view records they have no legitimate reason to see.
- **APP 6** — information collected for medical treatment purposes is being made accessible to billing and administrative staff who may not need it for their roles
- The **Minimum Necessary Principle** — access to sensitive information should be limited to those who genuinely need it

**What I do:**
1. **Do not access the records** — I would note their presence and the access issue, but I would not open individual records. Doing so beyond confirming the nature of the problem would itself be an unauthorised access issue.

2. **Report immediately to my supervisor and the practice manager** — this is not something I resolve on my own. It requires management action and potentially a review of all access permissions.

3. **Document what I found** — when I discovered the folder, its name, its apparent contents (medical records), and the access configuration — without opening individual files.

4. **Raise the Notifiable Data Breaches obligation** — if this has been an ongoing open access situation (which it appears to be), the organisation's privacy officer or the principal may need to assess whether a reportable data breach has occurred under the NDB scheme and whether the OAIC must be notified.

5. **Recommend remediation** — once management has been notified, recommend that access to the folder be restricted immediately to staff whose roles require access to patient records (clinical staff only, with appropriate authentication).

**What I would NOT do:** Fix the permissions myself without management authorisation (changes to access controls on systems containing sensitive information must be authorised and documented), or ignore the issue because "it's not my problem."

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies APP 11, the sensitive information category, and the NDB obligation. They correctly refuse to access the records, report immediately, document appropriately, and recommend remediation. They do not act unilaterally on a security issue of this significance. This is professional, legally aware practice. Strong Competent.
