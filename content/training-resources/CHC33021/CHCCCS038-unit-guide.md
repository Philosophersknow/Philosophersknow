## What This Unit Is About

Empowerment is one of the most misunderstood concepts in community services — and one of the most important. It does not mean giving people what they ask for. It does not mean making decisions on their behalf "for their own good." And it is not something you do *to* someone.

Empowerment means creating conditions in which people can identify and exercise their own power — over their choices, their self-expression, their relationships, their goals, and their participation in community life.

For a support worker, this means shifting from a mindset of *"I am here to help you"* to *"I am here so that you can do what matters to you."*

---

## Why This Unit Matters

People who use aged care, disability, or community services are at heightened risk of disempowerment. Systems, institutions, and well-meaning workers can — without intending to — strip people of agency by:

- Making decisions without adequate consultation
- Doing things *for* people that they could do themselves (with support)
- Treating people as passive recipients rather than active participants
- Ignoring preferences in favour of organisational efficiency
- Silencing people through language, communication barriers, or dismissiveness

CHCCCS038 asks you to actively work against these tendencies.

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Establish a supportive relationship

You need to:
- Build trust through consistent, respectful, and reliable interactions
- Demonstrate genuine interest in the person as an individual — their history, interests, goals, and identity
- Communicate in ways that match the person's communication needs and preferences
- Maintain appropriate professional boundaries while remaining warm and engaged

**What assessors look for:** This is not about being friendly. It is about demonstrating specific behaviours — active listening, using the person's preferred name and communication style, checking in about preferences, following through on commitments.

---

### Element 2: Promote independence and self-determination

You need to:
- Actively identify and build on the person's existing strengths and capabilities
- Provide the minimum assistance required — not the maximum available
- Support informed decision-making by providing clear, accessible information
- Respect decisions made by the person even when you disagree, within your duty of care obligations
- Recognise the concept of *dignity of risk* — the right of people to take reasonable risks in living their lives

**What assessors look for:** Evidence that you distinguish between what the person *can do* and what you do *instead of them* — and that you actively prioritise the former.

---

### Element 3: Support the person to advocate for themselves

You need to:
- Help the person understand their rights
- Provide information about advocacy services (formal and informal)
- Support the person to express their own views and concerns — including views about their own care
- Avoid speaking for the person when they can speak for themselves
- Recognise situations where a formal advocate may be needed (e.g., consent decisions, complaints, guardianship matters)

**What assessors look for:** The ability to distinguish between *supporting self-advocacy* (helping someone express their own views) and *advocacy on someone's behalf* (speaking for them when they cannot). Both have their place, but they are different and require different skills.

---

### Element 4: Identify and address barriers to empowerment

You need to:
- Recognise when environmental, attitudinal, systemic, or communication barriers are preventing a person from exercising their rights or making choices
- Take practical steps to address barriers within your scope
- Report barriers you cannot address to the appropriate person
- Contribute to a culture in your workplace that values empowerment

**Types of barriers to know:**
- **Attitudinal barriers**: negative assumptions about the capacity of older or disabled people; paternalism; ageism; ableism
- **Communication barriers**: language, literacy, cognitive impairment, speech disability, hearing loss
- **Environmental barriers**: physical inaccessibility; lack of appropriate technology; inflexible routines
- **Systemic barriers**: organisational policies that restrict choice; inadequate staffing; time pressures; rigid care routines

## Knowledge Evidence — What You Must Know

### Rights-based approaches
- The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) — particularly Article 12 (equal recognition before the law) and Article 19 (living independently and being included in the community)
- The Aged Care Quality and Safety Standards — Standard 1 (Consumer Dignity and Choice)
- Supported decision-making vs. substitute decision-making: when each applies
- The role of guardians, administrators, and enduring powers of attorney

### Empowerment frameworks
- Strengths-based practice: focus on what the person *can* do, not what they cannot
- Person-centred planning: the person drives the plan; services respond to the person
- Social model of disability: disability is created by barriers in society, not by the individual's impairment
- Recovery-oriented practice (relevant for mental health and dual disability)

### Diversity and empowerment
- Cultural safety: how cultural background, history, and experience affect a person's experience of services — particularly for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and people from CALD backgrounds
- The role of interpreter services: when to use them and how to access them
- Intersectionality: how gender, age, cultural background, disability, and socioeconomic status interact to shape a person's experience of empowerment or disempowerment

## Common Mistakes

**1. Confusing empowerment with agreement**

Empowerment does not mean going along with everything. If a person makes a decision that poses serious risk to themselves or others, your duty of care may require you to act. But the threshold for overriding a person's choice is high — much higher than many students assume. "I thought it was for their own good" is not empowerment; it is paternalism.

**2. Talking about the person without the person**

In any scenario where the person receiving support is present, the conversation should be *with* them, not *about* them. Discussing a client's needs with a family member while the client sits in the room is a common real-world failure of empowerment practice.

**3. Confusing 'doing things for people' with 'helping'**

Doing everything for a person — dressing them, making decisions for them, speaking for them — can feel like good care. It is often the opposite. Unless the person has no capacity for a particular activity, the goal is to support their participation, not to replace it.

**4. Vague strengths-based language**

"I used a strengths-based approach" is not evidence. What strengths did you identify? What specific things did you do to build on them? What did the person achieve as a result?

## Worked Example — Empowerment Scenario

**Prompt:**

*Margaret, 79, lives in residential aged care. She has mild dementia and uses a wheelchair. Her daughter visits regularly and often tells staff what Margaret "wants" and "needs" during these visits. Staff have noticed that Margaret rarely speaks during her daughter's visits and sometimes appears distressed afterward. Margaret has told two different care workers privately that she doesn't like some of the arrangements her daughter has made for her — particularly that she doesn't want to attend the facility's craft group, but her daughter has told staff it's important for Margaret to go.*

*Question: How would you respond to this situation to support Margaret's empowerment?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

The first step is to create a private opportunity to hear directly from Margaret, without her daughter present. People with dementia retain the capacity to express preferences even when their cognition is affected, and Margaret has already indicated to staff that she has views about her care. I would sit with Margaret at a time she is comfortable and use simple, direct questions: "Margaret, can we talk about the craft group? How do you feel about going?" I would use active listening and allow her time to respond without rushing.

If Margaret confirms she does not wish to attend the craft group, I would explain that her preferences matter and should be reflected in her support plan. I would support her to express this to the care coordinator — either directly or with me present as support — rather than speaking for her. If communication is difficult due to her dementia, I would explore whether there are communication aids or strategies that help her express herself more clearly.

Regarding her daughter's involvement: I would speak with the care coordinator about the pattern of staff receiving instructions from the daughter that may not reflect Margaret's wishes. Family involvement is important in aged care, but under the Aged Care Quality and Safety Standards (Standard 1, Consumer Dignity and Choice), Margaret — not her family — is the primary decision-maker for lifestyle preferences where she has capacity to decide. The facility has an obligation to ensure Margaret's voice is heard. A family meeting facilitated by the care coordinator or social worker may be appropriate.

I would also document my observations — that Margaret has expressed preferences inconsistent with her daughter's instructions, and that I have reported this to the care coordinator. This creates a record that the concern was raised and responded to.

**Examiner Annotation:** Excellent. The response demonstrates person-centred practice, communication adaptations for dementia, appropriate advocacy support (not substituting voice but enabling it), knowledge of the Aged Care Quality Standards, understanding of the distinction between family involvement and decision-making authority, appropriate escalation, and documentation. The student understands the difference between empowering Margaret and managing the situation.

---

**Insufficient Response:**

I would talk to the daughter and explain that Margaret doesn't seem happy. I'd try to find a balance between what the daughter wants and what Margaret wants. The daughter probably knows Margaret better than we do and wants what's best for her. I'd try to keep everyone happy.

**Examiner Annotation:** NYC. This response treats the daughter's views as equally or more valid than Margaret's own expressed preferences. It does not demonstrate understanding of the Aged Care Quality Standards, person-centred practice, or Margaret's right to make her own lifestyle choices. The student's approach of "keeping everyone happy" is a people-pleasing instinct, not an empowerment practice. Resubmit with specific reference to Margaret's rights, the role of the support plan, the Aged Care Quality Standards, and concrete steps to hear Margaret's direct voice.

## Scaffolding Questions

1. **What does this person already have?** (skills, knowledge, relationships, experience, preferences)
2. **What are they being prevented from doing or deciding** — and by what or whom?
3. **What information does this person need** to make an informed choice?
4. **What would it look like if this person's voice were truly heard** in this situation?
5. **Is this a situation where they need support to speak** — or where they have been spoken for without their consent?
6. **What would I do if I were in their position and wanted my rights respected?**
