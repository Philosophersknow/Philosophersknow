## What This Unit Is About

The people you will support in community services are as varied as the Australian population — diverse in age, cultural background, language, religion, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, and life experience. CHCDIV001 asks you to demonstrate that you can work respectfully and effectively with this diversity, and that you understand how your own cultural background and assumptions affect the way you work.

This unit is not primarily about knowledge of other cultures. It is about your capacity to reflect on your own perspective, recognise where it might create blind spots, and adapt your practice in response.

## Core Concepts

### Culture
Culture is the shared set of values, beliefs, practices, languages, and ways of being that are transmitted within and between generations of a group. Culture shapes:
- How people understand health, illness, and help-seeking
- Who is considered an appropriate decision-maker (individual? family? community elder?)
- What is considered respectful or disrespectful in communication
- Attitudes toward touch, eye contact, personal space, and privacy
- What is and is not discussed openly (mental health, sexuality, financial matters)

**Important:** Culture is not monolithic. People within a cultural group vary enormously. Knowing someone's cultural background is a starting point, not a blueprint. Always ask rather than assume.

### Cultural Safety
Cultural safety means that people from minority cultural groups feel safe — that their identity, culture, and values are respected, not diminished, in the service setting. Cultural safety is determined by the person receiving care, not by the person providing it.

A person is not culturally safe because you think you were respectful. They are culturally safe when they feel respected, understood, and able to participate fully.

### Cultural Competence
Cultural competence is a continuous learning process — developing the awareness, knowledge, and skills to work effectively across cultural differences. It is never "completed" — the more you learn, the more you recognise you do not know.

### Privilege and Power
In health and community services, the worker generally holds more institutional power than the person being supported. Cultural differences that intersect with this power imbalance (race, language, class, disability) can compound the vulnerability of the person. Recognising this dynamic is part of working ethically in this sector.

## First Nations People — Special Considerations

Working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in community services requires specific awareness:

- **Respect for cultural protocols:** Traditional roles, seniority, and gender-appropriate services vary by community and mob. Do not assume one-size-fits-all.
- **The impact of colonisation:** Many First Nations people have deeply held reasons to distrust government and government-funded services. This is not irrationality — it is a rational response to history, including the Stolen Generations, forced removal from country, and institutional abuse.
- **Family and community models of decision-making:** For many First Nations people, decisions about health and care appropriately involve family and community members, not just the individual.
- **Connection to country:** For many First Nations people, connection to country, community, and cultural practice is central to wellbeing — not a lifestyle preference that can be set aside.
- **Closing the Gap:** Acknowledge the health disparities that exist and understand that trauma-informed, culturally safe practice is part of addressing — not ignoring — these disparities.

## Diverse Communities in Community Services

While every individual is unique, support workers in Australian community services commonly work with people from:

- **CALD (Culturally and Linguistically Diverse) communities** — people born overseas or who speak a language other than English at home; includes large communities from South and Southeast Asia, East Asia, Middle East and North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Europe, and the Pacific
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities**
- **LGBTIQ+ communities** — people with diverse sexual orientations, gender identities, and intersex variations; face specific risks in care settings including fear of discrimination and loss of identity
- **People with disability** — intersects with all cultural identities
- **Older people** — age itself creates a specific cultural context; older people may hold values and worldviews that differ significantly from younger workers
- **People experiencing homelessness** — community and life experience shapes worldview and service interaction

## Reflecting on Your Own Culture and Bias

A central requirement of CHCDIV001 is self-reflection. Assessors are not looking for you to claim you have no biases — everyone does. They are looking for evidence that you can:

1. **Identify** your own cultural background and the values it has given you
2. **Recognise** how those values shape your assumptions about "normal" behaviour, family structure, health practices, etc.
3. **Acknowledge** that your perspective is not universal
4. **Commit** to setting aside assumptions and working with the person in front of you

**Example of self-reflection:** "I grew up in a household where asking for help was seen as weakness. I recognise this may make me assume that people who accept a lot of support 'prefer' dependency when in fact they may simply have different values around interdependence and community — and both are valid."

## Anti-discrimination Law — Summary

| Legislation | What It Protects |
|---|---|
| Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth) | Race, ethnicity, national origin, descent |
| Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth) | Sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, intersex status, relationship status, pregnancy, breastfeeding |
| Age Discrimination Act 2004 (Cth) | Age (applies to older AND younger people) |
| Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth) | Physical, intellectual, psychiatric, sensory, learning, and neurological disability |
| Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986 (Cth) | Religion, political opinion, social origin (in employment) |
| State/territory legislation | Adds further protections (varies by jurisdiction) |

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are a support worker visiting Amara, a 72-year-old woman from Ethiopia who has been in Australia for 8 years. Amara has a home care package. Her English is limited. Her daughter, who lives with her, has been acting as interpreter during all care visits. On this visit, you need to discuss a change in Amara's care plan that involves a male personal care worker taking over some shifts. The daughter says her mother will not accept this. The daughter says it is "cultural" and that her mother has told her she would rather not have care than have a male worker come to the house.*

*How do you respond to this situation?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This situation requires me to centre Amara's preferences and cultural values, not my own assumptions about what the care plan should look like.

First, the daughter's communication on her mother's behalf raises two issues I need to think about carefully. Using a family member as interpreter for significant care planning conversations is not best practice — family members may filter, interpret, or add to what is said based on their own perspective. For a conversation of this significance, I should explore whether an accredited interpreter service can be arranged for future conversations. However, I acknowledge this is a systemic resource question, not something I can resolve on this visit.

Regarding the substance: Amara's preference is clear. She does not want a male personal care worker. This is a preference that:
- Falls within her right to choose her own support
- Has a clear cultural and potentially religious basis (many women from Muslim-majority communities, and others, hold preferences about gender-based personal care)
- Should be documented and respected — not challenged

My role is not to determine whether her reasons are "valid enough." My role is to document her preference, communicate it through the correct channels, and ensure the care coordinator and my organisation know that this preference must be accommodated in rostering.

I would:
1. Thank Amara and her daughter for communicating this clearly
2. Assure them that I will document the preference and ensure it is reflected in Amara's care plan
3. Follow up with my supervisor and the care coordinator to ensure male workers are not rostered for Amara's personal care shifts
4. Note in the record that the family has raised this preference and that it aligns with Amara's cultural values — without including any judgement about the preference

If Amara's preference makes it difficult to provide continuity of care (for example, limited female workers are available on certain shifts), this is a rostering and resource challenge for my organisation to solve — not a reason to tell Amara she must compromise her values.

**Examiner Annotation:** This response demonstrates cultural safety in practice: the student centres Amara's preference, does not challenge or attempt to change it, identifies the interpreter issue as a systemic concern rather than a personal failing, and knows the correct course of action (document, report, ensure accommodation). The student also correctly identifies that the burden of accommodation falls on the organisation, not on Amara. Strong Competent.
