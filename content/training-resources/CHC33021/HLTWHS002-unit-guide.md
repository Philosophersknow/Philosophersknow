## What This Unit Is About

Health and community services is physically and emotionally demanding work. Workers support people with complex needs in sometimes unpredictable settings. The risks are real — manual handling injuries, infection, exposure to hazardous substances, aggressive behaviour, and psychological stress are all present in this sector.

HLTWHS002 asks you to demonstrate that you understand workplace health and safety (WHS) obligations in direct care settings — and that you can apply safe work practices consistently. This protects you, protects the people you support, and protects your organisation.

---

## The Legal Framework

### Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Cth)
The model WHS Act (adopted in most states and territories) establishes the primary legislative framework. Key provisions:

- **Primary duty of care:** The employer (Person Conducting a Business or Undertaking — PCBU) must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health, safety, and welfare of workers and others.
- **Worker duties:** Workers must take reasonable care for their own health and safety and not adversely affect others; must comply with reasonable instructions and cooperate with WHS policies and procedures.
- **Right to cease unsafe work:** Workers have the right to cease or refuse to carry out unsafe work — and must not be penalised for doing so.

### Regulators
Each state and territory has a WHS regulator (SafeWork NSW, WorkSafe Victoria, Workplace Health and Safety Queensland, etc.). They set and enforce WHS standards.

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Follow WHS procedures in the workplace

You need to:
- Access and apply your organisation's WHS policies and procedures
- Know the location of your WHS information, emergency procedures, and relevant contacts
- Participate in WHS induction, training, and briefings provided by your organisation
- Report any WHS concerns through the correct channels

---

### Element 2: Identify and report hazards

You need to:
- Recognise hazards in the care environment (physical, chemical, ergonomic, psychological, biological)
- Use your organisation's hazard reporting system (typically a hazard report form or system)
- Not wait to report — report hazards as soon as they are identified
- Know the difference between a hazard, a risk, and an incident

**Definitions:**
- **Hazard:** Something with the potential to cause harm (e.g., a wet floor, an unsecured dog, a client who has been known to be physically aggressive)
- **Risk:** The likelihood and severity of harm actually occurring from the hazard
- **Incident:** An event that has caused (or could have caused) harm; requires documentation and reporting

---

### Element 3: Manage the risks of manual tasks

You need to:
- Understand the risks of manual handling in direct care (the leading cause of injury in the sector)
- Apply safe manual handling principles
- Use mechanical aids and assistive equipment appropriately (hoists, transfer boards, sit-to-stand lifts)
- Know when a task exceeds your safe capability or the scope of your role — and stop and seek assistance
- Never perform a manual handling task alone if the care plan specifies two-person assist

---

### Element 4: Apply standard precautions for infection control

You need to:
- Understand the chain of infection and how to break it
- Apply standard precautions consistently (regardless of whether a person has a known infection)
- Use personal protective equipment (PPE) correctly — donning and doffing order matters
- Follow hand hygiene protocols (the single most effective infection control measure)
- Follow your organisation's procedures for management of blood, body fluids, sharps, and soiled linen

---

### Element 5: Participate in workplace incident response and reporting

You need to:
- Know what constitutes a reportable incident in your setting
- Document incidents accurately and promptly
- Know the escalation pathway for incidents (supervisor → manager → regulator, depending on severity)
- Participate in post-incident review processes

## Manual Handling — What You Must Know

Manual handling injuries (primarily musculoskeletal injuries to the back, neck, and shoulders) are the most common workplace injuries in community services. They are largely preventable.

### Risk Factors for Manual Handling Injury
- Heavy or awkward loads
- Repetitive movements
- Sustained postures (bending, twisting, reaching)
- Lack of mechanical aids
- Rushing, fatigue, or inadequate training
- Inadequate assistance (single-worker transfer when two are needed)

### Safe Manual Handling Principles
1. **Assess before you act** — know the person's abilities, weight, and the task requirements before you start
2. **Use equipment** — hoists, transfer belts, slide sheets, and other aids exist to reduce the biomechanical load; use them
3. **Position yourself** — stand close to the load, feet shoulder-width apart, stable base
4. **Bend knees, not your back** — keep your spine in neutral alignment, use your legs to lift
5. **Do not twist** — turn your whole body with your feet, not just your upper body
6. **Communicate** — with the person, with any co-worker, so movements are coordinated and the person is not surprised
7. **Stop if it feels unsafe** — if the task does not match what you expected (person heavier than assessed, equipment unavailable, environment too cramped), stop and seek assistance

## Infection Control — The Chain of Infection

Infection requires all six links in the chain to be present. Breaking any link prevents transmission.

| Link | Description | Break It By... |
|---|---|---|
| **Infectious agent** | The pathogen (bacteria, virus, fungus, parasite) | Cleaning and disinfecting surfaces |
| **Reservoir** | Where the pathogen lives (a person, equipment, surface, water) | Proper cleaning; isolating infected persons |
| **Portal of exit** | How it leaves the reservoir (cough, blood, stool) | PPE; covering wounds; respiratory hygiene |
| **Mode of transmission** | How it moves (contact, droplet, airborne) | Hand hygiene; PPE; distancing |
| **Portal of entry** | How it enters the new host (mucous membranes, breaks in skin) | PPE; covering your own wounds |
| **Susceptible host** | A person at risk of infection | Supporting immunity; standard precautions |

### Standard Precautions

Standard precautions apply to **all** people in **all** care settings, regardless of whether a known infection is present. They include:

- **Hand hygiene:** Before and after every client contact, before and after donning PPE, after contact with potentially contaminated surfaces
- **PPE:** Gloves for any contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin; mask and eye protection for splash/spray risk; gown/apron for risk of soiling
- **Sharps safety:** Never recap used needles; dispose in sharps container immediately after use; report needlestick injuries immediately
- **Linen and waste management:** Soiled linen into designated bags without shaking; clinical waste into correct bins
- **Environmental cleaning:** Clean and disinfect shared equipment between uses

### Hand Hygiene — The 5 Moments (WHO Standard)

1. Before touching a client
2. Before a clean/aseptic procedure
3. After body fluid exposure risk
4. After touching a client
5. After touching client surroundings

**Method:** Either soap and water (minimum 20 seconds, including all surfaces of hands) or alcohol-based hand rub (cover all surfaces, rub until dry). Soap and water is required when hands are visibly soiled or when Clostridioides difficile (C. diff) is suspected.

## Psychological Health and Safety

Community services work carries significant psychological risk — vicarious trauma, emotional exhaustion, moral distress, and burnout are real and documented in this sector. A safe workplace addresses psychological safety as well as physical safety.

**Signs of burnout and compassion fatigue:**
- Emotional exhaustion — feeling drained regardless of rest
- Depersonalisation — feeling detached from the people you support, going through the motions
- Reduced sense of accomplishment — feeling your work makes no difference
- Physical symptoms — headaches, insomnia, lowered immunity

**What you should know:**
- Your employer has obligations to support your psychological wellbeing, including appropriate supervision and access to Employee Assistance Programs (EAP)
- Seek supervision proactively — not just when something goes wrong
- You are not expected to absorb unlimited emotional distress without support
- Recognising your own limits is professional, not weak

## Common Mistakes

**1. Confusing hazard with risk**

These are not the same word for the same thing. A wet floor is a hazard. The risk is that someone slips and falls. Your assessment task will often ask you to identify the hazard, then assess and manage the risk. Getting these terms wrong throughout suggests a fundamental misunderstanding of WHS concepts.

---

**2. Not applying the hierarchy of controls**

When asked how to manage a hazard, students frequently jump straight to PPE (personal protective equipment). PPE is the lowest level of control — it protects the worker but does not remove or reduce the hazard. The hierarchy runs: elimination → substitution → isolation → engineering controls → administrative controls → PPE. Always consider whether higher-level controls are possible before defaulting to PPE.

---

**3. Ignoring psychosocial hazards**

WHS is not just about physical injury. Psychosocial hazards (workload, client aggression, isolation, vicarious trauma) are real and legally recognised hazards. Assessment responses that address only physical hazards are incomplete.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You arrive at Helen's home for a morning care visit. Helen has early-stage dementia and usually mobilises independently with a four-wheel walker. Today you find her on the floor in the hallway. She is conscious and says she has been there for "a while." She is distressed. She says her right hip hurts and she cannot stand up. How do you respond, and what WHS obligations are relevant here?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

My immediate priorities are Helen's safety and getting her appropriate help.

**Immediate response:**
I would not attempt to lift Helen from the floor. A person who cannot stand up after a fall, with hip pain, may have a fractured hip. Attempting to lift her without appropriate equipment and assessment risks serious further injury — both to her and to me.

I would:
1. Call emergency services (000) — hip pain after a fall in an older person with suspected fracture is a medical emergency
2. Stay with Helen, speak to her calmly to reduce her distress, and keep her warm with a blanket until help arrives
3. Call my supervisor immediately and advise them of the situation
4. If safe to do so (Helen is not in immediate danger, the area is safe), gather relevant information — when did she fall, does she have any relevant medical history, who is her GP and next of kin
5. Document everything — time of arrival, what I found, what she told me, what I did, who I contacted and when

**What I do NOT do:**
- Attempt to lift or move Helen without appropriate assistance and equipment
- Leave her alone
- Wait before calling emergency services "to see how she goes"

**WHS obligations:**
As a worker, I have a duty of care to Helen (not to cause her further harm) and to myself (not to attempt an unsafe lift). My employer's WHS procedures should guide this situation — most organisations have specific procedures for falls in a home care setting that I would be expected to follow.

This is also a reportable incident. I would complete an incident report as soon as practicable after the immediate emergency is managed, documenting the facts of what I found and what occurred.

**After the immediate emergency:**
I would raise with my supervisor whether there are risk factors in Helen's home that need to be reviewed — is the walker in good condition? Is there a falls prevention plan in place? Is the hallway hazard-free? A near-miss or fall like this should trigger a risk review, not just an incident report.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student knows not to attempt an unsafe lift, calls emergency services immediately, communicates with the supervisor, stays with Helen, identifies the documentation requirement, and connects the incident to systemic risk review. This is exactly the response a competent and safe worker would give.
