## What This Unit Is About

To provide effective support, a community services worker needs a working knowledge of the human body — how it functions when healthy, what changes indicate dysfunction, and what those changes mean for the people you support.

CHCCCS041 is not asking you to diagnose illness. It is asking you to recognise what is normal for the person you support, notice when something has changed, understand what body system is likely involved, and report appropriately. The clinical decisions belong to health professionals — the observation, recognition, and reporting belong to you.

## The Body Systems You Need to Know

### 1. Cardiovascular System
**Function:** Pumps blood around the body, delivering oxygen and nutrients, removing waste.

**Key structures:** Heart, arteries, veins, capillaries, blood.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Regular heartbeat (pulse: 60–100 bpm at rest for adults)
- Normal blood pressure (typically 120/80 mmHg — varies by individual)
- Skin warm and normal colour for the individual
- No unusual swelling in limbs

**Signs of concern:**
- Chest pain or tightness (possible cardiac event — escalate immediately)
- Irregular or very fast/slow pulse
- Swelling in ankles or legs (possible fluid retention / heart failure)
- Pale, bluish, or grey skin tone (cyanosis)
- Shortness of breath at rest or with minimal activity

---

### 2. Respiratory System
**Function:** Brings oxygen into the body, removes carbon dioxide.

**Key structures:** Nose, mouth, trachea, bronchi, lungs, diaphragm.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Breathing is quiet, effortless, and regular
- Normal respiratory rate: 12–20 breaths per minute (adult)
- No pain with breathing

**Signs of concern:**
- Breathing is laboured, noisy (wheezing, rattling), or very rapid
- Respiratory rate significantly above or below normal
- Blue tinge to lips or fingernails
- Person is unable to complete a sentence without stopping to breathe
- Persistent cough (especially with discoloured mucus)
- Any complaint of difficulty breathing

---

### 3. Musculoskeletal System
**Function:** Provides structure, enables movement, protects organs.

**Key structures:** Bones, muscles, tendons, ligaments, joints, cartilage.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Ability to move joints through full range of motion without pain
- Muscle tone and strength adequate for daily activities
- No deformity of limbs or joints

**Signs of concern:**
- Pain on movement or at rest
- Swelling, redness, or warmth around joints (possible inflammation or infection)
- Reduced range of motion compared to the person's baseline
- Muscle wasting (atrophy) — loss of muscle bulk
- Falls or near-falls (loss of coordination or muscle strength)
- Fracture signs: severe pain, deformity, inability to bear weight

---

### 4. Nervous System
**Function:** Controls the body's functions and responses; enables thought, sensation, and movement.

**Key structures:** Brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Person is alert, oriented to person, place, and time
- Movements are coordinated and purposeful
- Speech is clear and coherent

**Signs of concern:**
- Sudden confusion, disorientation, or behavioural change (especially in a person who is usually clear)
- Weakness or numbness in limbs (one-sided weakness is a stroke warning sign)
- Difficulty speaking or understanding speech (stroke warning sign)
- Loss of consciousness or seizure (escalate immediately)
- Facial drooping
- Severe, sudden headache described as "worst ever"

**Stroke recognition — FAST:**
- **F**ace: drooping on one side
- **A**rms: one arm drifts down when both are raised
- **S**peech: slurred or confused
- **T**ime: call emergency services immediately

---

### 5. Digestive System
**Function:** Breaks down food, absorbs nutrients, eliminates waste.

**Key structures:** Mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, pancreas.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Regular bowel patterns normal for the individual (range is wide — daily to 3 times per week is within normal range)
- Appetite consistent with baseline
- No persistent pain in abdomen

**Signs of concern:**
- Significant change in bowel habits (more frequent, less frequent, change in consistency)
- Blood in stool (bright red or very dark — black tarry stools suggest upper GI bleed)
- Persistent nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain
- Significant unexplained weight loss
- Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) — risk of aspiration

---

### 6. Integumentary System (Skin)
**Function:** Protects the body, regulates temperature, provides sensation.

**Key structures:** Skin, hair, nails, sweat glands.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Skin is intact (no breaks, wounds, or pressure areas)
- Skin colour normal for the individual
- Skin is appropriately hydrated (not excessively dry or moist)
- Nails are intact and clean

**Signs of concern:**
- Redness, especially over bony prominences (heels, sacrum, hips) — possible pressure injury developing
- Skin breaks, wounds, ulcers, or open areas
- Rashes, unusual markings, or bruising (especially where unexplained — safeguarding concern)
- Yellow tinge to skin or eyes (jaundice — liver concern)
- Excessively dry skin (often associated with dehydration or diabetes)
- Signs of infection in any wound (redness, warmth, swelling, pus, odour)

---

### 7. Urinary System
**Function:** Filters waste from blood, produces and eliminates urine, maintains fluid balance.

**Key structures:** Kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra.

**Signs of healthy function:**
- Regular urination pattern normal for the individual
- Urine is pale yellow to straw-coloured
- No pain or burning with urination

**Signs of concern:**
- Dark, concentrated urine (may indicate dehydration)
- Red or pink urine (blood in urine — report immediately)
- Pain or burning with urination (UTI symptoms)
- Frequency (much more frequent than usual) or difficulty urinating
- Foul-smelling urine (UTI symptom — especially important in older adults where UTI can cause confusion)
- Urinary incontinence that is new or worsening

---

### 8. Endocrine System
**Function:** Produces hormones that regulate body processes including blood sugar, metabolism, growth, and stress response.

**Key structures:** Pancreas (insulin/glucagon), thyroid, adrenal glands, pituitary.

**Relevance to support workers:**
The most common endocrine condition you will encounter is **diabetes** (both Type 1 and Type 2). Understanding blood sugar regulation is essential.

**Hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar) — Common with insulin-dependent diabetes:**
- Symptoms: shakiness, sweating, confusion, pallor, rapid heartbeat, hunger
- Can deteriorate rapidly — treat as emergency if person loses consciousness
- If person is conscious and can swallow safely: give a fast-acting sugar source (e.g., glucose tablets, orange juice, regular (not diet) soft drink)
- Report and document; person may need medication adjustment

**Hyperglycaemia (high blood sugar):**
- Symptoms: excessive thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision, headache
- Usually develops more slowly than hypoglycaemia
- Report to health professional; do not adjust medication yourself

## Vital Signs — What You Need to Know

| Vital Sign | Normal Adult Range | What to Report |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 36.1°C – 37.2°C | Above 38°C (fever) or below 36°C (hypothermia) |
| Pulse (heart rate) | 60–100 bpm | Below 60 or above 100; irregular rhythm |
| Respiratory rate | 12–20 breaths/min | Below 12 or above 20; any difficulty breathing |
| Blood pressure | ~120/80 mmHg (individual baseline varies) | Significant deviation from person's baseline |
| Oxygen saturation (SpO2) | 95–100% | Below 94% — escalate; below 90% — emergency |

**Important:** You are not responsible for recording all vital signs in every setting — this depends on your role, the care plan, and the organisation's policies. But you do need to know what normal looks like so you can recognise and report when something looks wrong.

## Reporting Changes — Your Role

As a support worker, you are often the person who knows the individual best and sees them most frequently. This makes you the first line of observation.

**When you observe a change:**
1. Do not dismiss it as "probably nothing" — if it is a change from the person's baseline, it is worth reporting
2. Note what you observed, when, and in what context
3. If the concern is acute (breathing difficulty, chest pain, stroke symptoms, loss of consciousness, severe pain) — call emergency services first, then notify your supervisor
4. For non-emergency changes, report through your organisation's established channels (supervisor, care coordinator, registered nurse or clinician if available in your setting)
5. Document your observation factually and objectively: what you saw, heard, or measured — not your diagnosis

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are supporting Elvira, 78, who has Type 2 diabetes and lives at home. When you arrive for her morning visit you find her sitting at the kitchen table. She looks pale and is shaking slightly. She seems confused — she calls you by the wrong name and says she can't remember if she has eaten breakfast. Her skin is damp with sweat. What do you do, and what are you thinking about in terms of body systems?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

My immediate concern is hypoglycaemia — low blood sugar. Elvira's presentation is highly consistent with this: shakiness, pallor, sweating, confusion, and uncertainty about whether she has eaten. In people with diabetes who use insulin or certain oral medications, blood sugar can drop dangerously, particularly if a meal has been missed or delayed.

**Immediate action:**
Because Elvira is confused but conscious and likely still able to swallow, I would give her a fast-acting sugar source — 150–200ml of orange juice, a regular (not diet) soft drink, or glucose tablets if she has them. I would stay with her and not leave her alone.

I would then call my supervisor and the care coordinator to report what I found, what I did, and Elvira's current status. Depending on the organisation's procedures, this may also trigger contact with the GP or a diabetes nurse.

If Elvira loses consciousness or is unable to swallow, I would call emergency services (000) immediately and not attempt to give her anything by mouth.

**Body systems involved:**
- **Endocrine system:** Diabetes affects the pancreas's ability to regulate blood glucose; hypoglycaemia is a direct consequence of this disruption
- **Nervous system:** The brain is highly dependent on glucose; low blood sugar causes confusion and altered consciousness
- **Cardiovascular system:** Sweating and rapid heart rate are the body's stress response to low blood sugar

After Elvira stabilises, I would document the episode — including the time of my arrival, her presentation, what I gave her, and her response — and ensure this is communicated to her care team, as it may indicate that her medication or meal schedule needs review.

I would not assume this was just "one of those mornings." A hypoglycaemic episode requires follow-up by a health professional.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student identifies the likely cause (hypoglycaemia) correctly and quickly, knows the immediate response (fast-acting sugar, do not leave), knows the escalation threshold (loss of consciousness → emergency services), correctly identifies the body systems involved and explains the connections, and prioritises documentation and referral. This is safe practice.
