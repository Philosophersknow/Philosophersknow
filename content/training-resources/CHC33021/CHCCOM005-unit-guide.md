## What This Unit Is About

Communication is not a soft skill in community services — it is a core professional competency. This unit asks you to demonstrate that you can communicate effectively with the people you support, with their families and carers, and with the health and community services workforce. It also asks you to understand the documentation systems, communication channels, and interprofessional practices that keep people safe.

Miscommunication in health and community services has serious consequences. Missed information, poorly written progress notes, assumptions that were never checked — these lead to adverse events, safeguarding failures, and breakdowns in care. Communication in this sector has to be precise, timely, and in the right channel for the right purpose.

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Communicate effectively with people

You need to:
- Use communication skills appropriate to the person's needs, background, and preferences
- Apply active listening — not just waiting for your turn to speak
- Use open and closed questions purposefully
- Recognise and respond to non-verbal communication
- Adapt your communication when working with people who have hearing impairment, vision impairment, communication difficulty, or cognitive impairment
- Maintain the dignity of the person in all communication

---

### Element 2: Collaborate with colleagues and other professionals

You need to:
- Communicate effectively with supervisors, colleagues, and other team members
- Understand your scope of role and communicate within it
- Participate constructively in team meetings and handover processes
- Contribute to handover communication accurately and concisely
- Know when to escalate concerns to a supervisor or other professional

---

### Element 3: Address constraints to communication

You need to:
- Identify barriers to effective communication in health and community services settings
- Adapt your approach when standard communication is not effective
- Use interpreters, communication aids, and alternative communication methods appropriately
- Recognise when cultural factors affect communication and respond appropriately

---

### Element 4: Report and document information

You need to:
- Record information accurately, objectively, and in the appropriate format
- Report significant information through the correct channels in a timely way
- Understand the difference between observations, inferences, and opinions in documentation
- Follow your organisation's documentation policies and requirements
- Understand the legal and ethical dimensions of records — confidentiality, privacy, and accuracy

## Communication Skills — What Assessors Are Looking For

### Active Listening

Active listening is demonstrated behaviour, not just attention. It includes:
- Maintaining appropriate eye contact
- Not interrupting
- Using verbal and non-verbal acknowledgment ("I see," "yes," nodding)
- Reflecting back what the person has said: "So what I'm hearing is..."
- Asking for clarification rather than assuming
- Noticing what is not being said (body language, tone, pauses)

### Types of Questions

| Question Type | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | To invite the person to share in their own words | "How have you been feeling this week?" |
| **Closed** | To confirm specific information | "Did you have breakfast this morning?" |
| **Reflective** | To show you have understood and invite elaboration | "It sounds like you've been feeling quite lonely lately — can you tell me more about that?" |
| **Clarifying** | To check your understanding | "When you say you're feeling 'not right,' can you tell me more about what that feels like?" |

**Avoid:** Leading questions ("You're feeling better today, aren't you?") — these can shut down communication and produce inaccurate information.

---

### Non-verbal Communication

In community services, you need to read and respond to non-verbal cues. This matters especially when working with people who have difficulty communicating verbally.

**Non-verbal cues to observe:**
- Facial expression (grimacing, tearfulness, flat affect)
- Body posture (tensed, withdrawn, relaxed)
- Eye contact (avoiding, glazed, alert)
- Movement (restless, reluctant to move, guarding a body part)
- Touch response (flinching, leaning in)

**Your own non-verbal communication matters too.** Approaching a person from above (standing over them while they sit), crossing your arms, speaking with a rushed tone, or checking your phone during an interaction communicates disrespect regardless of the words you use.

---

### Communication Adaptations

| Communication Need | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Hearing impairment | Face the person, speak clearly at a normal pace, don't shout, use written communication if helpful, ensure hearing aids are in and working |
| Vision impairment | Identify yourself when you enter, describe what you are doing, give verbal information rather than gestures |
| Cognitive impairment (dementia) | Use simple sentences, one idea at a time, repeat if needed using same words, use the person's name, allow extra time to respond |
| Communication disorder (e.g., aphasia, dysarthria) | Allow time, don't finish sentences, yes/no questions if needed, communication aids |
| Language difference | Use accredited interpreters for significant health/care conversations; visual aids; plain language |

## Documentation — What Good Practice Looks Like

### The Purpose of Documentation

Documentation in health and community services is:
- A legal record
- A communication tool between team members and across shifts
- Evidence that care was provided
- A safety mechanism — changes documented become visible; changes not documented are lost

### Principles of Good Documentation

**Factual:** Record what you observed, heard, or measured. Not what you think it means.

**Objective:** Avoid language that judges, assumes, or diagnoses. "Client appeared distressed, crying, and refused to engage with personal care" is objective. "Client was being difficult again" is not.

**Accurate:** Do not exaggerate or minimise. Record what actually happened.

**Timely:** Document as close to the event as possible. Memory is unreliable, especially across a busy shift.

**Legible and clear:** Even in digital systems, records must be readable and unambiguous.

**In the correct format:** Follow your organisation's documentation policies — different information goes in different places (progress notes, incident reports, medication records, care plans).

---

### Observation vs Inference vs Opinion

This distinction is critical and frequently tested in assessment.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | What you directly see, hear, or measure | "Client did not eat lunch. Half a cup of tea consumed." |
| **Inference** | A reasonable conclusion drawn from what you observed | "Client may have reduced appetite" |
| **Opinion** | A personal judgement | "Client doesn't want to get better" |

**In documentation, lead with observations.** You may include a careful inference if it is clearly labelled as such ("This may suggest...") and relevant. Opinions have no place in formal records.

---

### Handover Communication

Handover (shift handover, care handover between services) is one of the highest-risk moments in health and community care. Information lost at handover has caused preventable harm.

**Effective handover includes:**
- What happened this shift that is relevant to the next shift
- Any changes in the person's condition, behaviour, or presentation
- Anything not completed that needs follow-up
- Concerns that need monitoring or escalation

**Structure tool: ISBAR** (used in many health and community settings)

| Letter | Stands for | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Identify | Who are you? Who are you talking about? |
| **S** | Situation | What is the current situation / concern? |
| **B** | Background | What is the relevant background? |
| **A** | Assessment | What do you think is happening? |
| **R** | Recommendation | What do you think needs to happen? |

## Common Mistakes

**1. Documenting opinions as facts**

"Mrs Chen is being stubborn and refusing to cooperate with personal care" tells a reader what you think of Mrs Chen, not what happened. "Mrs Chen declined personal care on three occasions this shift, saying she was 'too tired.' She accepted a wash of hands and face" is what happened. Assessors consistently flag subjective, judgmental documentation as a NYC.

---

**2. Failing to distinguish communication with the person from communication with the team**

What you say to a person you support (direct communication) and what you write in a progress note (professional record) serve different purposes and have different requirements. Mixing these up — writing in a progress note as though you are talking to the person, or conversely speaking to the person in bureaucratic clinical language — suggests you have not understood either function.

---

**3. Not adapting communication**

Describing a single communication approach regardless of who you are communicating with (a person with dementia, a hearing-impaired person, a colleague at handover, a family member) suggests the student has not understood that communication is context-specific. Assessors want to see that you can adapt.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You support Kofi, 65, who has had a stroke and now has aphasia — he finds it difficult to find words and speaks in short phrases. He can understand most of what is said to him but takes time to process. During your visit, he seems upset — his face is tense, he is not making eye contact, and he has refused his morning medication. Write a progress note documenting this visit, and describe how you would communicate with Kofi about what is happening.*

---

**Exemplary Response — Progress Note:**

> **Progress Note — [Date/Time/Worker Name]**
>
> Arrived at Kofi's home at 09:15. Kofi was seated in living room. He appeared distressed — facial expression was tense, avoided eye contact throughout the visit, and did not initiate conversation.
>
> Kofi declined morning medication when offered. He did not provide a verbal reason. When asked using yes/no questions whether he was in pain, he shook his head. When asked whether something was worrying him, he nodded and said "son... no phone." This was interpreted as a concern about his son not calling.
>
> Medication was not administered. Supervisor notified at 09:35 of Kofi's refusal and presentation. Supervisor has noted concern for follow-up.
>
> Kofi accepted a cup of tea and appeared slightly calmer by the end of the visit. No other incidents noted.
>
> Reported to: [Supervisor name], 09:35.
> Next visit: [date/time].

---

**Communication approach with Kofi:**

Given Kofi's aphasia, I would adapt my communication in the following ways:

- Allow extra time for Kofi to respond — not fill the silence or finish his sentences
- Use short, simple sentences with one idea at a time: "How are you feeling today?" rather than "How are you feeling, and did you sleep well, and are you ready for your medication?"
- Use closed yes/no questions where an open question is too demanding: "Are you in pain? Yes or no?"
- Use non-verbal cues actively — if he nods or shakes his head or gestures, I acknowledge this as valid communication
- Maintain calm, unhurried body language — sitting at his level, not standing over him
- Not assume he does not understand because he is struggling to speak — comprehension and expression are separate functions; Kofi likely understands more than he can say

I would not rush through the visit or push the medication, and I would report the refusal promptly because medication refusal is clinically significant.

**Examiner Annotation:** Excellent. The progress note is factual, objective, and includes only what was observed and what happened — no opinions or judgements. The student documents the medication refusal correctly (including supervisor notification). The communication description shows genuine understanding of aphasia and appropriate adaptations. Strong Competent.
