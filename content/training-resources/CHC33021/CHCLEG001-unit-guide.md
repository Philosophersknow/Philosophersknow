## What This Unit Is About

This unit underpins everything you do as a community services or health worker. Legal and ethical practice is not a separate part of your role — it is the foundation of every interaction, every decision, and every piece of documentation.

CHCLEG001 addresses three interconnected areas:
1. **Legal obligations** — what the law requires you to do (or not do)
2. **Ethical practice** — how professional values guide decision-making when the law alone is not enough
3. **Workplace policies and procedures** — how your organisation translates legal and ethical requirements into day-to-day practice

---

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Identify and respond to legal requirements

You need to:
- Know the key legislation affecting your role (see knowledge evidence section)
- Understand your legal duties, including duty of care, mandatory reporting, and confidentiality
- Recognise when a situation has legal implications and respond appropriately
- Know when to seek guidance or escalate rather than act alone

---

### Element 2: Identify and respond to ethical frameworks

You need to:
- Understand the core ethical principles of community services practice
- Recognise ethical dilemmas and use a systematic approach to think them through
- Apply professional codes of conduct (your organisation's and sector-wide)
- Know the difference between personal values and professional ethical obligations

---

### Element 3: Recognise and respond to ethical and legal risks

You need to:
- Identify situations that create legal or ethical risk for yourself, the client, or your organisation
- Know how to respond to suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation
- Report concerns through appropriate channels — internal and external
- Understand the protections available to workers who report concerns in good faith (whistleblower protections)

---

### Element 4: Reflect on own ethical conduct

You need to:
- Recognise when your own values, beliefs, or attitudes could compromise professional practice
- Seek supervision or support when facing ethical challenges
- Continuously develop your understanding of legal and ethical obligations through professional development

## Core Ethical Principles

| Principle | Meaning in Practice |
|---|---|
| Autonomy | People have the right to make their own decisions |
| Beneficence | Act in the person's best interests |
| Non-maleficence | Do no harm — avoid actions that cause harm |
| Justice | Treat people fairly and equitably |
| Fidelity | Keep promises; be reliable and trustworthy |
| Veracity | Be honest; do not deceive |

**The tension between principles is where ethics gets hard.**

Example: A client tells you they have been saving their pain medication to take all at once. Autonomy says it is their body and their choice. Non-maleficence says you must prevent harm. Beneficence says you must act in their best interest. These principles pull in different directions — and resolving the tension requires judgement, not just a rule.

In this scenario: your duty of care and mandatory reporting obligations mean you cannot simply keep this confidential. You must report immediately to your supervisor and/or the appropriate external authority.

## Duty of Care — Detailed Explanation

Duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable steps to avoid causing foreseeable harm to people who could be affected by your actions (or inactions).

**In community services, this means:**
- Monitoring and reporting changes in a client's condition
- Following safe work practices to prevent injury
- Not leaving a client in an unsafe situation
- Reporting hazards, risks, and concerns
- Following prescribed support plans and medication protocols

**What duty of care does NOT mean:**
- Doing everything possible regardless of the person's wishes
- Preventing all risk at the cost of a person's autonomy
- Making decisions on behalf of a person who has capacity to decide

**The balance:** Duty of care operates alongside the right to self-determination. A person can make a choice that involves some risk. Your obligation is to ensure they have the information to make an informed choice, that the risk is reasonable, and that you have documented the situation appropriately.

## Mandatory Reporting

Most states require workers in health and community services to report certain concerns regardless of whether the person wants them reported. **The obligation to report overrides confidentiality in these situations.**

| Scenario | Likely Mandatory Reporting Obligation |
|---|---|
| Child at risk of harm | Yes — all Australian states (scope varies) |
| Older person experiencing abuse or neglect in aged care | Yes — Serious Incident Response Scheme (SIRS) |
| NDIS participant experiencing abuse, neglect, exploitation, or violence | Yes — NDIS Commission mandatory reporting |
| Worker witnessing another worker abusing a client | Yes — depending on sector and state |

**Key principle:** If you are unsure whether something must be reported, report it. The consequences of failing to report are more serious than the consequences of reporting something that turns out not to require it.

## Confidentiality and Privacy

Confidential information is information about a client that is obtained in the course of the professional relationship. It must be:
- Kept secure (locked, password-protected, not left in public areas)
- Shared only with those who need it for the care/support purpose
- Not disclosed to family members without the client's consent (unless required by law)
- Not discussed in public spaces (corridors, lifts, cafeterias)

**When you CAN share without consent:**
- With other members of the care team who need it to provide safe care
- When mandatory reporting obligations apply
- When there is serious and immediate risk to life (the person's or others')
- When required by law (court order, investigation)

## Ethical Dilemmas — A Framework for Working Through Them

When you face a situation where two or more ethical principles pull in different directions, use this approach:

1. **Identify the dilemma clearly.** What are the competing obligations or values?
2. **Gather information.** What do you actually know? What are you assuming?
3. **Identify whose interests are at stake.** Client, family, organisation, community?
4. **Identify the relevant principles, legislation, and policies.**
5. **Consider the options.** What could you do? What would each option lead to?
6. **Consult.** Talk to your supervisor. Seek guidance. Do not act alone on a significant ethical dilemma.
7. **Act, document, and reflect.** Make the decision, record your reasoning, and reflect on the outcome.

## Worked Example — Ethical Dilemma Response

**Prompt:**

*You are supporting Ahmed, a 67-year-old man living at home with his wife. During your visit, Ahmed tells you that his son has been taking money from his bank account without his knowledge. Ahmed says he doesn't want to report it to anyone because it will "destroy the family." He asks you to keep this a secret. What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This situation presents a conflict between respecting Ahmed's right to confidentiality and self-determination, and my obligations related to elder abuse and my organisation's mandatory reporting requirements.

The first thing I would do is respond to Ahmed with empathy and without judgment. I would thank him for trusting me with this information and acknowledge how difficult his position is. I would not promise confidentiality because I know I cannot keep that promise.

I would then be honest with Ahmed: I need to explain that what he has described — money being taken from his account without his knowledge or consent — is a form of financial abuse and that my organisation has obligations to respond to this under our duty of care and, depending on our policies and the relevant state legislation, potentially under mandatory reporting requirements. I would be clear that I am not able to keep this secret, but I want to work through the options with him.

I would explain his options: his organisation may have a financial counselling service or social worker who specialises in elder abuse situations; the police and/or elder abuse helpline can provide confidential advice without immediately escalating to formal action; and he may be able to explore options that address the financial situation without immediately involving formal reporting if he has capacity to make this choice.

I would tell him I need to report this to my supervisor immediately, which I would do — without delay. I would document the conversation factually and objectively: what Ahmed disclosed, what I said, and what actions I took. I would not record anything beyond the facts.

This situation may also involve my organisation's serious incident reporting obligations if it meets the threshold under the Serious Incident Response Scheme (SIRS) for aged care, which I would check with my supervisor.

**Examiner Annotation:** This response demonstrates empathy, honesty about limits of confidentiality, knowledge of financial abuse as a category of elder abuse, appropriate escalation, legislative knowledge (SIRS), documentation practice, and the balance between autonomy and duty of care. Strong Competent response.
