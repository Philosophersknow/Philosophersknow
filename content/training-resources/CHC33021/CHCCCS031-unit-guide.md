## What This Unit Is About

This is the central unit of the entire qualification. Everything else in CHC33021 connects back to it. CHCCCS031 is about understanding that support is not something you do *to* a person — it is something you do *with* them, in a way that reflects their individual preferences, goals, background, and rights.

**The key concept: individualised support**

Individualised support means that the way you support each person is tailored specifically to that person — their needs, their history, their cultural background, their goals, their preferences, and their choices. It is the opposite of a one-size-fits-all approach where every client gets the same routine regardless of who they are.

## Elements of Competency

This unit has five elements. Your assessment will check that you can demonstrate all five:

### Element 1: Prepare for individualised support activities

You need to:
- Review and understand the individual support plan (also called care plan, person-centred plan, or service plan)
- Identify any changes in the person's condition, preferences, or circumstances that might affect support
- Prepare any equipment, materials, or environment needed before starting support
- Know what you are authorised to do — and what requires referral or escalation

**What assessors look for:** Evidence that you read the support plan BEFORE you start — not during, not after. Preparation means the person is not kept waiting while you figure out what to do.

---

### Element 2: Provide support in accordance with the individual support plan

You need to:
- Deliver support exactly as specified in the plan
- Respect the person's right to make choices — including choices you personally disagree with
- Provide only the level of support required — not more (which creates dependency) and not less (which neglects needs)
- Adapt support activities to meet the person's changing needs *within your scope*
- Report anything outside your scope immediately

**What assessors look for:** The balance between following the plan and being responsive to the person in the moment. A support plan is a guide, not a script. If the person says "I don't want help with that today," you respect that — but you document it.

---

### Element 3: Support the person to use and maintain assistive technology and equipment

You need to:
- Understand what assistive technology or equipment the person uses (mobility aids, communication devices, continence products, hearing aids, etc.)
- Assist the person to use equipment safely and in the way they prefer
- Report any equipment that is damaged, missing, or not functioning correctly
- Never use equipment you are not trained or authorised to use

**Common mistake:** Students describe helping a person *with* their equipment but forget to address maintaining it — cleaning, checking for damage, reporting faults. Both are required.

---

### Element 4: Support the person in valued roles and relationships

You need to:
- Understand that people have social roles beyond "client" — they are parents, grandparents, community members, friends, hobbyists, workers
- Actively support the person to maintain and strengthen those roles and relationships
- Avoid practices that undermine social connection (e.g., talking to carers about the person as if they aren't there, treating them as passive recipients)

**What assessors look for:** Concrete examples of how you *actively* support social connection — not just "I encouraged her to participate" but *how* you created the opportunity, what you said, what happened.

---

### Element 5: Complete reporting and documentation

You need to:
- Accurately record all support activities, observations, and any changes in condition
- Complete documentation in the timeframe required by your organisation
- Maintain confidentiality — records contain sensitive personal information
- Report changes or concerns through the correct channels (not just to a colleague)

**What assessors look for:** Timeliness, accuracy, and professional language. Documentation written as if it might one day be used in a legal proceeding — because it might.

## Knowledge Evidence — What You Must Know

Your assessor will ask questions to check you understand the following. You cannot just describe what you do; you must demonstrate WHY.

### Person-centred practice
- What does "person-centred" actually mean in practice? (It means the person's goals, choices and preferences guide everything — not the organisation's preferences or the worker's convenience)
- How does a support plan reflect person-centred principles?
- What is the difference between needs and preferences?

### Rights and responsibilities
- The right to self-determination: people can make choices you disagree with, including ones that involve some risk, as long as they have capacity to make that choice
- Duty of care: your legal obligation to take reasonable steps to protect the person from harm
- Dignity of risk: the right of people with disability or in aged care to take reasonable risks — over-protection is a form of discrimination
- Mandatory reporting: what you are legally required to report (abuse, neglect, injury, significant changes in health)

### Legislation and policy
- Aged Care Quality and Safety Standards (for aged care settings)
- NDIS Practice Standards (for disability settings)
- Privacy Act 1988 and your organisation's confidentiality policy
- Work Health and Safety Act (your jurisdiction)
- Relevant state/territory legislation (varies by state)

### Documentation requirements
- What goes in a progress note vs an incident report vs a handover note
- How to write factual, objective, non-judgmental documentation
- What abbreviations are acceptable in your organisation's system

## Common Assessment Mistakes — What Gets Students NYC

**1. Describing tasks without connecting to person-centred principles**

*Weak:* "I helped Mrs Jones get dressed."

*Strong:* "I supported Mrs Jones to dress herself as much as possible, offering assistance only where she indicated she needed help, following her preferences for the order she puts clothes on, which she has told her care team she feels strongly about."

---

**2. Ignoring the support plan**

If your answer to a question doesn't mention consulting or following the support plan, it will likely be marked NYC. The plan is the authorising document. Everything you do should connect back to it.

---

**3. Making decisions outside scope**

Support workers do not make clinical decisions, change medication regimes, override care plans, or decide to restrict a person's choices "for their own good." If your answer describes doing any of these things without referral or escalation, it suggests you don't understand scope of practice.

---

**4. Vague documentation language**

"Client seemed sad." — NYC

"Client was tearful during morning care and stated she was missing her daughter. This is a change from her usual presentation. Reported to team leader [name] at [time]. Recorded in progress notes." — Competent

---

**5. Confusing confidentiality with secrecy**

Confidential information is shared on a need-to-know basis within the care team. You do not share it with the person's family without consent. You do not discuss it in public spaces. But you do share it with colleagues who need it to provide safe, quality care. Treating confidentiality as absolute secrecy is a misunderstanding that creates safety risks.

## Worked Example — Case Study Response

**Case Study Prompt:**

*You are a support worker at a residential aged care facility. You arrive for the morning shift and are assigned to support William, 84, who has moderate dementia and uses a walking frame. His support plan states he prefers to shower at 7:30am, likes to choose his own clothes, and takes his time getting dressed. The shift is busy and your team leader suggests you encourage William to shower quickly so the team can keep to schedule.*

*Question: How would you approach William's morning care, and how would you respond to your team leader's suggestion?*

---

**Exemplary Response (Competent — High Standard):**

Before approaching William, I would review his support plan to confirm his preferences and any specific notes from the previous shift. I would note that his plan specifies a 7:30am shower, that he likes to choose his own clothes, and that he takes his time — all of which are his stated preferences and part of his individualised support plan.

I would approach William at the agreed time, introduce myself (noting that people with dementia may not immediately recognise care staff), and follow his preferred morning routine. I would lay out a choice of clothes and allow him to indicate what he would like to wear — maintaining his dignity and sense of control, which is particularly important for people with dementia who may have limited areas of autonomy remaining.

Regarding my team leader's suggestion to rush William: I would respectfully explain that William's support plan documents his preferences, and that rushing his routine would not be consistent with our organisation's person-centred approach or with his rights under the Aged Care Quality and Safety Standards (specifically Standard 1, Consumer Dignity and Choice). I would suggest we look at how the team's workload is organised rather than compromising William's care. If this became a pattern, I would raise it with the care coordinator or use my organisation's feedback mechanism.

I would document William's morning care accurately, noting how long the shower took, any observations about his wellbeing or presentation, and the conversation with the team leader.

**Examiner Annotation:** This response demonstrates understanding of person-centred practice, the support plan as an authorising document, the specific context of dementia care, rights-based practice, appropriate escalation within scope, and documentation. The reference to the Aged Care Quality Standards demonstrates required legislative knowledge.

---

**Acceptable Response (Competent — Minimum Standard):**

I would follow William's support plan and give him his shower at 7:30 as planned. I would let him choose his clothes because the plan says he likes to do this and it's important for his dignity. Even though the shift is busy, I wouldn't rush him because the support plan is there for a reason and it's about what William wants, not what is convenient for the team.

I would tell my team leader politely that I need to follow William's care plan and that rushing him wouldn't be right. I'd document what happened in my progress notes.

**Examiner Annotation:** This response demonstrates the key principles — support plan compliance, person-centred practice, advocacy within scope. It is competent but thin on legislative reference and doesn't demonstrate knowledge of the specific context of dementia care. Passes, but the student would benefit from developing more specific knowledge.

---

**Insufficient Response (Not Yet Competent):**

I would try to be quick but still make sure William was clean and dressed. I understand the team leader is under pressure and we all need to work together. I'd do my best to keep to the schedule while still being nice to William.

**Examiner Annotation:** NYC. This response prioritises organisational convenience over the client's rights and the support plan. There is no reference to person-centred practice, the support plan, or William's right to have his preferences respected. The response suggests the student does not yet understand that a support plan creates an obligation, not just a guideline. Resubmission required with specific reference to the support plan, person-centred principles, and how the student would advocate for the client.

## Scaffolding Questions — Build Your Own Answer

If you are stuck on a case study or knowledge question, work through these prompts:

1. **Who is the person in this scenario?** What do we know about them — their history, preferences, goals, condition?
2. **What does the support plan say?** Does the scenario tell you? If not, what would a good support plan include for this person?
3. **What are this person's rights in this situation?** (Choice, dignity, safety, privacy, advocacy)
4. **What is within my scope?** What can I do, and what requires referral?
5. **What legislation or standards apply?** (Aged Care Quality Standards / NDIS Practice Standards / WHS)
6. **How would I document this?** What would I record, and when?
7. **What would a person-centred worker do differently from a task-focused worker in this situation?**
