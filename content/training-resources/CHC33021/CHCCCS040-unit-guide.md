## What This Unit Is About

Independence and wellbeing are two of the most important outcomes in community services work — and they are closely linked. People who maintain independence have greater control over their lives, which directly contributes to their wellbeing. People who experience genuine wellbeing are better equipped to maintain and extend their independence.

CHCCCS040 asks you to actively support both — not by doing things for people, but by creating the conditions in which people can do things for themselves, maintain their connections, and experience a good quality of life according to their own definition of what that means.

---

## The Dimensions of Wellbeing

Wellbeing is multidimensional. In community services, we understand wellbeing to include:

| Dimension | What It Includes |
|---|---|
| **Physical** | Health, mobility, nutrition, pain management, sleep, personal hygiene |
| **Emotional/psychological** | Mental health, mood, self-esteem, sense of purpose, emotional safety |
| **Social** | Relationships, community connection, family, friendship, belonging |
| **Cultural** | Cultural identity, connection to country (for First Nations people), religious and spiritual practice |
| **Cognitive** | Mental stimulation, memory, decision-making, learning |
| **Financial** | Economic security, access to resources, financial autonomy |
| **Environmental** | Safe, accessible, and comfortable physical environment |

A support worker who focuses only on physical wellbeing — keeping someone clean, fed, and medicated — while ignoring social isolation or loss of purpose is providing incomplete support. Comprehensive wellbeing support attends to all dimensions.

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Provide individualised support for independence

You need to:
- Identify the person's current capabilities and support what they can do, rather than replacing it
- Provide the *minimum* level of support required — not more
- Use a strengths-based approach: build on what the person can do
- Support the person to use mobility aids, assistive technology, and other tools that extend their independence
- Gradually withdraw support as the person's capacity increases (where applicable)

**The key mistake to avoid:** Over-servicing. Doing everything for someone because it is faster, because they seem to want you to, or because it makes you feel helpful is not good support — it creates dependency and undermines the person's confidence and capability.

---

### Element 2: Support emotional and psychological wellbeing

You need to:
- Recognise signs of emotional distress, anxiety, depression, or deteriorating mental health
- Respond with empathy and without judgment
- Know when and how to refer or escalate mental health concerns
- Support the person to maintain or develop coping strategies and social connections
- Recognise the role of purpose, meaning, and identity in psychological wellbeing

**Signs of deteriorating psychological wellbeing to recognise and report:**
- Withdrawal from previously enjoyed activities
- Changes in appetite or sleep
- Increased confusion or agitation (especially in dementia)
- Expressions of hopelessness or worthlessness
- Increased irritability or tearfulness
- Neglect of personal hygiene (when this is a change from baseline)

---

### Element 3: Support social wellbeing

You need to:
- Identify the person's social network and connection needs
- Actively support the person to maintain existing relationships
- Support access to community participation — groups, events, activities, services
- Recognise social isolation as a significant health risk (equivalent to smoking 15 cigarettes a day in terms of mortality risk)
- Connect people to appropriate community resources, groups, and services

---

### Element 4: Recognise and report changes in wellbeing

You need to:
- Observe the person's wellbeing across all dimensions as part of every interaction
- Compare current presentation to baseline (what is normal for this person)
- Report any significant changes through the correct channels promptly
- Document observations accurately, factually, and objectively
- Understand that changes in one dimension of wellbeing often signal changes in others

## Independence — What It Really Means

Independence does not mean doing everything alone. It means having control over decisions about your own life — including decisions about when to accept help and when not to.

A person who cannot walk unaided but chooses their own clothes, manages their own finances, maintains their friendships, and makes decisions about their care is living independently. A person who can walk but has every aspect of their daily routine scheduled and managed by others, without their input, is not.

**Support for independence includes:**
- Offering choices rather than making choices for people
- Giving people enough time to complete tasks themselves, even when it takes longer
- Teaching skills rather than just completing tasks
- Supporting access to technology that extends independence (voice control, mobility aids, communication devices)
- Respecting a person's right to make choices that others might consider unwise

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You support Frank, 82, who lives alone at home with home care services. Frank was a keen gardener for 50 years and still has a large backyard. Since a hip replacement 6 months ago he has not gone into the garden and his daughter has arranged for a gardening service to maintain it. On your visits, Frank rarely leaves his recliner, watches television most of the day, and has told you "there's no point in anything anymore." His meals are being delivered and he eats them in front of the TV. His personal hygiene is adequate. His daughter is satisfied with the current arrangements.*

*Question: Using a wellbeing framework, identify what concerns you about Frank's current situation and describe how you would respond.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

Frank's physical health appears stable — his hygiene is maintained and he is eating. However, across almost every other dimension of wellbeing, there are significant concerns.

**Social isolation:** Frank appears to have minimal social contact. He is alone most of the day, eating alone in front of the television. Social isolation at this level is a serious health risk — research consistently links it to accelerated cognitive decline, depression, and increased mortality.

**Psychological/emotional wellbeing:** Frank's statement "there's no point in anything anymore" is a significant concern. While it could reflect transient low mood, it could also indicate clinical depression, which is common but frequently undiagnosed and untreated in older people. This requires immediate reporting to my supervisor and the care coordinator, and potentially a referral to a GP or mental health service. I would not dismiss it as "just his age" or "understandable given his mobility."

**Loss of purpose and identity:** Frank was a gardener for 50 years. The removal of his connection to his garden — while practically well-intentioned — has also removed a core part of his identity and daily purpose. The fact that a gardening service now does what he spent his life doing is likely contributing to his sense of uselessness.

**Physical independence:** While his mobility is limited, it is not clear that Frank has been actively supported to rehabilitate or adapt his gardening practice. Could he garden from a raised bed or a seated position? Has occupational therapy been involved to assess adaptive equipment?

My response would be:

1. Document my observations, including Frank's exact words ("there's no point in anything anymore"), and report them to my supervisor and care coordinator immediately. This statement requires follow-up — it may warrant a GP referral or a mental health assessment.

2. In my interaction with Frank, explore his connection to the garden with genuine curiosity. Has anyone asked him whether he would like to be involved in a modified way? Does he watch the gardening service through the window? Does he have opinions about how the garden is being managed?

3. Discuss with the care coordinator whether a referral to an occupational therapist is appropriate to assess whether Frank could safely participate in modified gardening activities.

4. With Frank's consent, explore community options: men's sheds, community gardening groups, or programs specifically designed for older people who are losing engagement.

5. Gently explore the family arrangement — the daughter is satisfied, but satisfaction with hygiene and meals does not equal satisfaction with overall wellbeing. With appropriate privacy considerations, I would raise my concerns with the care coordinator, who may wish to involve the family in a conversation about Frank's broader wellbeing.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. This response demonstrates multidimensional wellbeing assessment, identifies depression risk correctly and with appropriate urgency, connects loss of purpose to the specific situation, proposes concrete and practical responses including referral pathways, raises the family dynamic without overstepping, and prioritises documentation and escalation. This is what a competent, caring support worker looks like.
