## What This Unit Is About

CUAIND312 covers how to *work* in the industry — contracts, rates, etiquette, rights. CUAIND311 covers how to *research and stay current in* it: knowing where the industry is going, who is making what, which platforms and formats are growing, and how to find that information reliably rather than by assumption.

The screen and media industry changes faster than almost any other creative sector. A practitioner whose understanding of the market is three years old is making career decisions on stale information.

## The Australian Screen Sector — Current Shape

### Content Commissioning Landscape

| Commissioner | What They Fund | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **ABC / SBS** | Drama, documentary, factual, children's, digital-first | Public broadcasters; distinct charter obligations |
| **Commercial FTA (Seven, Nine, Ten)** | Reality, light entertainment, news, some drama | Advertising-funded; ratings-driven |
| **Streaming platforms (Netflix, Stan, Prime Video, Binge, Disney+, Paramount+)** | Drama, documentary, unscripted | Now a major share of Australian commissioning |
| **Advertising agencies / brands** | TVCs, branded content, social campaigns | Shorter-form, faster turnaround, often the largest volume of paid work |
| **Corporate / government** | Training, internal communications, public information | Steady, less glamorous, reliable income |
| **Independent / self-funded** | Short films, web series, documentary | Portfolio-building; funding-dependent |

**[VERIFY: Streaming platform presence in the Australian market changes with corporate consolidation and market entry/exit. Confirm current commissioners before relying on this in a career or funding decision.]**

### Australian Content Obligations

Australian content quotas and investment obligations have historically applied to free-to-air and subscription broadcasters, requiring minimum levels of Australian-produced content. Extending obligations to streaming platforms has been a sustained policy debate.

**Why this matters to you:** Content obligations directly determine how much Australian production work exists. Changes to these rules move the volume of available work in the sector.

**[VERIFY: Australian content obligations for streaming services have been subject to ongoing government review and legislative change. Confirm the current position with Screen Australia and the Department of Infrastructure, Transport, Regional Development, Communications and the Arts.]**

### Production Volume Drivers

Australian production volume is driven by:
- **Domestic commissioning** — broadcasters and streamers funding local content
- **Offshore production** — international productions shooting in Australia, attracted by the Location Offset, crew depth, locations, and exchange rates
- **Post, Digital and Visual Effects (PDV)** — international work sent to Australian post houses
- **Advertising spend** — tracks the broader economy
- **Government incentives** — federal offsets and state attraction funds

**Offshore production is volatile.** It responds to exchange rates, competing incentives in other jurisdictions, and international studio production cycles. A boom year for offshore work does not guarantee the next one.

## Researching the Industry

A core requirement of this unit is being able to **find** industry information, not just recall it.

### Primary Sources

| Source | What It Provides |
|---|---|
| **Screen Australia** | Drama Report, production statistics, funding data, audience research — the authoritative source for Australian production data |
| **State screen agencies** | Local production activity, funding rounds, local incentive programs |
| **ACMA** | Broadcasting regulation, content quota compliance, licensing |
| **Screen Producers Australia (SPA)** | Industry advocacy, production business issues |
| **MEAA** | Rates, agreements, industrial conditions, member campaigns |
| **IF Magazine / Mediaweek / TV Tonight** | Trade press — commissions, appointments, ratings, industry news |
| **Variety / Deadline / Screen Daily** | International trade press |
| **Company websites and credits** | Who makes what; who to approach |

### Reading an Industry Report

When you read production statistics, distinguish:
- **Number of titles** vs **total expenditure** — a year with fewer, larger productions can have higher spend and more crew days than a year with many small ones
- **Domestic vs foreign** production — very different implications for who is employed and in what roles
- **Genre breakdown** — drama, documentary, children's, and unscripted employ different crew mixes
- **Where production occurred** — state distribution matters if you are deciding where to base yourself

### Evaluating Source Reliability

Not all industry information is equal:

| More reliable | Less reliable |
|---|---|
| Screen Australia statistics | A production company's own promotional claims |
| MEAA published rates | "What someone told me on set they got paid" |
| Trade press with named sources | Anonymous forum posts and social media speculation |
| Government policy documents | Commentary about what policy "will probably" become |

**Apply the same scepticism to industry claims you would apply to any other information.** People in this industry talk confidently about rates, opportunities, and who is hiring on the basis of very small samples.

## Formats, Platforms, and Where Content Goes

### Format Characteristics

| Format | Length | Production Scale | Entry Accessibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feature film** | 80–120+ min | Large crew, long schedule | Low — competitive, credit-dependent |
| **TV drama series** | 6–13 × 45–60 min | Large crew, block shooting | Low–moderate |
| **Documentary** | 50–90 min or series | Small crew | Moderate — small crews take on juniors |
| **Reality / factual entertainment** | Series | Large crew, fast turnaround | **High — a major entry point** |
| **TVC / advertising** | 15–60 sec | Compressed, high spend per second | Moderate |
| **Branded / corporate content** | 1–5 min | Small crew | **High** |
| **Social / short-form vertical** | 15–90 sec | Very small crew, often solo | **Very high** |
| **Podcast / audio** | Variable | Minimal crew | **Very high** |
| **Live streaming / events** | Live | Technical crew | Moderate |

**Practical point for a Certificate III graduate:** The realistic entry points are unscripted television, corporate and branded content, social/short-form, and events — not feature drama. This is not a lesser path. Crew who work consistently in unscripted and corporate build credits, networks, and technical range that later open other doors.

### Aspect Ratio and Delivery Considerations

Content is now made for multiple aspect ratios simultaneously:
- **16:9** — standard horizontal broadcast and streaming
- **9:16** — vertical for TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts
- **1:1** — square for social feeds
- **2.39:1** — cinematic widescreen

**Shooting for multi-format delivery** means framing with awareness that the image will be cropped — protecting the centre of frame, avoiding critical action at the edges, and sometimes shooting the same setup twice. This is now a standard requirement, not a specialist skill.

### Delivery Specifications

Every broadcaster and platform publishes **delivery specifications** — technical requirements a finished production must meet:
- Codec, container, and bitrate
- Resolution and frame rate
- **Audio loudness** — Australian broadcast typically requires compliance with a loudness standard measured in LUFS
- Captioning and audio description requirements
- Textless elements and masters
- Metadata

**A production that does not meet delivery spec is rejected.** Delivery is not an afterthought; it is a hard requirement built into the schedule.

**[VERIFY: Specific loudness standards, caption requirements, and delivery specifications vary by broadcaster and platform and are periodically updated. Always obtain the current spec document from the commissioner.]**

## Accessibility and Inclusion

### Accessibility in Content

| Feature | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Closed captions** | Access for Deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences; also used widely by hearing audiences in sound-off environments |
| **Audio description** | Narration of visual information for blind and low-vision audiences |
| **Auslan interpretation** | Sign language access |
| **Plain language** | Cognitive accessibility |
| **Colour contrast and text legibility** | Low-vision access; also improves legibility on small screens |

Captioning obligations apply to Australian broadcast content under broadcasting regulation, and platform requirements increasingly match this.

**Captions are also a commercial consideration.** A very large proportion of social video is watched with sound off. Content without captions or on-screen text simply fails to communicate to that audience.

### Inclusive Representation

The Australian screen sector has documented significant under-representation, both on screen and in crews — of First Nations people, culturally and linguistically diverse people, people with disability, and women in certain technical roles.

**Practically, for someone entering the industry:**
- Screen agencies operate funding and development programs specifically supporting under-represented practitioners
- Many productions have diversity and inclusion commitments attached to funding
- Consultation protocols apply to some content — see below

### First Nations Protocols

Screen Australia publishes protocols for working with First Nations content, communities, and stories. Core principles include:

- **Free, prior and informed consent** from communities and individuals whose stories are involved
- **Cultural consultation** with appropriate community representatives, not simply any available person
- **Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP)** — recognising community ownership of cultural knowledge, stories, designs, and practices, which is not adequately protected by ordinary copyright
- **Appropriate employment and capacity building** — involvement rather than extraction
- **Restrictions on images of deceased persons** — a warning is conventionally provided, and in some communities images and names of deceased people are not shown

**[VERIFY: Refer to the current Screen Australia First Nations protocols document. Protocols are revised and should be consulted directly rather than summarised.]**

**This is not optional courtesy.** Failing to follow protocols can end a production, forfeit funding, and cause genuine harm.

## Industry Networks and Development

### Where the Industry Gathers

- **Film festivals** — Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide, Brisbane, Perth; short film festivals such as Flickerfest and Tropfest; documentary festivals
- **Industry markets and conferences** — SPA Screen Forever, AIDC (Australian International Documentary Conference)
- **Awards** — AACTA Awards; guild awards (ASE, ACS, AWG, ADG)
- **Screen agency programs** — talent development, attachment schemes, mentorships
- **Local meetups and screenings** — often the most accessible entry point

### Attachment and Placement Schemes

Screen agencies and some productions run **attachment** schemes — paid placements on a production for emerging practitioners, shadowing a head of department. These are among the most effective entry routes because they provide a real credit and direct network access.

Check Screen Australia and your state agency for current attachment and placement opportunities.

### Building Industry Knowledge Over Time

- Watch Australian content deliberately and analytically — note the production company, the DOP, the editor, the broadcaster
- **Read the credits.** Knowing who does what, at which companies, is directly employable knowledge
- Follow production companies and key practitioners
- Subscribe to trade press
- Keep a record of productions shooting in your state and who is crewing them

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are about to finish CUA31120 and you live in a regional centre, not a capital city. A friend tells you "there's no screen work outside Sydney and Melbourne — you'll have to move or give up." You have limited savings and cannot relocate immediately.*

*Research and construct an evidence-based response. What work realistically exists, how would you find it, and what would you do in the next twelve months?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

My friend's claim is partly true and mostly outdated. Scripted drama production does concentrate heavily in Sydney and Melbourne, but that is a small fraction of the paid screen work in Australia — and it is not the fraction a Certificate III graduate enters through anyway.

Rather than accept or reject the claim, I would establish what is actually true for my region.

---

**Step 1 — Find out what is actually here.**

**Sources I would use:**

- **My state screen agency.** Every state and territory has one, and most have a regional production focus, a location register, and a crew register. Several run specific regional funding or attraction programs. This is the single most useful starting point.
- **Screen Australia production statistics** — for the state-level breakdown of production activity, which tells me the actual scale rather than the anecdote.
- **Local council and regional development bodies** — many actively attract production and maintain film-friendly location and permit information.
- **The local crew register or Facebook crew group** — nearly every region has one, and they are where short-notice work is posted.

**What I am looking for:** which productions have shot in the region in the last two years, which local businesses produce content, and who the existing local crew are.

---

**Step 2 — Identify the work that actually exists regionally.**

Based on how the industry is structured, regional paid screen work reliably includes:

| Source of work | Why it exists regionally |
|---|---|
| **Corporate and business video** | Every region has businesses, and they all need content. Consistently the largest volume of regional paid work |
| **Local government and health** | Councils, health services, and state agencies produce public information, training, and campaign content, often through procurement panels |
| **Agriculture, mining, tourism** | Regional industries with real budgets and a genuine need for content — and often no local supplier |
| **Events and live streaming** | Conferences, sport, council meetings, weddings, festivals. Steady and skill-building |
| **Real estate** | High volume, quick turnaround, reliable |
| **Education** | Universities, TAFEs, and schools produce substantial content |
| **Regional news and community broadcasting** | Local news bureaus, community TV and radio |
| **Offshore and domestic productions on location** | Productions shoot regionally for locations. When they do, they hire local crew for base-level roles because it is cheaper than accommodating a full unit |
| **Documentary** | Small crews, story-driven, frequently regional subject matter |

**The critical insight:** In a capital city I would be one of hundreds of Certificate III graduates competing for entry roles. In a regional centre I may be one of very few people who can competently shoot, record clean audio, and edit. **Scarcity is an advantage, and it is one I lose the moment I relocate.**

---

**Step 3 — Position for the productions that do come through.**

When a production shoots in a region, they hire locally for roles where local knowledge or simple availability is valuable — additional crew, runners, production assistants, locations assistants, extras casting, catering support, local fixers.

To be hired, I need to be **findable and already registered** before the production arrives, not scrambling when I hear about it.

Actions:
- Register on my state agency's crew register
- Join the regional crew group and be visibly active
- Introduce myself to any local production business
- Make sure my details are with the council's film liaison contact if one exists

---

**Step 4 — My twelve-month plan.**

**Months 1–3: Establish and evidence.**
- Complete the qualification
- Build a showreel from my best coursework, confirming I have the rights to include it
- Build a simple portfolio site with contact details, showreel, and what I offer
- Register on the state agency crew register and any local registers
- Identify and list every business, agency, and organisation within 100km that produces or needs content

**Months 4–6: Get real credits, paid where possible.**
- Approach local businesses directly with a specific offer, not a general availability email — "I noticed you have no video on your site; here is what I would make and what it would cost"
- Take event and live stream work; it pays, builds technical range, and teaches working under pressure
- Do a small number of carefully chosen unpaid or low-paid projects **only where they produce genuinely strong portfolio material** — for a community organisation with a real story, not for a business that can afford to pay
- Document everything for the portfolio

**Months 7–9: Specialise and deepen.**
- Identify which discipline I am strongest in and want to pursue — camera, sound, editing
- Build depth there rather than remaining a generalist forever
- Target productions shooting regionally; make contact early
- Consider a short trip to a capital city for a festival or industry event to build network without relocating

**Months 10–12: Review and decide.**
- Assess: is regional work sustaining me, growing, and building credits I want?
- If yes, continue and deepen — a regional practitioner with a reputation and no local competition is a good position
- If no, I will by then have a showreel, real credits, a network, and savings from twelve months of paid work — which is a far stronger position from which to relocate than moving now with none of those things

---

**Step 5 — What I would say to my friend.**

The claim conflates "screen industry" with "scripted drama in a capital city." That is one part of the industry and the hardest to enter. The larger volume of paid screen work in Australia is corporate, branded, event, and unscripted content, and a meaningful proportion of it is regional and under-served.

Relocating with no credits, no reel, no network, and no savings into the most competitive market in the country is not obviously the stronger move. Building all four where I already live, and then choosing, is.

**Where my friend has a point:** if my specific ambition is scripted drama camera department, I will eventually need to be where that work is. But "eventually, from a position of strength" is a different proposition from "immediately, with nothing."

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student refuses to accept or reject the claim on assertion and instead constructs a research method with named, authoritative sources — state screen agency, Screen Australia statistics, local government — which is precisely what this unit assesses. The identification of regional work categories is accurate, specific, and demonstrates genuine understanding of where the volume of paid screen work actually sits, as opposed to where the prestige sits. The observation that scarcity of skilled operators is a competitive advantage in a regional market, and one forfeited by relocating, is a sophisticated piece of career reasoning. The twelve-month plan is concrete, sequenced, and includes a decision point rather than an open-ended commitment. The stipulation that unpaid work is acceptable only where it produces strong portfolio material and only for organisations that genuinely cannot pay shows appropriate professional self-respect. The closing concession — acknowledging the friend's legitimate point about scripted drama specifically — demonstrates balanced reasoning rather than defensiveness. Strong Competent.
