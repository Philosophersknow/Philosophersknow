## What This Unit Is About

An idea that exists only in your head cannot be funded, crewed, scheduled, or built. Screen production is collaborative — a director's intention has to reach a DOP, a production designer, an editor, and a client, and it has to survive the journey intact.

CUAPPR311 covers the documents and techniques that carry an idea from concept to something a team can execute and a client can approve.

## Developing an Idea

### Generating

| Technique | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | Rapid generation without evaluation. The rule is to separate generating from judging — evaluation during generation kills the weaker-sounding ideas that lead somewhere |
| **Mind mapping** | Central concept with radiating branches; surfaces connections between ideas |
| **Mood boarding** | Collecting visual references — images, frames, colour, typography — to define a feel before it can be articulated in words |
| **"What if" reframing** | Systematically inverting assumptions: what if this were funny, silent, one shot, from the other character's view? |
| **Reference analysis** | Studying how comparable work solved the same problem, then deliberately departing from it |
| **Constraint setting** | Deliberately limiting resources — one location, no dialogue, 60 seconds. Constraint reliably produces more inventive work than open briefs |

### Interrogating an Idea Before Committing

Before developing a concept, test it against:

- **Who is the audience?** Not "everyone". A concept aimed at everyone reaches nobody.
- **What is the single thing it must communicate?** If you cannot state it in one sentence, it is not resolved.
- **What is the platform?** A 15-second vertical social ad and a 5-minute website film are different concepts, not the same concept at different lengths.
- **Is it achievable?** With this budget, crew, schedule, and equipment.
- **What is the strongest objection to it?** Find it yourself before the client does.

## The Core Documents

### The Brief

The brief is the client's statement of what they want. A complete brief covers:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| **Objective** | What is this meant to achieve? |
| **Audience** | Who is it for? |
| **Key message** | What must the viewer take away? |
| **Deliverables** | How many pieces, what lengths, what formats and aspect ratios? |
| **Platform** | Where will it be seen? |
| **Tone** | Serious, warm, funny, authoritative? |
| **Mandatories** | Logo, tagline, legal disclaimers, brand guidelines |
| **Budget** | What is available |
| **Timeline** | Delivery date and key milestones |
| **Approvals** | **Who signs off** — see below |

**Most briefs arrive incomplete.** Part of the job is filling the gaps by asking, and then **writing the brief back** to the client for confirmation. A brief you have restated and had confirmed in writing is your protection when the project drifts.

**The approvals question is the one most often skipped and most often regretted.** "Who has final sign-off?" prevents the situation where a project is approved by a marketing manager and then reopened by a director who was never consulted.

### The Treatment

A **treatment** is a prose document describing what the finished piece will look and feel like. It is written for a client or commissioner — persuasive as well as descriptive.

A treatment typically covers:
- **The concept** in one or two sentences
- **The approach** — how it will be executed
- **Visual style** — look, colour, camera treatment, pace
- **Structure** — how it unfolds from open to close
- **Sound and music** — tone, whether voiceover or interview-led
- **Talent** — who appears
- **Locations**
- **Why this works** — connecting the approach back to the brief's objective

**Written in present tense, second or third person, describing the experience of watching it:**

> *We open on an empty workshop at dawn. Light comes through high windows and catches dust in the air. A single figure enters frame, unhurried. We hear only footsteps and the building settling — no music yet.*

**This is far more effective than:** "The video will start with a wide shot of a workshop. Then a person will walk in."

The first version makes the client *see* it. That is the treatment's job.

### Mood Boards and Visual References

A mood board communicates tone faster than any amount of writing. Include:
- Reference frames from existing work
- Colour palette
- Typography if graphics are involved
- Lighting references
- Location and wardrobe references

**Always caption references with what you are referencing.** A frame from a film without explanation invites the client to think you are copying it. "Reference: the quality of window light and the muted palette — not the camera movement" is precise and prevents misunderstanding.

**Reference is not a plan.** Showing a reference shot from a production with a seven-figure budget and a crew of eighty, without qualifying it, sets an expectation you cannot meet.

### The Script

| Type | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Drama / narrative** | Scene headings (INT./EXT. LOCATION – TIME), action in present tense, character names, dialogue. Industry-standard formatting matters — it makes timing and breakdown possible |
| **Two-column (AV script)** | **The standard for corporate, documentary, and commercial work.** Video described in the left column, audio in the right, aligned so you can see what is heard over what is seen |
| **Interview-led** | Question list rather than scripted dialogue; structure emerges in the edit |

**Two-column example:**

| VIDEO | AUDIO |
|---|---|
| Wide: workshop at dawn, empty. Dust in light. | Ambient: building tone, distant traffic. |
| Maria enters, crosses to bench. | MARIA (V/O): I started here with two hundred dollars and a borrowed ute. |
| CU: hands selecting a tool. | Music in — sparse piano, low. |

**Timing rule of thumb:** natural speech runs at roughly **140–160 words per minute** for voiceover. A 60-second script is approximately 150 words. Scripts that are 250 words for a 60-second slot will be rushed and unlistenable — check word count against duration before the client approves it.

### Storyboards

A storyboard is a shot-by-shot visual plan. Each frame shows:
- The framing (shot size and composition)
- Camera movement, indicated with arrows
- Shot number
- Brief action or dialogue note
- Duration if known

**Drawing ability is not the point.** Stick figures with clear framing communicate perfectly. What matters is that the shot size, angle, and movement are unambiguous.

**When storyboards are essential:** anything with visual effects, animation, complex action, precise timing, or client approval of specific shots before the shoot. **When they are less necessary:** documentary and interview-led work, where the material is discovered rather than planned.

**Shot list** — a simpler alternative or companion: a numbered list of every shot required, with size, subject, location, and any notes. Used on the day to track coverage and confirm nothing is missed.

### The Shot Language You Must Know

| Shot | Abbreviation | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme wide / establishing | EWS / ELS | Subject small in a large environment; establishes place |
| Wide / long shot | WS / LS | Full body, environment visible |
| Mid shot | MS | Roughly waist up |
| Medium close-up | MCU | Chest up — the standard interview framing |
| Close-up | CU | Head and shoulders / face |
| Extreme close-up | ECU | Detail — eyes, hands, an object |
| Over the shoulder | OTS | From behind one subject onto another |
| Point of view | POV | What the character sees |
| Two-shot | — | Two subjects in frame |

| Movement | Description |
|---|---|
| **Pan** | Horizontal pivot on a fixed point |
| **Tilt** | Vertical pivot on a fixed point |
| **Track / dolly** | Camera physically moves through space |
| **Crane / jib** | Camera moves vertically through space |
| **Zoom** | Focal length change — *not* a move; the perspective does not change |
| **Handheld** | Operator-carried; energy and immediacy |
| **Steadicam / gimbal** | Stabilised movement |

**Zoom versus track is a distinction that matters and is commonly confused.** A zoom magnifies; a track changes viewpoint and therefore perspective. They look completely different and are not interchangeable.

## Production Planning Documents

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Shot list** | Every shot required, tracked on the day |
| **Schedule** | What is shot when, in what order, with what and whom |
| **Call sheet** | Issued before each shoot day: call times, location and address, contacts, schedule, weather, parking, nearest hospital, WHS notes |
| **Risk assessment** | Hazards identified and controls stated. Required, not optional — see CUAIND312 |
| **Location recce notes** | Power availability, acoustics, light direction and timing, access, parking, permissions, hazards |
| **Equipment list** | Everything required, checked out and checked back in |
| **Release forms** | Talent and location releases, signed before shooting |
| **Budget** | Costs against the available figure |

**The call sheet is the single most useful document on a shoot.** If everyone knows where to be, when, and what is happening, most production problems disappear.

## Presenting to a Client

### Structuring a Pitch

1. **Restate the brief** — demonstrates you listened, and confirms alignment before you present anything
2. **State the concept in one sentence** — if it needs a paragraph, it is not resolved
3. **Show, do not only tell** — mood board, references, key frames
4. **Walk through the treatment** — take them through the experience in order
5. **Address the practical** — timeline, deliverables, what you need from them
6. **Invite response** — and then be quiet and listen

### Presenting Well

- **Know your material** so you are not reading from the slide
- **Lead with the idea, not the process.** Clients care what it will be, not how many concepts you discarded
- **Anticipate the obvious objection** and address it before it is raised
- **Do not oversell.** Overclaiming creates expectations that the delivery will fail
- **Be honest about constraints.** "That is achievable within the budget; the version with the crane shot is not" is respected

### Receiving Feedback

- **Listen fully before responding.** Defending an idea before understanding the objection wastes everyone's time
- **Distinguish the symptom from the cause.** "I don't like the music" often means "the pace feels wrong" or "the tone is off". Ask what the music is making them feel
- **Ask clarifying questions** rather than guessing at vague notes. "Make it pop" means nothing until you ask what they want the viewer to feel
- **Separate subjective preference from brief failure.** A client entitled to their preference is different from an execution that misses the objective — and both are worth identifying
- **Push back where it matters, with reasons.** "We can do that, but it will cost the emotional beat we built at the end — here is an alternative that keeps both" is professional. Silent compliance with a bad note is not doing your job
- **Document what was agreed.** Verbal feedback in a meeting becomes a dispute later. Confirm in writing

### Version Control and Approvals

- **Number versions clearly** — `ProjectName_v01`, `v02`, never `final`, `final2`, `FINAL_actual_use_this`
- **Record what changed** between versions
- **Get sign-off in writing** at defined stages — concept, script, rough cut, fine cut
- **Define how many revision rounds are included** in the quote. Unlimited revisions are a business risk and a common cause of unpaid work

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*A local community health service emails you: "We need a video about our new youth mental health drop-in service. Something engaging that will really speak to young people. We'd like it to go on our website and social media. Budget is $4,000. We need it by the end of next month. Can you send us a proposal?"*

*Identify what is missing from this brief, write the clarifying questions you would ask, and outline how you would approach the treatment.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This is a typical real-world brief: a genuine need, a real budget, and about a third of the information required to actually make anything. I would not write a proposal from this — I would go back with questions first, because a proposal built on assumptions will be wrong and rewriting it is unpaid work.

---

**What is missing:**

| Missing | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Specific audience** | "Young people" spans 12 to 25 — a 13-year-old and a 24-year-old have almost nothing in common in tone, platform, or reference. This single answer changes everything else |
| **The actual objective** | Is it awareness that the service exists, reducing the fear of walking in, or driving a specific action like booking? These produce different films |
| **Deliverables and formats** | "Website and social media" is not a deliverable list. How many pieces? What lengths? Which platforms? Aspect ratios? |
| **Who appears** | Real service users? Staff? Actors? This is the single biggest determinant of both approach and ethical complexity |
| **Tone** | Youth mental health content can be warm, frank, funny, or serious. Getting this wrong is worse than getting it bland |
| **Mandatories** | Crisis line numbers, service details, brand guidelines, funding body acknowledgements |
| **Approvals** | Who signs off? Health services often have communications, clinical governance, *and* an executive — all with views |
| **Clinical and ethical review** | Mental health content has safe messaging obligations. This is not optional |
| **What "$4,000" covers** | Inclusive of GST? Does it include talent, music licensing, captioning, travel? |
| **The real deadline** | "End of next month" — is that final delivery, or first cut for approval? Approval rounds in a health service take time |

---

**My clarifying questions — as I would actually send them:**

> Thanks for getting in touch — this sounds like a worthwhile project and I'd like to help. Before I put a proposal together I want to make sure I'm proposing the right thing, so a few questions:
>
> **Audience and purpose**
> 1. What age range specifically? A film for 13–15s looks quite different from one for 18–25s.
> 2. What do you most want a young person to do or feel after watching? Know the service exists, feel less nervous about coming in, or something else?
> 3. Are you also speaking to parents, carers, or referrers — teachers, GPs? That may need a separate piece rather than one film trying to do both.
>
> **Content and people**
> 4. Would young people who use the service appear on camera, or would you prefer staff, actors, or a voice-only approach? There are good options either way and it significantly affects the approach.
> 5. Can we film in the space itself?
> 6. Is there a real story you'd want at the centre, or should it be more general?
>
> **Deliverables**
> 7. How many pieces, and what lengths? My suggestion would be one main film for the website plus two or three short vertical cuts for social, since a single horizontal video performs poorly on social feeds.
> 8. Which platforms specifically — Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook?
>
> **Governance**
> 9. Who needs to approve, and at what stages? I'd suggest sign-off at concept, script, and rough cut.
> 10. Does this need clinical or communications review against safe messaging guidelines for mental health content? I'd want to build that into the timeline rather than discover it at the end.
> 11. Any mandatory inclusions — crisis line numbers, funder acknowledgements, brand guidelines?
>
> **Practical**
> 12. Is $4,000 inclusive of GST, and does it need to cover music licensing and captioning? I'd include both.
> 13. Is end of next month your final delivery date, or the date you need a first cut for approval? Approval rounds in health services often take longer than expected.

---

**The issue I would raise unprompted — safe messaging.**

Mental health content carries genuine risk of harm if handled badly. Australia has established **safe messaging guidelines** for communicating about mental health and suicide, developed by organisations including Mindframe and Everymind. These cover things like avoiding depiction of method, avoiding presenting distress as inevitable or hopeless, always including help-seeking information, and being careful with imagery.

**[VERIFY: Refer to current Mindframe and Everymind safe messaging guidelines directly rather than relying on a summary. Guidelines are periodically updated.]**

I would flag this in my first reply, because:
- It is a real duty of care to the audience
- It will affect the creative approach, so it needs to be known before the treatment is written
- It will require review time in the schedule
- Raising it demonstrates that I understand the subject matter, which builds client confidence

If young people with lived experience appear on camera, there are further considerations: informed consent including the right to withdraw, whether they fully understand that this will be public and permanent, consent from parents or guardians for minors, and appropriate support available during and after filming. **A young person's consent to appear when they are well does not straightforwardly cover how they may feel about it in two years.** I would want the service's clinical view on who is appropriate to approach.

---

**How I would approach the treatment — subject to the answers:**

**The core creative problem:** young people have a highly developed detector for institutional content pretending to be youth culture. Anything that feels like adults performing relatability will be dismissed within seconds — and the dismissal transfers to the service itself.

So the approach I would lean toward is **honest and understated rather than energetic and produced.**

**Likely direction:**
- **Real voices over polish.** Actual young people and actual staff speaking plainly, not to camera as a pitch but as themselves. If lived-experience participation is not appropriate, then staff speaking honestly about what the room is actually like
- **Answer the real question.** The thing a nervous 17-year-old wants to know is not the service model — it is *what happens when I walk in, will I have to explain myself, and can I leave.* A film that answers that concretely is more useful than one that says the service is welcoming
- **Show the actual space.** Fear of the unknown is the barrier. Seeing the door, the waiting area, and the room removes it
- **Restraint in tone.** No stock-footage sunsets, no hopeful piano over slow motion. That visual language reads as inauthentic to this audience precisely because it is so familiar from campaign material
- **Vertical-first for the social cuts.** Not horizontal cropped down. Framed for vertical from the shoot

**A one-sentence concept, to be tested with them:**
*A short, plainly-shot film that walks a young person through exactly what happens when they come in — so the unknown stops being the barrier.*

**Practically, within $4,000:** that is a modest budget, which suits this approach. A small crew, one location, one shoot day, and an edit is achievable. It would not fund actors, multiple locations, or original music. Being clear about that up front is better than discovering it in the edit.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly refuses to write a proposal from an inadequate brief and identifies the gaps systematically rather than impressionistically — particularly the audience question, correctly recognised as the answer on which everything else depends. The clarifying questions are drafted as an actual sendable email, are grouped logically, and demonstrate professional judgement in themselves: the suggestion of vertical social cuts, the recommendation of staged approvals, and the query about whether the deadline is delivery or first cut all add value while asking. The unprompted identification of safe messaging obligations is the strongest element — it is ethically correct, commercially credible, and shows genuine subject-matter engagement rather than treating the topic as generic content. The observation that a young person's consent when well may not represent how they feel about permanent public content years later is a sophisticated and genuinely important point that most practitioners would miss. The creative direction is well-reasoned from the audience outward, correctly identifies authenticity as the central risk, and is honestly matched to the available budget. Strong Competent.
