## What This Unit Is About

Audiences will tolerate a mediocre image with good sound. They will switch off good images with bad sound within seconds. This is the most consistently underestimated fact in screen production, and it is why sound recordists exist as a distinct discipline.

CUASOU313 asks you to capture clean, usable audio to a professional standard — choosing and placing the right microphone, setting correct levels, controlling the acoustic environment, and delivering files that work in post-production.

**The governing principle: audio problems are far harder to fix in post than picture problems.** A poorly exposed shot can often be graded. A recording with room reverb, air conditioning rumble, and clipped peaks frequently cannot be saved at all.

## Microphone Fundamentals

### Transducer Types

| Type | How It Works | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dynamic** | Moving coil in a magnetic field | Robust, handles high volume, no power required, less sensitive | Live vocals, loud sources, harsh environments |
| **Condenser** | Charged capacitor plate | Sensitive, detailed, extended frequency response, **requires power** | Studio, dialogue, location boom work, ambience |
| **Electret condenser** | Permanently charged condenser | Small, sensitive, low cost | Lavalier mics, camera mics, consumer devices |

**Phantom power (+48V)** is supplied down the XLR cable to power condenser microphones. Dynamic microphones do not need it and are generally unharmed by it, but always check — some ribbon microphones can be damaged by phantom power.

### Polar Patterns

The polar pattern describes the directions from which a microphone picks up sound. **Choosing the right pattern is often more important than choosing an expensive microphone.**

| Pattern | Pickup | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Omnidirectional** | Equal in all directions | Natural sound, no proximity effect, less handling noise | Ambience, lavaliers, roomtone |
| **Cardioid** | Heart-shaped — front, rejects rear | General-purpose directional | Interviews, general dialogue, presenters |
| **Supercardioid / Hypercardioid** | Narrower front, small rear lobe | More directional than cardioid; **has a rear pickup lobe** | Boom work, noisy environments |
| **Shotgun (lobar)** | Highly directional forward lobe | Rejects off-axis sound; long interference tube | Location dialogue, outdoor boom |
| **Figure-8 (bidirectional)** | Front and rear, rejects sides | Two-person face-to-face; mid-side recording | Interviews across a table |

**Critical practical point about supercardioid and shotgun microphones:** they have a **rear lobe**. Pointing a shotgun at your subject with a noisy generator directly behind *you* will pick up the generator. Directionality is not a shield — you must think about what is behind the mic as well as in front of it.

**Shotgun microphones indoors:** shotguns work by cancelling off-axis sound arriving through an interference tube. In a small reflective room, reflections arrive from all directions and the cancellation becomes uneven, producing a hollow, phasey sound. **In small rooms, a cardioid or hypercardioid usually outperforms a shotgun.** This is counterintuitive and commonly got wrong.

### Microphone Types in Screen Production

| Type | Mounting | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boom mic** | On a pole, above or below frame | Best sound quality; natural perspective; operator can follow the subject | Requires an operator; must stay out of frame and out of shadow |
| **Lavalier (lapel)** | Clipped to clothing | Consistent level regardless of movement; unobtrusive; good in noisy or reverberant spaces | Clothing rustle; limited by placement; sounds "close" and less natural |
| **Handheld** | Held or on a stand | Robust; presenter control; visible authority in news | Visible in frame; handling noise |
| **Camera-mounted** | On the camera | Convenient | Distance from subject means poor dialogue quality — **use only as a backup or guide track** |
| **Plant mic** | Hidden in the set | Covers a fixed position | Only works where the action stays put |

**The distance rule:** sound quality degrades rapidly with distance. **The inverse square law** means doubling the distance from source to mic reduces the direct sound level by 6dB — while room reverb and background noise stay constant. This is why proximity matters more than microphone price. **A cheap microphone close to the subject beats an expensive microphone far away, every time.**

**Boom positioning:** typically above the subject, just out of frame, angled down toward the mouth. Below and angled up is the alternative when the frame allows — it can be cleaner because it points away from overhead noise sources like air conditioning. Watch for the boom's **shadow**, which is as visible a mistake as the boom itself entering frame.

## Levels and Gain

### Understanding dBFS

Digital audio is measured in **dBFS** (decibels relative to Full Scale). **0 dBFS is the absolute maximum** — there is nothing above it. Exceeding it produces **clipping**: the waveform is squared off, generating harsh distortion that **cannot be repaired**.

### Target Levels

| Content | Target average | Peaks |
|---|---|---|
| **Dialogue** | Around −18 to −12 dBFS | Not above −6 dBFS |
| **Loud/unpredictable sources** | Lower average | Leave more headroom |

**Headroom** is the gap between your working level and 0 dBFS. It exists so that an unexpected loud moment — a laugh, a door slam, a raised voice — does not clip.

**Recording too quietly is the safer error but not a free one.** Very low levels require heavy gain in post, which raises the noise floor with the signal, producing hiss. Aim for a healthy level with headroom rather than either extreme.

### Metering

- **Peak meters** show instantaneous maximum — essential for avoiding clipping
- **VU / average meters** show perceived loudness
- **Watch the meters continuously while recording.** Levels are not "set and forget" — subjects get louder when they become animated

### Limiters

A **limiter** prevents the signal exceeding a set ceiling. Most professional recorders have one. Using a limiter as a **safety net** is good practice. Relying on it to fix consistently excessive levels is not — heavy limiting audibly squashes the sound.

### Automatic Gain Control (AGC)

Consumer cameras often apply **AGC**, which continuously adjusts gain to keep levels constant. **Turn it off.** AGC produces audible pumping — it raises the noise floor during pauses in speech, making room noise swell up and down between sentences. Manual gain control, set with headroom, is always preferable.

## Controlling the Environment

**The single highest-value skill in location sound is choosing and controlling the space** before you touch a microphone.

### Room Acoustics

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| **Reverb / echo** | Hard parallel surfaces — glass, tile, concrete, bare plasterboard | Add soft material — blankets, curtains, cushions, clothing racks; move away from walls; get the mic closer |
| **Boxy / boomy sound** | Small hard room resonating at low frequencies | Move to a different space; add absorption; high-pass filter |
| **Flutter echo** | Parallel hard walls | Break up the parallel — introduce absorption on one side |

**Practical rule:** a bedroom with carpet, curtains, and a bed sounds vastly better than a large tiled foyer, regardless of equipment. Choose the room before you choose the microphone.

### Noise Sources — Find and Kill

Before recording, **stop and listen for thirty seconds** with your eyes closed. You will hear things you have been unconsciously filtering out.

| Source | Action |
|---|---|
| **Air conditioning / HVAC** | Turn it off. This is the single most common location audio problem. Set a reminder to turn it back on |
| **Refrigerators** | Unplug — with a note on the door so it is not forgotten |
| **Fluorescent lights and dimmers** | Buzz; can induce electrical hum. Switch to other lighting where possible |
| **Computers, projectors, servers** | Fan noise — shut down or relocate |
| **Traffic, aircraft, construction** | Reschedule, relocate, or wait. Aircraft pass; construction does not |
| **Phones** | Everyone to flight mode — including yours. A phone on a table causes GSM buzz in audio even on silent |
| **Clothing** | Nylon, leather, and stiff fabrics rustle. Address at wardrobe stage |
| **Jewellery** | Bracelets and necklaces click against lavalier mics |

**Handling and cable noise:** use a shock mount, dress cables so they do not rub, and secure cable to the boom pole. A "strain relief" loop at the lavalier prevents cable movement transferring to the capsule.

### Wind

Outdoors, wind noise is the primary enemy — it produces low-frequency rumble that can overwhelm a recording entirely.

**Protection, in ascending order of effectiveness:**
1. **Foam windscreen** — minimal; indoor use and very light breeze only
2. **Furry cover ("deadcat", windjammer")** — effective for moderate wind
3. **Blimp / zeppelin with furry cover** — a rigid basket with fur; the professional outdoor solution
4. **Physical shielding** — position yourself, a vehicle, or a building to block the wind

**A high-pass filter** (rolling off low frequencies below roughly 80Hz) further reduces wind rumble and handling noise without meaningfully affecting speech intelligibility. Applying a gentle high-pass on dialogue is standard practice.

## Recording Practice

### Room Tone — Never Skip This

**Room tone** (or "atmos", "presence") is 30–60 seconds of the recording location with nobody speaking and nothing happening.

**Why it is essential:** editors use room tone to fill gaps, smooth edits, and cover joins. Without it, silence between edited lines is *actually* silent, and the abrupt drop from room noise to digital black is jarringly obvious.

**Record it at every location, at every setup where the sound changes.** It takes one minute and every editor you ever work with will be grateful. Announce it clearly — "Room tone, everyone hold, thirty seconds" — because people will otherwise start talking.

### Slate and Sync

Where audio is recorded separately from the camera (**dual system**), the two must be synchronised in post.

- **Clapperboard** — the clap provides a sharp transient in audio and a visible frame of contact in picture, allowing manual alignment
- **Timecode** — professional cameras and recorders can share timecode, enabling automatic sync
- **Waveform sync software** — matches the camera's scratch audio to the good audio automatically

**Always record a guide track to camera even when recording separately.** It is what waveform sync uses, and it is your fallback if the separate recording fails.

### Redundancy

Professional practice records a safety:
- **Dual-channel recording** — the same source recorded at two different levels, one several dB lower as a safety against unexpected peaks
- **Two microphones** where possible — boom plus lavalier gives options in post and a backup if one fails
- **Check the recording is actually running.** The most expensive error in sound recording is discovering afterwards that nothing was captured. Confirm at the start, and monitor throughout

### Monitoring

**Wear closed-back headphones and actually listen.** Meters show level; they do not show rustle, distortion, interference, a failing cable, or an air conditioner that just cycled on.

**Open-back headphones are not suitable for location work** — they leak sound into the microphone.

**Listen for:** clothing rustle, cable noise, radio interference and dropouts on wireless, distortion, mouth noise, background changes, and — critically — that the mic is on the right subject.

## File Formats and Delivery

| Format | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **WAV (PCM)** | Uncompressed, lossless | **The professional standard for recording** |
| **BWF (Broadcast WAV)** | WAV with embedded metadata and timecode | Professional production |
| **AIFF** | Uncompressed, lossless | Equivalent to WAV, Apple-origin |
| **MP3 / AAC** | Lossy compressed | **Delivery only — never for recording** |
| **FLAC** | Lossless compressed | Archival |

### Sample Rate and Bit Depth

| Setting | Common values | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sample rate** | 48 kHz standard for video; 44.1 kHz for music-only; 96 kHz for high-end or heavy sound design | **Use 48 kHz for anything going with picture** |
| **Bit depth** | 16-bit, 24-bit, 32-bit float | **24-bit is standard professional practice** — it provides far more dynamic range and headroom than 16-bit |

**32-bit float recording**, available on newer recorders, captures such a wide dynamic range that clipping becomes practically impossible — levels can be corrected in post even from apparently overloaded recordings. Where available it is a significant safety net, but it does not remove the need for good technique.

**Never record dialogue as MP3.** Lossy compression discards data permanently, and further processing in post compounds the artefacts.

### File Management

- **Consistent naming** — project, scene, take, date
- **Sound report / log** — what was recorded on which file, which mic, any problems noted. This saves the editor hours
- **Back up before formatting cards** — 3-2-1 rule, same as picture

## Legal and Consent

- **Consent to record** — obtain it, and for broadcast or commercial use obtain a signed **release**
- **Surveillance devices legislation** — Australian states regulate recording private conversations without consent. Recording people covertly can be a criminal offence
- **Music** — see CUAIND312. Recording a location where copyright music is playing creates a clearance problem. **Ask for background music to be turned off** — this is both an audio quality and a legal issue
- **Sound effects and library audio** — licensed; check the terms

**[VERIFY: Surveillance devices and listening devices legislation varies significantly between Australian states and territories. Confirm the position in your jurisdiction before recording any conversation without express consent.]**

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are recording a 20-minute interview with a business owner for a corporate video. The only available location is her office: a small room with a glass wall on one side, a tiled floor, a desk with a computer, a wall-mounted air conditioner, and a window facing a main road. She will be seated at the desk. You have a shotgun mic on a boom, a lavalier, a recorder, and headphones.*

*Describe your approach. What will you do before you record anything, and what microphone choice will you make and why?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This room is close to a worst-case acoustic environment: glass and tile are both hard reflective surfaces, and there are three separate noise sources. I would spend the first ten minutes on the room, not the equipment — that is where the recording will actually be won or lost.

---

**Before anything else — listen.**

I would stand in the room, close my eyes, and listen for thirty seconds. This always reveals things I have been filtering out unconsciously — a fan, a hum, a distant plant room. I would then walk the room listening for where reflections are worst.

---

**Step 1 — Kill the noise sources.**

| Source | Action |
|---|---|
| **Air conditioner** | **Turn it off.** This is the single most important action in the whole setup. AC rumble is broadband, constant, and effectively impossible to remove cleanly in post. I would set a phone reminder to turn it back on afterwards, and warn her the room will get warm — 20 minutes is tolerable |
| **Computer** | Shut it down, or at minimum sleep it. Fan noise is constant and sits right in the frequency range that muddies dialogue |
| **Road noise through the window** | Close the window if it is open. Position her so the window is **behind the microphone's rejection axis** rather than behind her. Accept that some traffic will remain — record room tone so the editor can work with a consistent bed |
| **Phones** | Hers and mine to flight mode. A phone on the desk causes GSM buzz even on silent |
| **Desk items** | Move anything she might tap, click, or shuffle. Ask her to keep hands off the desk surface |
| **Jewellery** | Check for bracelets or a necklace that will click against a lavalier |

---

**Step 2 — Treat the acoustics.**

Glass wall plus tiled floor means significant reflection and a hard, hollow sound.

- **Move her away from the glass wall.** Distance from a reflective surface reduces its contribution. If the desk can be repositioned, or if she can sit at a different side of it, that alone helps considerably
- **Break up the reflections.** Anything soft helps — a coat on the back of the door, a jacket over a chair placed just out of frame, cushions, even a rug or a couple of blankets laid on the tile out of shot. This looks improvised because it is, and it works
- **Get the mic closer.** This is the most powerful acoustic tool I have. The inverse square law means halving the mic distance gains 6dB of direct sound while the reverb stays constant — dramatically improving the direct-to-reverberant ratio. Proximity is my primary weapon against a bad room

---

**Step 3 — Microphone choice. This is where I would deviate from the obvious answer.**

**I would use the lavalier as my primary microphone, not the shotgun.**

The instinctive choice is the shotgun on a boom because it generally sounds better. In this room it will not. Shotgun microphones work by cancelling off-axis sound arriving down an interference tube. In a small, hard, reflective room, reflections arrive from many directions at once and that cancellation becomes uneven — producing a hollow, phasey, coloured sound. **Shotguns are outdoor and large-space microphones.**

The lavalier is the right choice here because:
- It sits a few centimetres from the mouth, giving me a very high direct-to-reverberant ratio — it is close enough that the room barely matters
- It gives a consistent level regardless of how much she moves or turns
- It is an omnidirectional or cardioid capsule at very close range, which is exactly the right tool in a reflective space
- She is seated and static, which is ideal lavalier conditions
- It frees me from operating a boom for twenty minutes

**I would still rig the boom as a second channel** if I can record two tracks — but positioned as a **hypercardioid or with the shotgun close in**, not at typical shotgun distance. Two sources gives me a backup if the lav fails, rustles, or drops out, and gives the editor options.

**If I only had the shotgun**, I would bring it much closer than usual — just out of frame, well within a metre — and accept a tighter shot to allow it.

---

**Step 4 — Rig the lavalier properly.**

- **Placement** — high on the chest, roughly a hand's width below the chin, centred. Too low and it sounds dull and distant; too high and it catches breath and mouth noise
- **Clothing** — check the fabric. Silk, nylon, and stiff synthetics rustle badly. If her top is problematic, reposition to a collar or lapel, or use tape and a fabric cover
- **Cable strain relief** — a small loop of cable taped just below the capsule, so any cable movement is absorbed by the loop rather than transferred into the microphone
- **Route the cable** down inside clothing where possible, secured so it cannot swing
- **Test by asking her to move** — turn her head, lean forward, gesture — and listen for rustle before committing

---

**Step 5 — Levels and monitoring.**

- **Set gain during a genuine conversation, not a test count.** People speak at a very different level when they say "test, one, two" versus when they are actually talking. I would chat with her about something unrelated and set levels off that
- **Target around −18 to −12 dBFS average, peaks no higher than −6 dBFS.** Interview subjects get louder as they warm up and become animated, so I want real headroom
- **Enable the limiter** as a safety net, not as a working tool
- **Disable any AGC**
- **Apply a gentle high-pass filter** around 80Hz to reduce road rumble and handling noise without touching speech intelligibility
- **Record 24-bit / 48 kHz WAV** — 48 kHz because it is going with picture, 24-bit for dynamic range
- **Monitor on closed-back headphones throughout.** Meters will not tell me about rustle, a dropout, or the air conditioner cycling back on

---

**Step 6 — Before rolling.**

- **Confirm the recorder is actually recording.** Roll, speak, stop, play back, listen. Then roll for real. Discovering after 20 minutes that nothing was captured is the most expensive mistake in this discipline
- **Record a slate** — project, subject, date
- Ask her to **repeat the question in her answer**, since my questions may not be used
- Ask her to **pause briefly before answering** — it gives the editor a clean edit point

---

**Step 7 — After the interview, before packing up.**

- **Record 60 seconds of room tone.** Announce it clearly and ask everyone to hold still and silent, because otherwise someone will start talking at second three. This is non-negotiable — the editor needs it to fill gaps and smooth edits, and it must be recorded in the same room with the same setup and the same AC state
- **Turn the air conditioner back on** and restart the computer
- **Complete a sound report** — files, channels, mic used, any issues noted (e.g. "traffic audible at 04:20, siren at 11:50")
- **Back up the files** before leaving, and again before formatting the card

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding — and the microphone decision is what elevates it. Most candidates reach for the shotgun on the assumption that it is the "better" microphone; this student correctly identifies that a shotgun's interference-tube cancellation degrades in a small reflective space and selects the lavalier on sound acoustic reasoning, while still rigging a second source for redundancy. The prioritisation is professional throughout: the student spends effort on the room before the equipment, correctly identifies the air conditioner as the highest-value single action, and invokes the inverse square law as the primary defence against a bad room rather than as abstract theory. Practical details — setting gain from real conversation rather than a test count, strain relief loops, checking fabric rustle by asking the subject to move, confirming the recorder is running by playing back — are all things learned from doing the job rather than reading about it. Room tone is recorded, announced, and correctly specified as same-room-same-setup. The sound report with timecoded problem notes shows genuine consideration for post. Strong Competent.
