## What This Unit Is About

The sale of property is the transaction that brings together everything in the CPP41419 qualification — the appraisal, the marketing, the vendor relationship, the buyer relationship, the legal framework. CPPREP4105 asks you to demonstrate that you understand the entire sales process from preparing the property for sale through to the exchange of contracts and the period leading to settlement.

---

## The Property Sales Process — Overview

| Stage | Key Activities |
|---|---|
| **1. Pre-listing** | Appraisal, agency agreement, property preparation advice, marketing plan |
| **2. Campaign launch** | Listing goes live, inspections begin, buyer enquiry |
| **3. Active marketing** | Ongoing inspections, buyer follow-up, vendor updates |
| **4. Offer and negotiation** | Offers received, presented to vendor, negotiated |
| **5. Exchange** | Offer accepted, contracts exchanged |
| **6. Conditional period** | Finance and building/pest conditions (if applicable) |
| **7. Settlement** | Transfer of title, payment of purchase price |

The agent's primary role ends at exchange. Post-exchange matters (finance approval, settlement) are primarily managed by the parties' solicitors or conveyancers.

---

## Preparing a Property for Sale

A well-presented property typically achieves a better price. The agent's role is to advise vendors on presentation, not to act as an interior designer. Common advice includes:

- **Declutter and clean:** Remove personal items, clean thoroughly
- **Minor repairs:** Fix obvious defects (broken fly screens, leaking taps, scuffed walls)
- **Kerb appeal:** First impressions on arrival; garden presentation, driveway, front door
- **Styling:** Professional property styling for vacant properties or where presentation is significantly below buyer expectations
- **Photography timing:** Shoot on a fine day; ensure property is at its best

**Vendors have the right to make their own decisions about presentation.** The agent advises; the vendor decides. Document the advice given and the vendor's response.

## Contract for Sale

Before a residential property can be marketed in most Australian states, a **Contract for Sale** (or contract of sale) must be prepared by the vendor's solicitor or licensed conveyancer. The agent cannot prepare the contract.

**The contract includes:**
- Details of the property (address, title reference, lot/plan number)
- Purchase price
- Deposit amount and payment date
- Settlement date
- List of inclusions and exclusions
- Vendor's disclosure documents (e.g., Section 32 in VIC; vendor's disclosure statement in other states)
- Special conditions agreed by both parties

**The agent's role regarding the contract:**
- Ensure a contract is available for inspection at open homes
- Provide the contract to interested buyers on request
- Transmit offers and accepted terms to the relevant solicitors/conveyancers who formalise the exchange

## Negotiation

Negotiation between vendor and buyer is managed by the agent. Key principles:

### The Agent's Position
The agent negotiates on behalf of the vendor — to achieve the best possible price and terms for the vendor. However, the agent must:
- Present all offers to the vendor, including low offers
- Not misrepresent either party's position to the other
- Not create false urgency or false competition (e.g., fabricating competing offers)

### Negotiation Strategies

**Price negotiation:**
When a buyer makes an offer below the asking price, the typical response is a counter-offer at a higher level, seeking to find a point acceptable to both parties. The agent's role is to:
- Present the offer to the vendor with context
- Present the vendor's counter-offer to the buyer with context
- Keep both parties engaged through the process without pressuring either

**Managing the gap:**
When there is a significant gap between what the vendor wants and what the buyer will pay, options include:
- Price adjustment (the most direct solution)
- Terms adjustment (longer/shorter settlement; deposit variation; inclusion of chattels)
- Patience — sometimes a buyer needs time to adjust expectations

### What the Agent Cannot Do
- Accept an offer on the vendor's behalf without specific authority
- Disclose one buyer's offer price to another buyer
- Fabricate competing offers to pressure a buyer
- Make representations about the vendor's position that are not true

## Exchange of Contracts

**Exchange** is the moment at which both parties become legally bound to the transaction. Before exchange, either party can withdraw without legal consequence (subject to any holding deposit arrangement). After exchange, withdrawal typically results in forfeiture of the deposit (buyer) or significant damages liability (vendor).

**The exchange process:**
1. Vendor signs the contract
2. Buyer signs an identical copy of the contract
3. The agent or solicitors effect the exchange — often by physically swapping signed copies, though electronic exchange is increasingly common
4. A deposit is paid (typically 10% of purchase price, though 5% or other amounts are negotiated)
5. The cooling-off period (if applicable) begins

**Cooling-off period:**
In most states, residential property buyers have a statutory cooling-off period after exchange during which they can rescind the contract by paying a small penalty (typically 0.25% of the purchase price). Cooling-off periods do not apply to auction purchases in most states. [VERIFY: state-specific cooling-off rules]

## After Exchange — The Agent's Role

After exchange, the agent's active sales role effectively ends. However, the agent should:
- Ensure both parties' solicitors/conveyancers have the executed contracts
- Remove the property from active marketing (update listings to "Under Contract")
- Ensure the deposit is banked correctly (into the trust account)
- Monitor the conditional period (if applicable) and advise the vendor if conditions are not being satisfied

If a condition fails (e.g., finance not approved, unsatisfactory building inspection), the buyer may rescind. The vendor will need advice on next steps — relisting, negotiating an extension, or accepting a reduced price.

## Auction

Auction is an alternative method of sale in which buyers bid openly and the highest bidder at or above the reserve price wins. The auction is conducted by a licensed auctioneer.

**Key auction concepts:**

- **Reserve price:** The minimum price below which the vendor will not sell; set by the vendor before the auction
- **Vendor bid:** A bid made on behalf of the vendor (to start bidding or to keep it moving); regulated in all states — must be clearly announced as a vendor bid; dummy bidding (using a third party to falsely bid up the price) is a criminal offence
- **Passed in:** If bidding does not reach the reserve, the property is "passed in" — the highest bidder typically has the first right to negotiate privately
- **Sold under the hammer:** If the reserve is met or exceeded, the property is sold unconditionally at the fall of the hammer — no cooling-off period applies

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are acting as the auctioneer's assistant at an auction. After 10 minutes of bidding, there are two active bidders. Buyer A is at $920,000. The vendor's reserve is $930,000. Your principal whispers to you: "Get my friend Marcus to make a bid to push it over the reserve." What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I would not arrange for Marcus to bid. What my principal is describing is dummy bidding — using a person who is not a genuine buyer to artificially inflate the bidding price. Dummy bidding is a criminal offence under real estate legislation in all Australian states. It is also a fundamental breach of auction integrity and consumer protection legislation.

I would say to my principal, clearly and professionally: "I can't do that — that would be dummy bidding, which is illegal. We need to work with what we have."

**The legal position:**
Dummy bidding is prohibited across Australia. It deceives genuine buyers into believing there is more competition than exists, forcing them to bid higher than they otherwise would. Penalties include fines and, in serious cases, criminal charges and licence cancellation — for both the auctioneer and the agent involved.

**What are the legitimate options?**
1. **Vendor bid:** The auctioneer can make a vendor bid (on behalf of the vendor) — but this must be clearly announced as such ("I have a vendor bid at $925,000"). This is not the same as arranging a third party to bid as a genuine buyer.
2. **Negotiate after passing in:** If the reserve is not met, the property is passed in to Buyer A as the highest bidder. The vendor can then negotiate privately with Buyer A.
3. **Accept the bids received:** If Buyer A's next bid reaches or exceeds $930,000, the property sells. If not, it passes in.

Regardless of whether the property sells today, arranging a dummy bid is not an option I will be party to.

I would document the instruction I received and my response, and I would discuss the matter with my principal after the auction — including the legal exposure they created by asking me to do this.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies dummy bidding as illegal, refuses the instruction clearly and professionally, explains the legal framework, offers legitimate alternatives, and correctly identifies the need to document and escalate. The student does not equivocate — "I can't do that" is correct; it is not a judgment call or a matter of preference. This is exactly what a competent and ethical agent looks like under pressure. Strong Competent.
