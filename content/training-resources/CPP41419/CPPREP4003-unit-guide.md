## What This Unit Is About

Real estate practice is heavily regulated. Every transaction, every client relationship, every piece of marketing material, and every fee arrangement is subject to legislation. An agent who does not know what the law requires is an agent who will eventually breach it — and in real estate, breaches can mean lost licences, civil claims, and criminal charges.

CPPREP4003 asks you to demonstrate that you can access the legislation relevant to real estate practice, understand what it requires, and apply it to practical situations. You do not need to memorise every section of every Act — but you need to know what the key legislation is, what it covers, and how to find and apply specific requirements.

## The Legislative Framework for Real Estate

Real estate agents operate within multiple layers of legislation:

### 1. State Real Estate Licensing Legislation
This is the primary regulatory framework for agents — it governs who can act as an agent, what they must do, and what happens when they breach their obligations.

| State | Primary Act |
|---|---|
| NSW | Property and Stock Agents Act 2002 |
| VIC | Estate Agents Act 1980 |
| QLD | Property Occupations Act 2014 |
| SA | Land Agents Act 1994 |
| WA | Real Estate and Business Agents Act 1978 |
| ACT | Agents Act 2003 |
| NT | Agents Licensing Act 1979 |
| TAS | Property Agents and Land Transactions Act 2016 |

These Acts cover:
- Licensing requirements for agents
- Agency agreement requirements
- Trust account obligations
- Disclosure requirements
- Grounds for disciplinary action and licence cancellation
- Consumer protection in real estate transactions

### 2. Consumer Protection Legislation

**Australian Consumer Law (ACL)** — Schedule 2 to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth)

The ACL applies to all businesses conducting commercial activity in Australia, including real estate agents. Key provisions:
- **Misleading and deceptive conduct (s18):** Must not engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive
- **Unconscionable conduct (s20–22):** Must not engage in conduct that is unconscionable in connection with the supply of services
- **False representations (s29):** Must not make false or misleading representations about services, price, or characteristics

### 3. Residential Tenancies Legislation

Property managers must understand the residential tenancies legislation in their state, as it governs virtually every aspect of the landlord-tenant relationship:

| State | Primary Act |
|---|---|
| NSW | Residential Tenancies Act 2010 |
| VIC | Residential Tenancies Act 1997 |
| QLD | Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008 |
| SA | Residential Tenancies Act 1995 |
| WA | Residential Tenancies Act 1987 |
| ACT | Residential Tenancies Act 1997 |
| NT | Residential Tenancies Act 1999 |
| TAS | Residential Tenancy Act 1997 |

Key areas covered: security deposits (bonds), entry notices, repairs and maintenance, rent increases, ending tenancies, dispute resolution.

### 4. Anti-Discrimination Legislation

| Legislation | Protected Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth) | Race, ethnicity, national origin |
| Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth) | Sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, pregnancy |
| Age Discrimination Act 2004 (Cth) | Age |
| Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth) | Disability |
| State anti-discrimination legislation | Varies — generally adds religion, political opinion, and other characteristics |

Real estate agents and property managers cannot refuse to sell or lease to someone based on a protected characteristic.

### 5. Privacy Legislation

**Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)** and **Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)**

Agents collect substantial personal information about clients, buyers, tenants, and landlords. The Privacy Act requires:
- Collection only of information that is reasonably necessary
- Secure storage and restricted access
- Not disclosing personal information to third parties without consent (subject to exceptions)
- Providing individuals access to their own information on request

### 6. Anti-Money Laundering Legislation

**Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006 (Cth)**

Real estate is a known vehicle for money laundering (purchasing properties with proceeds of crime). Agents have obligations to:
- Conduct customer due diligence (verify identity of clients)
- Report suspicious transactions
- Keep records of transactions

## Interpreting Legislation — How to Do It

Assessment tasks frequently ask you to interpret a legislative provision. This does not mean reading every word aloud — it means extracting the obligation that applies.

**Step-by-step approach:**

1. **Identify which Act applies** — what area of law is this (licensing, tenancies, consumer protection)?
2. **Find the relevant provision** — use the Act's table of contents or index; most legislation is now freely available at legislation.gov.au or your state's legislation website
3. **Read the provision carefully** — identify the key terms (who it applies to, what they must or must not do, in what circumstances)
4. **Define any defined terms** — many Acts define key terms in a definitions section; the everyday meaning may differ from the legal definition
5. **Identify exceptions or qualifications** — many obligations are qualified by conditions ("unless," "except where," "so far as is reasonably practicable")
6. **Apply to the facts** — does this provision apply to the situation? What does it require?

## Key Legislative Concepts

### Material Disclosure
Agents have a duty to disclose material facts — information that a reasonable buyer, seller, tenant, or landlord would consider relevant to their decision. Failure to disclose can:
- Expose the agent and vendor to legal liability
- Entitle the buyer to rescind the contract
- Constitute fraudulent misrepresentation in serious cases

### Cooling-Off Periods
Most residential property contracts in Australia include a cooling-off period during which the buyer can rescind without forfeiting the purchase price (though a small penalty may apply). Details vary significantly by state:
- Duration (typically 2–5 business days)
- Whether it applies (auction purchases are typically excluded)
- How it is waived (requires a certificate from a solicitor or conveyancer in some states)

### Trust Accounting
All money received by an agent on behalf of a client must be held in a trust account — a separately maintained bank account. Trust account requirements include:
- Monthly reconciliation
- Audit requirements
- Immediate banking of receipts
- Prohibition on withdrawing money for commission before it is due

### Auction Regulations
Auctions are subject to specific legislative requirements in each state covering:
- Dummy bidding (prohibited — a form of fraud)
- Vendor bidding (permitted in some states with specific disclosure requirements)
- Reserve price (the minimum price below which a property cannot be sold)
- Passed-in properties (what happens when no bid reaches the reserve)

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are a property manager in NSW. Your landlord, Mr Patel, wants to enter the property to do an inspection. He tells you he is going to go to the property tomorrow morning without giving the tenant any notice because "it's my property and I have a right to go in." The tenant is currently living there. What do you tell Mr Patel, and what are the relevant legal obligations?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

Mr Patel has a fundamental misunderstanding of his rights as a landlord. While he owns the property, the tenant has a right to quiet enjoyment — a right to use the property without interference. This right exists in common law and is codified in residential tenancies legislation.

**Relevant legislation — NSW:**
The Residential Tenancies Act 2010 (NSW) sets out when and how a landlord (or their agent) can enter a rented property. Entry without following the correct notice requirements is a breach of the Act and potentially a civil wrong against the tenant.

**Notice requirements for routine inspection (NSW):**
A landlord or agent can enter for a routine inspection but must give at least 7 days' written notice to the tenant. The entry can only be at a reasonable time. There are limits on how many routine inspections can be conducted per year (typically 4 in NSW).

**Mr Patel cannot:**
- Enter the property tomorrow without notice
- Enter at any time without appropriate notice regardless of reason (with narrow exceptions for emergencies)

**What I would tell Mr Patel:**
I would explain, professionally and clearly, that entering the property without the required notice would breach the Residential Tenancies Act 2010 (NSW) and could expose him to a complaint by the tenant to the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal (NCAT). I would explain that I, as the property manager, have a legal obligation to act within the law when managing his property — and that includes following correct entry notice procedures.

If Mr Patel wants to conduct an inspection, I would arrange it properly: serve the tenant with 7 days' written notice of the date, time, and purpose of the entry.

**[VERIFY: Current notice periods and entry frequency limits under the Residential Tenancies Act 2010 NSW — these can be amended. Check NSW Fair Trading's current guidance.]**

**Examiner Annotation:** This response correctly identifies the relevant legislation (Residential Tenancies Act 2010 NSW), explains the tenant's right to quiet enjoyment, gives the specific notice requirement, explains the consequences of breach, and describes what the agent would do. The student correctly positions themselves as having a professional obligation to act within the law — not simply following the landlord's instruction. Strong Competent.
