## What This Unit Is About

Managing a tenancy is the ongoing work of property management — from the moment the tenant moves in to the moment they vacate. It requires balancing the landlord's interests (protecting the investment, maximising income) with the tenant's rights (quiet enjoyment, liveable conditions, due process). Both must be managed within the legal framework of residential tenancies legislation.

CPPREP4123 covers the full lifecycle of tenancy management: rent collection, maintenance, inspections, renewals, tenancy issues, and ending the tenancy in a legally correct way.

---

## The Landlord's Obligations to Tenants

A landlord (and their managing agent) has legal obligations to tenants that exist independently of the management agreement. These include:

- **Fitness for habitation:** The property must be habitable — structurally sound, with functioning locks, windows, hot water, sanitation, and electricity
- **Quiet enjoyment:** The tenant has the right to live in the property without unreasonable interference
- **Repairs and maintenance:** The landlord must carry out repairs in a timely manner — urgent/emergency repairs require immediate response; non-urgent repairs must be addressed within a reasonable time
- **Lawful entry only:** The landlord (and agent) can only enter the property with the required notice and at permitted times
- **Bond handling:** The bond must be lodged correctly and can only be claimed for specific, lawful purposes

## Rent Management

### Collecting Rent
Rent is typically collected electronically into the agency's trust account, then disbursed to the landlord (less management fees) in accordance with the disbursement cycle (usually monthly).

### Rent Arrears Process
Rent arrears are one of the most common property management challenges. The correct process:

**Step 1 — Contact:** When rent is overdue, contact the tenant promptly by phone or text. Often arrears are due to a banking issue, missed payment, or genuine oversight that is quickly resolved.

**Step 2 — Formal notice:** If arrears are not resolved within the time required by state legislation, serve a formal notice of breach (notice to remedy breach or similar). The notice gives the tenant a specified time to pay the outstanding amount.

**Step 3 — Tribunal application:** If the breach is not remedied within the notice period, the property manager can apply to the tenancy tribunal (NCAT in NSW, VCAT in VIC, QCAT in QLD, etc.) for an order — including an order for possession of the property (eviction).

**Step 4 — Order for possession:** If the tribunal makes an order for possession, the tenant must vacate by the date specified. If they do not, the property manager can apply for enforcement — which in most states involves police attendance, not the agent acting alone.

**Critical:** At no stage does the property manager have the right to:
- Change the locks
- Remove the tenant's belongings
- Cut off utilities
- Harass or pressure the tenant by illegal means

These are illegal means of eviction and constitute serious breaches of the Residential Tenancies Act and potentially criminal law.

## Maintenance and Repairs

### Urgent / Emergency Repairs
Urgent repairs are those that make the property unsafe or uninhabitable, or that involve essential services. They require immediate attention and must be arranged even if the landlord cannot be contacted.

**Typically classified as urgent:**
- Burst water pipe
- Gas leak
- Electrical fault creating a hazard
- No hot water service
- Roof that is leaking significantly
- Broken/non-functioning locks or windows (security issue)
- Failure of heating in extreme cold weather

The property management agreement typically specifies a disbursement limit for emergency repairs — the amount the property manager can spend without prior landlord approval. If the repair exceeds this, the property manager must attempt to contact the landlord but must still arrange repairs if the situation is urgent.

### Routine / Non-Urgent Repairs
Routine maintenance requests should be communicated to the landlord promptly and actioned within a reasonable timeframe. The property manager should document:
- The request (date received, nature of issue)
- Communication to the landlord
- The landlord's instruction
- Trade arranged and date completed

Allowing significant maintenance to remain unaddressed risks:
- Tenant compensation claims (for breach of quiet enjoyment or habitability)
- Further property damage (a small leak becomes a large problem)
- Tenancy tribunal orders against the landlord

## Routine Inspections

Routine inspections serve two purposes: ensuring the property is being maintained by the tenant and identifying maintenance issues before they become serious.

**Inspection protocol:**
1. Provide written notice to the tenant (typically 7 days — [VERIFY by state])
2. Conduct the inspection at a reasonable time
3. Complete the inspection report (internal and external)
4. Take photographs
5. Report to the landlord promptly
6. Identify any maintenance issues for action
7. If the tenant has caused damage beyond fair wear and tear, document and raise with the landlord

**Fair wear and tear vs damage:**
Fair wear and tear is the deterioration that occurs through normal use over time — it is not chargeable to the tenant. Damage beyond fair wear and tear (a hole in a wall, stained carpet caused by spilling, broken fixtures) can be the subject of a bond claim. The distinction matters enormously at the end of the tenancy.

## Tenancy Renewals

Before a fixed-term tenancy expires, the property manager should:
- Contact the tenant to ascertain their intentions (do they wish to stay?)
- Advise the landlord of the tenant's position and current market rent
- If both parties agree to renew: prepare and execute a new fixed-term agreement at agreed rent
- If the tenancy rolls over to periodic: advise both parties

A tenancy that rolls over to periodic (month-to-month) has different notice requirements for termination — which may be less favourable to the landlord in a market where they want to secure a new fixed term.

## Ending the Tenancy

### Tenant-initiated ending
The tenant can end a tenancy by giving the required notice:
- At the end of a fixed term: typically 14–28 days' notice (varies by state and agreement)
- During a periodic tenancy: typically 21–28 days' notice

The tenant vacates, keys are returned, and the property manager conducts a final inspection.

### Landlord-initiated ending
The landlord can end a tenancy:
- At the end of a fixed term: by giving required notice (typically 30–90 days depending on state)
- During a periodic tenancy: by giving required notice AND (in most states) a valid reason (renovations, sale, owner-occupancy)
- For breach: by following the breach notice process (see rent arrears above — the same process applies to other breaches such as property damage, unauthorized subletting, or illegal activity on the premises)

### Final Bond Claim
At the end of the tenancy, the property manager conducts an outgoing inspection comparing the property's condition to the entry condition report.

**Bond can be claimed for:**
- Rent arrears
- Cleaning costs (if the property is not returned in the same clean condition as when let)
- Damage beyond fair wear and tear
- Removal of rubbish

**Bond cannot be claimed for:**
- Fair wear and tear
- Pre-existing damage (documented at entry)
- Items not documented in the entry condition report

If the landlord and tenant cannot agree on the bond claim, the dispute is resolved through the state tenancy authority (bond lodgement authority) or the tenancy tribunal.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*Your tenant, Sophie, has been in the property for 2 years on a periodic tenancy. She has always paid rent on time. She calls to say the oven has stopped working. She has small children and needs it for cooking meals. The landlord, Mr Tan, is travelling overseas and is not responding to messages. The estimated repair cost is $350. The disbursement authority in the management agreement allows you to spend up to $500 without prior approval. What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

A non-functioning oven for a tenant with small children is a significant maintenance issue that materially affects the habitability and usability of the property. I would treat this as an urgent repair requiring prompt action.

**Immediate steps:**

1. **Attempt to contact Mr Tan:** Send an email and leave a voicemail clearly documenting the issue, the cost estimate, and my intention to arrange repair if I don't hear back within [reasonable timeframe — given urgency, probably 4–6 hours].

2. **Arrange the repair:** The disbursement authority allows me to spend up to $500 without prior approval. The estimated cost is $350 — within my authority. I would arrange a licensed repairer to attend as soon as possible.

3. **Document everything:** Log the maintenance request (time and date), my attempts to contact Mr Tan, the trade arranged, the quote received, and the date the repair was completed.

4. **Advise Sophie:** Confirm the repair has been arranged and the expected timeframe. Keep her informed.

5. **Advise Mr Tan:** Once the repair is completed, ensure Mr Tan receives a full report — the nature of the fault, the repair arranged, the cost, and the receipt. This will appear on his next monthly statement as a disbursement.

**Why I can act without prior approval:**
The management agreement specifically grants disbursement authority for repairs up to $500 without requiring the landlord to be contacted first. This authority exists precisely for situations like this — where the landlord is unavailable and a repair needs to be arranged promptly. The residential tenancies legislation also supports emergency repair action in cases affecting essential services.

If the estimated cost had exceeded $500, I would have made more urgent attempts to contact Mr Tan before proceeding, and would have documented my decision if I had to act without approval due to urgency.

**Examiner Annotation:** Excellent. The student correctly identifies the repair as urgent, applies the disbursement authority correctly, documents their attempts to contact the landlord, arranges the repair, communicates with the tenant, and follows up. The student also correctly addresses the question of what they would do if the cost exceeded the disbursement authority. Strong Competent.
