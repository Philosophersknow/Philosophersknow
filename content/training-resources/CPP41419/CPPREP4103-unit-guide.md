## What This Unit Is About

The vendor relationship is the foundation of a sales agent's business. Vendors choose their agent based on trust — trust that the agent knows the market, will represent their interests honestly, and will communicate throughout the campaign. Building and maintaining that trust requires understanding the vendor's goals, setting realistic expectations from the start, and communicating consistently throughout the sale.

CPPREP4103 focuses on establishing and managing vendor relationships — from the initial listing consultation to exchange of contracts. It connects closely to CPPREP4102 (marketing) and CPPREP4105 (selling).

---

## The Vendor's Decision to Sell — Understanding Their Needs

Vendors are not all the same. Before recommending a marketing strategy, you need to understand:

- **Why are they selling?** (Downsizing, upsizing, financial pressure, relationship change, estate, investment review) — this affects urgency, flexibility on price, and the emotional context of the sale
- **What is their timeline?** When do they need to be settled? This affects method of sale and marketing duration.
- **What is their price expectation?** Is it realistic? If not, how do you manage this conversation?
- **What are their concerns?** Privacy, disruption to tenants, settlement timing, the buyers they want (or don't want)
- **Have they sold before?** First-time vendors need more explanation and support through the process.

A vendor who feels understood is more likely to list with you — and more likely to stay listed with you when the campaign faces challenges.

## The Listing Consultation

The listing consultation is your opportunity to win the listing and establish the foundation of the relationship.

**What it covers:**
1. **Your market knowledge** — demonstrate that you know the local area, the recent comparable sales, and the buyer demand
2. **Your marketing strategy** — explain your recommended approach and why
3. **Your appraisal** — present your evidence-based price estimate honestly (see CPPREP4101)
4. **Your fees** — explain your commission and any marketing costs clearly
5. **The agency agreement** — explain the key terms before the vendor signs
6. **What the vendor can expect** — inspection schedule, communication cadence, reporting

**Common mistake:** Telling the vendor what they want to hear to win the listing. A relationship built on an inflated appraisal or unrealistic promises begins badly and ends badly.

## The Agency Agreement

A vendor cannot be represented without a signed agency agreement. The agency agreement is a legal contract — explain it clearly before asking for a signature.

**Key terms to explain:**
- **Exclusivity:** Sole, exclusive, or open agency. Sole agency means only your agency can sell (but the vendor can sell themselves without paying commission). Exclusive agency means only your agency can sell — including the vendor privately. Open agency means multiple agents can market and only the one who produces the buyer receives commission.
- **Commission:** The fee payable on successful sale, expressed as a percentage of the sale price (or occasionally as a fixed fee). Commission rates are negotiable and vary by state and market. [VERIFY: State-specific rules around commission advertising and disclosure]
- **Term:** How long the agreement runs. After expiry, the agency relationship ends.
- **Marketing authority:** What the vendor authorises the agent to do — including incurring marketing costs
- **Auction authority (if applicable):** If the property is to go to auction, the vendor must authorise the auctioneer and set a reserve price

## Communicating With Vendors During the Campaign

**What vendors expect:**
- Regular contact (minimum once per week in most agencies; more during active campaigns)
- Feedback from every inspection or enquiry
- Honest reporting — not just good news
- To be consulted before any significant decision

**What communication should include:**
- Number of inspections and enquiries since last update
- Buyer feedback (aggregate — not identifying individual buyers in a way that damages negotiation position)
- Your assessment of how the campaign is performing
- Any recommended adjustments
- Next steps

**The difficult conversation: price review**

If the campaign is not performing as expected, you need to raise a price review. This is one of the most challenging conversations in real estate, but it is your professional obligation.

Structure for a price review conversation:
1. Present the data — what has happened (inspections, enquiries, comparable sales)
2. Explain your assessment — "Based on this feedback and the recent comparable sales, I believe the property is positioned slightly above the range buyers are prepared to pay for this type of property"
3. Make a recommendation — what price adjustment you believe is warranted and why
4. Acknowledge the vendor's position — this is not easy news to receive
5. Document the conversation and the vendor's response — regardless of whether they agree to adjust

## Receiving and Presenting Offers

When a buyer submits an offer, the agent:
1. Must present it to the vendor promptly (typically required by legislation)
2. Presents it with context: the buyer's financial position, any conditions, the agent's assessment of the offer relative to market
3. Does NOT make the decision — the vendor decides whether to accept, reject, or counter
4. Must not misrepresent an offer to either party

**Multiple offer situations:**
When more than one offer is received, the agent must:
- Inform all buyers that there are multiple offers (best practice and required in many states)
- Invite all buyers to submit their best and final offer
- Present all offers to the vendor fairly
- Not disclose one buyer's offer figure to another buyer (this could constitute a breach of the agent's duty)

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You receive two offers on a property you have listed at $875,000. Offer A is $840,000 unconditional. Offer B is $855,000 subject to finance and building and pest inspection, with a 14-day settlement. The vendor asks you which offer is better and tells you to "just accept whichever one gets us the most money."*

*What do you advise the vendor, and how do you handle this situation?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This scenario requires me to give the vendor honest, complete advice — not simply tell them to take the higher offer without explaining what "higher" actually means in context.

**The two offers compared:**

Offer A ($840,000 unconditional) is $15,000 less in dollar terms but is not subject to any conditions. If the vendor accepts, the contract is immediately binding and the sale will proceed unless the vendor defaults. There is no risk of the buyer pulling out due to a failed finance approval or an unsatisfactory building and pest inspection.

Offer B ($855,000 subject to finance and building/pest) is $15,000 more but carries conditions that can allow the buyer to withdraw without penalty within the condition periods. If the buyer's finance is not approved, or if the building and pest inspection reveals significant defects, the buyer can exit the contract and the vendor is back on the market.

**The vendor's situation matters:**

If the vendor needs certainty — for example, they have already purchased elsewhere and need this sale to proceed — an unconditional offer at a lower price may actually deliver more value by eliminating the risk of the sale collapsing. If the vendor has flexibility on timing and the conditions are short (the 14-day reference here seems to refer to settlement, not the condition period — I would clarify this), Offer B's premium may be worth the conditional period.

**What I tell the vendor:**

I would explain both offers in plain terms, highlight the conditions and their implications, and ask about their circumstances. My recommendation would depend on the vendor's situation — but the recommendation I give will be what I genuinely believe is in their best interests, not what generates my commission fastest.

I cannot accept either offer on the vendor's behalf — they must decide. I document their decision and their reasoning.

**Examiner Annotation:** Excellent. The student correctly analyses both offers (including the risk implications of the conditional offer), connects the recommendation to the vendor's individual circumstances, correctly identifies the agent's role as advisory not decision-making, and documents the interaction. This is sophisticated vendor relationship management. Strong Competent.
