## What This Unit Is About

Ethics in real estate is not a soft topic sitting alongside the "real" content of sales and property management. It is the framework that determines whether real estate professionals can be trusted — and in an industry where people make the largest financial decision of their lives, trust is everything.

CPPREP4002 asks you to understand the ethical obligations of a licensed real estate agent — what they are, where they come from, what happens when they are breached, and how to navigate situations where ethical obligations conflict with commercial pressure.

---

## Why Ethics Matters in Real Estate

Consider what is at stake in a typical residential property transaction:
- A family is selling the home they have lived in for 30 years to fund their retirement
- A first home buyer is borrowing the maximum they can to enter the market
- The buyer's and vendor's interests are directly opposed on price
- The agent is paid by the vendor but has obligations to deal honestly with the buyer
- Commission is typically percentage-based — the agent earns more on a higher sale price

This creates structural pressure that makes ethical conduct both more important and more challenging in real estate than in many other industries. The assessment in CPPREP4002 tests whether you understand these tensions — and how professional ethics and legislation address them.

## Fiduciary Duty — The Core of Real Estate Ethics

A fiduciary duty is the highest duty known in law. When you act as an agent for a client, you owe them a fiduciary duty — a duty of complete loyalty, honesty, and good faith that places their interests above your own.

**The four key obligations of fiduciary duty in real estate:**

| Obligation | What It Means | Example of Breach |
|---|---|---|
| **Loyalty** | Act in your client's best interests, not your own | Recommending a buyer who will generate you a referral fee over a better-qualified buyer |
| **Disclosure** | Tell your client everything that is material to their decision | Failing to tell a vendor that you have a financial interest in the buyer's company |
| **Confidentiality** | Protect your client's confidential information | Telling a buyer that the vendor is under financial pressure and must sell quickly |
| **Obedience** | Follow your client's lawful instructions | Accepting an offer below the vendor's stated minimum without authority |

## Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest exists when your personal interests, or the interests of a third party, could affect your ability to act loyally for your client.

**In real estate, conflicts of interest include:**
- Buying a property you are listing (purchasing your own listing)
- Referring the vendor to a conveyancer who pays you a referral fee without disclosure
- Acting for both vendor and buyer in the same transaction (dual agency) without informed consent
- Having a financial interest in the buyer or tenant
- Employing a family member in a role that benefits from a transaction you are managing

**Managing conflicts:**
1. Identify the conflict as early as possible
2. Disclose the conflict to your client
3. In many cases, obtain the client's informed written consent to proceed
4. In serious cases, withdraw from acting — you cannot always manage a conflict by disclosure alone

## Key Ethical Concepts

### Material facts and disclosure
You are legally and ethically required to disclose material facts — information that would affect a reasonable person's decision to buy, sell, or lease. Failing to disclose a material fact can:
- Expose you and the agency to legal liability
- Result in the transaction being rescinded
- Constitute fraud in serious cases

**Material facts include:**
- Known structural defects
- Past flooding or bushfire events affecting the property
- Pending legal proceedings related to the property
- That a death occurred on the property (in most states)
- Environmental contamination

### Unconscionable conduct
Unconscionable conduct means taking unfair advantage of someone in a weaker position. Under the Australian Consumer Law, it is prohibited. Examples in real estate:
- Using high-pressure tactics on elderly or vulnerable vendors
- Providing misleading information about the market to push a vendor to accept a low offer
- Exploiting a buyer's lack of market knowledge to discourage them from negotiating

### Misleading and deceptive conduct
Under the Australian Consumer Law, you must not engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive. This includes:
- Overstating the likely sale price to win a listing (price baiting)
- Quoting a price range that does not reflect genuine market expectations
- Omitting information in a way that creates a false impression

## Common Mistakes

**1. Treating ethics as the same as law**

Many students write "this is unethical because it's illegal." But ethics extends beyond legality. Something can be legal but still unethical — and your professional obligations may require you to decline to do it. Equally, something that appears to be legal may still constitute misleading conduct under consumer protection legislation.

---

**2. Missing the dual agency issue**

When you act for both the vendor and the buyer in the same transaction, you face an irreconcilable conflict of interest — your fiduciary duty to the vendor requires you to get the highest price; your duty to the buyer (if you are also their agent) requires you to get the lowest price. Assessors frequently test this scenario. The correct answer is: you must disclose the conflict to both parties and obtain informed consent, and in many cases you should decline to act for both.

---

**3. Failing to identify price baiting as an ethical issue**

Quoting an unrealistically low price range to attract buyers to a property — knowing you will need to tell them the vendor expects more — is misleading conduct and an ethical breach. Students often describe this as "standard practice" without recognising its ethical and legal implications. It is not standard practice; it is prohibited under the Australian Consumer Law.

## Worked Example — Ethics Scenario

**Prompt:**

*You are a property manager. Your landlord client, Mrs Nguyen, has asked you not to lease her property to anyone who appears to be from a particular ethnic background. She says she has had bad experiences with tenants from that background in the past. What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I cannot follow Mrs Nguyen's instruction. What she is asking me to do constitutes racial discrimination, which is unlawful under the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth) and under state anti-discrimination legislation. It is also unethical — it violates basic principles of fairness and equal treatment.

My first step is to explain this clearly to Mrs Nguyen, professionally and without judgment. I would tell her that I have a legal obligation to assess prospective tenants on objective criteria — ability to pay rent, rental history, references — and that I cannot consider ethnicity as a criterion. I would explain that if I were to follow her instruction, I would expose both of us to legal liability under the Racial Discrimination Act, which provides for complaints to the Australian Human Rights Commission and potential civil action.

I would also note that her own understanding of her "bad experiences" may be subject to confirmation bias — attributing problems to ethnicity rather than to other factors such as specific circumstances or poor reference checking.

If Mrs Nguyen insists on her instruction, I must decline to act for her. I cannot follow a lawful instruction that would require me to breach the law or act unethically. My fiduciary duty to my client does not include an obligation to facilitate illegal discrimination.

I would document the conversation — what Mrs Nguyen requested, what I said in response, and my decision to decline — and I would consult my principal or the agency's legal adviser about how to formally terminate the management agreement if Mrs Nguyen does not withdraw the instruction.

**Examiner Annotation:** Excellent. The response identifies the discrimination clearly, cites the correct legislation, explains the legal exposure to both parties, offers a professional approach that gives the client an opportunity to reconsider, is clear about the limit of the agent's duty to follow instructions (lawful instructions only), and includes appropriate documentation and escalation. The mention of confirmation bias shows sophisticated understanding of why the instruction arose. Strong Competent.

---

**Insufficient Response:**

I would try to explain to Mrs Nguyen that discrimination is wrong and ask her to reconsider. If she still insisted I would probably just try to find tenants who she would be happy with without making it obvious.

**Examiner Annotation:** NYC. This response suggests the student would attempt to implement a discriminatory instruction covertly — which would expose them to greater legal risk than refusing directly. There is no reference to relevant legislation. The student does not demonstrate understanding of the fiduciary duty to follow *lawful* instructions only, or of their own legal obligation not to discriminate. Resubmit with specific legislative reference and a clear statement of refusal.
