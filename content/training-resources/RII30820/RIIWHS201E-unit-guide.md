## What This Unit Is About

Civil construction combines almost every serious workplace hazard in one environment: heavy mobile plant, excavations, live traffic, buried services, confined spaces, silica dust, noise, and vibration — often outdoors, often at night, and often to a schedule under commercial pressure.

RIIWHS201E is the foundation WHS unit for the civil sector. It asks you to demonstrate that you can work safely within the site's WHS system: understanding the documents, following the procedures, recognising the hazards specific to civil work, and knowing what to do when something is not right.

## The Legal Framework

### The Model WHS Act

**PCBU duties (Person Conducting a Business or Undertaking):**
The employer must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers and others. This includes providing safe plant, safe systems of work, information, training, supervision, and consultation.

**Worker duties:**
- Take reasonable care for your own health and safety
- Take reasonable care that your acts or omissions do not adversely affect others
- Comply, so far as reasonably able, with reasonable instructions
- Cooperate with reasonable WHS policies and procedures

**Worker rights:**
- To be consulted about matters affecting your health and safety
- To be represented by a Health and Safety Representative (HSR)
- To cease unsafe work where there is a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk
- Protection from discriminatory conduct for raising WHS matters

### Principal Contractor Duties

On construction projects above a value threshold (commonly $250,000 — **[VERIFY: threshold and duties vary by jurisdiction]**), the principal contractor has additional duties, including preparing a **WHS Management Plan** for the site and ensuring high-risk construction work has a SWMS before it starts.

## Site WHS Documentation — What You Must Know

Civil sites run on documentation. You need to know what each document is and what it means for you.

| Document | What It Is | Your Obligation |
|---|---|---|
| **WHS Management Plan** | The overarching site safety plan prepared by the principal contractor | Understand the site rules that apply to you |
| **SWMS (Safe Work Method Statement)** | Required for high-risk construction work; identifies hazards and controls for a specific task | Read it, sign it, follow it, stop if conditions change |
| **JSA / JSEA (Job Safety Analysis)** | Task-level hazard analysis, often for lower-risk or non-HRCW tasks | Participate in preparing it; follow it |
| **Take 5 / Step Back 5** | A short personal pre-task risk assessment | Complete before starting a task, particularly if anything has changed |
| **SDS (Safety Data Sheet)** | Hazard, handling, PPE, and first aid information for a chemical | Read before using any chemical; know where the SDS register is |
| **Permit to Work** | Formal authorisation for specific high-risk activities (confined space entry, hot work, excavation near services, isolation) | Do not commence permit work without a valid, current permit |
| **Traffic Management Plan (TMP)** | How traffic (public and site) is controlled around the work | Understand the layout and your position within it |
| **Site induction record** | Evidence you have been inducted to this specific site | Cannot work on site without it |
| **Prestart / toolbox record** | Daily briefing on the day's work and hazards | Attend, contribute, sign |

**A SWMS you have signed without reading is worse than useless.** If an incident occurs, the signature is evidence you were informed of the controls. Read it.

## The Risk Management Process

The four steps, applied to every task:

### 1. Identify the Hazard
What could cause harm? Look at the task, the plant, the environment, the people, and what has changed since yesterday.

### 2. Assess the Risk
How likely is harm, and how severe would it be? Most sites use a **risk matrix** combining likelihood and consequence to produce a risk rating.

| | Insignificant | Minor | Moderate | Major | Catastrophic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Almost certain** | Medium | High | High | Extreme | Extreme |
| **Likely** | Medium | Medium | High | High | Extreme |
| **Possible** | Low | Medium | High | High | High |
| **Unlikely** | Low | Low | Medium | Medium | High |
| **Rare** | Low | Low | Medium | Medium | High |

**[VERIFY: Matrix structure and rating labels vary between organisations. Use your site's matrix.]**

### 3. Control the Risk
Apply the hierarchy of controls (below), highest level reasonably practicable.

### 4. Review the Controls
Are they working? Have conditions changed? Has a new hazard been introduced by the control itself?

## The Hierarchy of Controls — Civil Construction Examples

| Level | Control | Civil Construction Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Elimination** | Remove the hazard entirely — use trenchless boring instead of open-cut excavation, removing the trench collapse and entry hazard altogether |
| 2 | **Substitution** | Replace with something less hazardous — use a lower-silica engineered product; use a vacuum excavation truck instead of hand digging near services |
| 3 | **Isolation** | Separate people from the hazard — hard barriers between the work zone and live traffic; exclusion zones around operating plant; trench shields |
| 4 | **Engineering** | Physical modification — trench shoring and battering; reversing cameras and proximity detection on plant; on-tool dust extraction; wet cutting |
| 5 | **Administrative** | Procedures, training, signage — spotters for plant movements; permit systems; rotation to limit vibration exposure; TMP |
| 6 | **PPE** | Last line — hard hat, hi-vis, safety boots, eye and hearing protection, P2 respirator, gloves |

**A common assessment failure is jumping straight to PPE.** A P2 mask does not make silica dust safe — it is the last defence after wet cutting, extraction, and exclusion have been applied. State the higher controls first, every time.

## The Critical Civil Construction Hazards

### 1. Underground Services — Dial Before You Dig

**Before any excavation, penetration, or ground disturbance**, service location information must be obtained. In Australia this is done through the **Before You Dig Australia** service (formerly Dial Before You Dig — 1100).

**The process:**
1. **Enquire** — lodge a request; receive plans from asset owners
2. **Locate** — the plans show approximate positions only. Services must be physically located on site, typically by a qualified locator using electromagnetic locating equipment and/or vacuum excavation (potholing/daylighting)
3. **Mark** — mark the located services on the ground using the standard colour code
4. **Excavate carefully** — use non-destructive methods (vacuum excavation, hand digging) within the defined exclusion distance of a located service

**Standard service colour code:**

| Colour | Service |
|---|---|
| Red | Electricity |
| Yellow | Gas / oil / other hazardous liquids |
| Blue | Water |
| Cream / beige | Sewer / drainage |
| White | Communications / telecommunications |
| Green | Reclaimed / recycled water |
| Purple | Irrigation |

**[VERIFY: Colour code against AS 5488 (Subsurface Utility Information) and your state's specific requirements.]**

**Striking a live service is potentially fatal.** Contact with a high-voltage cable can kill instantly; a struck gas main can result in explosion and fire. Plans alone are never sufficient authority to dig — they show approximate location and are frequently inaccurate.

### 2. Excavation and Trenching

Trench collapse kills. A cubic metre of soil weighs roughly 1.5–2 tonnes — a partial collapse burying a worker to the waist can cause fatal crush injury without covering them completely.

**Controls (in order of preference):**
- **Benching or battering** — cutting the excavation walls back to a safe angle so they cannot collapse
- **Shoring** — installing supports (hydraulic shores, trench sheets) against the walls
- **Shielding** — a trench shield/box that protects workers within it even if the walls collapse
- **Exclusion** — no entry; do the work from outside the excavation

**Additional excavation requirements:**
- Spoil placed at least 1 metre back from the edge (spoil weight surcharges the wall and causes collapse)
- Safe access and egress — ladder within a defined distance of any worker in the trench
- Edge protection where there is a fall risk
- Consideration of ground water, previous excavation, adjacent traffic loading, and vibration from plant
- Excavations over a specified depth (commonly 1.5m) generally require engineered support — **[VERIFY: depth trigger in your jurisdiction]**

### 3. Mobile Plant and Vehicle Interaction

Being struck by mobile plant is a leading cause of civil construction fatalities.

**Key controls:**
- **Exclusion zones** around operating plant — do not enter the operating radius
- **Positive communication** — make eye contact with the operator and receive acknowledgement before approaching. Never assume you have been seen.
- **Spotters** for reversing and confined movements
- **Reversing alarms, cameras, and proximity detection**
- **Segregation** — physically separate pedestrian routes from plant routes wherever possible
- **Hi-vis** — necessary but not sufficient; hi-vis does not make you visible in a blind spot

**Blind spots:** Large plant has substantial blind spots, particularly immediately behind and to the right of the operator. Standing in a blind spot while wearing hi-vis provides no protection.

### 4. Traffic Management

Working on or beside live roads exposes workers to vehicles travelling at speed under the control of drivers who are not part of the site.

- Work must be conducted under a **Traffic Management Plan** prepared by a qualified person
- Traffic control devices must comply with **AS 1742.3** and the relevant road authority requirements
- **Traffic controllers** must hold current accreditation
- Understand your position in the TMP: where the taper is, where the buffer zone is, where the work area boundary is
- Never step outside the delineated work area into the live traffic lane
- Be aware of the risk of vehicle intrusion — the buffer zone exists because intrusions happen

### 5. Silica Dust

Cutting, grinding, drilling, or breaking concrete, brick, stone, and some engineered products releases **respirable crystalline silica (RCS)**. Inhalation causes silicosis — an irreversible, progressive, and potentially fatal lung disease — as well as lung cancer and other conditions.

**Controls in order:**
1. **Eliminate/substitute** — order materials pre-cut to size; use lower-silica products
2. **Engineering** — wet cutting (water suppression at the blade); on-tool dust extraction (H-class vacuum); enclosed cabs with filtered positive-pressure air
3. **Administrative** — exclusion zones downwind; limit exposure time; no dry sweeping (use wet methods or vacuum)
4. **PPE** — **P2 minimum** respirator, correctly fitted (fit testing required; facial hair compromises seal)

**Never dry cut concrete without engineering controls.** Workplace exposure standards for RCS have been significantly tightened in Australia. **[VERIFY: current workplace exposure standard for RCS — it has been reduced in recent years and further changes are under consideration.]**

### 6. Confined Spaces

Civil work frequently involves confined spaces — pits, manholes, culverts, tanks, shafts, and trenches in some circumstances.

A **confined space** is an enclosed or partially enclosed space that is not designed for continuous human occupancy, and that has a risk of oxygen deficiency or enrichment, harmful atmospheric contaminant, or engulfment.

**Requirements:**
- **Permit to work** — confined space entry requires a formal permit
- **Atmospheric testing** — before entry and continuously monitored during; oxygen, flammable gases, and toxic gases
- **Standby person** — remains outside, maintains communication, and initiates rescue (**does not enter**)
- **Rescue plan** — established before entry, not improvised during
- **Training** — confined space entry requires specific competency (RIIWHS202E)

**A high proportion of confined space fatalities are would-be rescuers** who entered without breathing apparatus to help a collapsed colleague. Never enter to attempt a rescue without the correct equipment and training.

### 7. Noise and Vibration

**Noise:** Plant, compaction equipment, concrete saws, and rock breakers routinely exceed 85 dB(A). Hearing loss is permanent and cumulative. Hearing protection is required, and engineering controls (quieter plant, barriers, distance) should be applied first.

**Vibration:**
- **Hand-arm vibration (HAV)** — from jackhammers, compactors, saws. Causes hand-arm vibration syndrome (HAVS), including permanent nerve and circulatory damage
- **Whole-body vibration (WBV)** — from plant operation, particularly on rough ground. Associated with chronic lower back injury
- Controls: low-vibration tools, seat suspension, ground condition maintenance, job rotation to limit exposure duration

## Incident Reporting

### What to Report
- All injuries, including those treated with first aid only
- **Near misses** — an event that could have caused injury but did not. Near misses are free lessons; a site with no reported near misses is a site with under-reporting, not a safe site.
- Property and plant damage
- Hazards identified
- Environmental incidents (spills, discharge)

### Notifiable Incidents
Certain incidents must be reported to the WHS regulator **immediately**:
- Death of a person
- Serious injury or illness requiring immediate hospital treatment (including amputation, serious head or eye injury, degloving, spinal injury, serious burns, loss of bodily function)
- **Dangerous incidents** exposing any person to a serious risk, including:
  - Uncontrolled escape, spillage, or leakage of a substance
  - Uncontrolled implosion, explosion, or fire
  - Uncontrolled escape of gas or steam, or of a pressurised substance
  - **Electric shock**
  - Fall or release from a height of any plant, substance, or thing
  - **Collapse or partial collapse of a structure or excavation**
  - **Collapse or failure of an excavation or of any shoring supporting an excavation**
  - Damage to or malfunction of any major plant

**The incident site must not be disturbed** until the regulator authorises it, except to make it safe, assist an injured person, or preserve evidence.

**Striking an underground service is typically a notifiable dangerous incident** — it exposes people to serious risk of electrocution, explosion, or asphyxiation.

## Fitness for Work

Civil construction sites commonly have fitness for work policies covering:

- **Fatigue** — long shifts, night work, and travel to remote sites. Fatigue impairs judgment and reaction time comparably to alcohol intoxication. Fatigue management plans, shift limits, and turnaround requirements are safety controls.
- **Drugs and alcohol** — most civil sites have zero-tolerance policies and conduct random testing. This includes prescription medications that affect alertness — declare these to your supervisor.
- **Health conditions** — conditions affecting consciousness, mobility, or judgment must be declared where they create risk (particularly for plant operation).
- **Heat stress** — outdoor work in Australian conditions. Controls include hydration, shade, rotation, rescheduling to cooler hours, and monitoring.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are part of a crew installing a stormwater line in a suburban street. The trench is to be 1.8m deep. The Before You Dig plans have been obtained and show a gas main and a communications cable in the vicinity, but no services have been physically located or marked on the ground. The excavator operator is ready to start. Your supervisor says the plans show the gas is "well clear of our line" and tells the operator to start digging. There is no shoring on site — the plan is to batter the walls back. The street is open to traffic with witches hats along the work area.*

*Identify the hazards and non-conformances, state what you would do, and explain the legal and safety basis for your position.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

There are four serious issues here, and I would stop the work before the excavator breaks ground.

---

**Issue 1 — Services not located or marked (MOST SERIOUS)**

Before You Dig plans show **approximate** positions only. They are frequently inaccurate — services get relocated, as-built records are often wrong, and the plans carry explicit disclaimers to that effect. Plans are the *first* step, not authorisation to excavate.

The correct sequence is **Enquire → Locate → Mark → Excavate carefully**. Only the first step has been completed.

Before mechanical excavation begins:
- A qualified service locator must physically locate the gas main and communications cable using electromagnetic locating equipment
- The located services must be **marked on the ground** (yellow for gas, white for communications)
- Within the exclusion distance of the located gas main, excavation must be by **non-destructive means** — vacuum excavation or careful hand digging — not by excavator bucket

"The plans show the gas is well clear" is precisely the reasoning that precedes gas main strikes. A struck gas main in a suburban street risks explosion, fire, and fatality — to the crew, and to the residents in the surrounding houses.

**This alone is enough to stop the job.**

---

**Issue 2 — 1.8m excavation with no engineered support arrangements confirmed**

The plan is to batter the walls. Battering is a legitimate control — but only if:
- The **batter angle is appropriate to the actual ground conditions**, determined by a competent person, not assumed
- There is **physical room** in the street to achieve that angle. In a suburban road corridor with services, kerbs, and traffic lanes, there frequently is not enough width to batter a 1.8m trench safely
- **Spoil is set back at least 1 metre from the edge** — spoil placed at the edge surcharges the wall and is a common cause of collapse
- Ground conditions, groundwater, previous excavation (this street clearly has existing services, meaning previously disturbed backfill — which is much less stable than undisturbed ground), and **traffic vibration loading from the live lane** have all been assessed

At 1.8m, a collapse would bury a worker above the waist. That is a fatality risk, not an injury risk.

I would want to see the SWMS confirming the excavation support method and the competent person's assessment of ground conditions before anyone enters that trench. I would also want confirmation there is a safe means of access and egress.

---

**Issue 3 — Traffic management is inadequate**

Witches hats (traffic cones) alone are not a traffic management plan. Work on a live street requires:
- A **Traffic Management Plan** prepared by a qualified person
- Traffic control devices compliant with **AS 1742.3** and the road authority's requirements — appropriate signage, advance warning, correct taper lengths, delineation, and a **buffer zone** between traffic and the work area
- **Accredited traffic controllers** if the TMP requires them

Cones provide delineation but no protection and no advance warning to approaching drivers. A vehicle intrusion into an open 1.8m trench with workers in it is catastrophic.

---

**Issue 4 — The SWMS**

Excavation where there is a risk of a person being **buried or trapped** is high-risk construction work. A **SWMS is legally required** before this work starts, and it must address the actual controls — service location, excavation support, traffic management, access and egress.

If a compliant SWMS exists, it clearly is not being followed. If it does not exist, the work cannot lawfully commence.

---

**What I would do:**

**1. Stop the work immediately, before the bucket goes in the ground.**

I would say so directly and without hedging: *"Stop — we can't dig here yet. The services haven't been located or marked, we've only got the plans. If we're wrong about where that gas main is, we take out the street."*

Raising it *before* excavation starts is the entire point. Once the bucket is in the ground, the opportunity is gone.

**2. State what needs to happen before work can proceed:**
- Service locator on site to locate and mark the gas and comms
- Non-destructive excavation (potholing) to positively expose the gas main before machine excavation nearby
- Confirmation of the excavation support method and ground assessment for a 1.8m trench, with spoil setback
- A compliant TMP with appropriate devices, not just cones
- A SWMS covering all of the above, which the crew has read and signed

**3. If the supervisor directs the work to proceed anyway:**

I would **exercise my right to cease unsafe work** under the model WHS Act. I have a reasonable concern that proceeding would expose me and others to a serious risk — striking a gas main, trench collapse, or vehicle intrusion. This right is legally protected, and I cannot lawfully be penalised for exercising it.

I would notify the supervisor that I am ceasing the work and why, and I would remain available for other safe work. I would then escalate — to the site HSR, the principal contractor's site manager, or if necessary the WHS regulator. I would document the direction I was given, what I said, and the time.

**4. Documentation:**

I would record the hazard on a hazard report regardless of the outcome, so there is a written record that the non-conformances were identified and raised.

---

**The legal basis for my position:**

- **Worker duty (WHS Act):** I must take reasonable care for my own safety and not adversely affect others. Participating in an excavation with unlocated services and unverified support would breach that duty.
- **Right to cease unsafe work (WHS Act):** I have a reasonable concern of exposure to serious risk. This right is not conditional on my supervisor agreeing with me.
- **Protection from discriminatory conduct (WHS Act):** It is an offence for an employer to dismiss, demote, or otherwise disadvantage a worker for raising a WHS concern or ceasing unsafe work.
- **High-risk construction work:** Excavation with risk of burial requires a SWMS before commencement.
- **Underground services:** Striking a service is a notifiable dangerous incident and, where a duty holder has failed to take reasonably practicable steps to locate services, exposes the PCBU to prosecution.

**What I would not do:** Let it go because the supervisor is senior, because the schedule is tight, or because I am junior on the crew. Deference is not a control measure. The people at risk here include the crew, the plant operator, and the residents of the street.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding — this is the strongest possible response to this scenario. The student identifies all four non-conformances, correctly ranks service location as the most serious, and demonstrates genuine understanding of *why* plans alone are insufficient (approximate positions, disclaimers, inaccurate as-builts). The excavation analysis is sophisticated: the student identifies that previously disturbed backfill and traffic vibration are relevant to batter stability, and notes the practical constraint that a suburban corridor may not have room to batter safely — both are points that working supervisors miss. The traffic management critique correctly distinguishes delineation from protection. The response stops the work *before* excavation, states the specific remediation required, correctly invokes the right to cease unsafe work with its legal basis, escalates appropriately, and documents. The closing line — "deference is not a control measure" — captures exactly the professional judgment this unit is assessing. Strong Competent.
