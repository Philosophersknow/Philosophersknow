## What This Unit Is About

This is the capstone practical unit of the qualification. Where RIICWD201D covers the individual activities, RIICWD301E asks you to **plan, sequence and deliver a piece of civil work** — reading the documents, working out the order, coordinating with plant and other trades, and delivering to specification.

It is the difference between being able to do the tasks and being able to run a section of work.

## Reading the Documents

### The Drawing Set

| Drawing | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Locality and site plan** | Where the work is, boundaries, access, existing features |
| **General arrangement / layout plan** | Overall extent and configuration |
| **Longitudinal section** | Levels along the alignment — existing surface, design surface, invert levels, gradients |
| **Cross-sections** | The shape at each chainage — cut and fill, batters, layer thicknesses, widths |
| **Detail drawings** | Pits, headwalls, kerb profiles, junctions, structural details |
| **Services drawings** | Existing and proposed services |
| **Erosion and sediment control plan** | Required controls and locations |
| **Traffic management drawings / TGS** | Traffic control arrangement |

**Read the longitudinal section and cross-sections together.** The long section tells you the levels along the line; the cross-sections tell you the shape across it. Neither on its own tells you what to build.

### Chainage

Civil work is located by **chainage** — distance along the alignment from a defined zero point.

Written as `Ch 1240` or `1+240`, meaning 1240 metres along the alignment.

**Everything is referenced to chainage:** cross-sections, levels, service locations, test lots, measurement records, the site diary. **Get comfortable with it, because every conversation on a civil site uses it.**

### Reading Levels Off a Long Section

The long section typically shows, at each chainage:
- **Existing surface level**
- **Design surface level**
- **Invert level** for drainage
- **Cut or fill depth** — the difference

**Cut and fill is the difference between existing and design.** Design above existing means fill; design below existing means cut. A long section is the fastest way to understand the earthworks task on a job.

## Planning the Work

### Working Out the Sequence

Civil work has a natural order driven by three things: **what must physically precede what**, **what would be damaged by later work**, and **what would be trapped by later work**.

**A typical drainage and pavement sequence:**

1. Traffic management and site establishment
2. Erosion and sediment controls
3. Service location and marking
4. Clear and grub; strip topsoil (stockpile for reuse)
5. Bulk earthworks to subgrade — cut and fill
6. **Drainage** — trench, lay, backfill, pits
7. Subgrade preparation, trim and compact
8. **Hold point: subgrade inspection and release**
9. Subbase — place, compact, test
10. Base course — place, compact, test
11. Kerb and channel
12. Sealing or asphalt
13. Line marking, signs, furniture
14. Topsoil, landscaping, stabilisation
15. Reinstatement and clean up

**Why drainage comes before pavement:** you do not build a road and then trench through it. This seems obvious and is nonetheless one of the most common sequencing failures on small jobs, usually because the drainage design arrives late.

**Why topsoil is stripped and stockpiled:** it is a resource. Topsoil mixed into fill is wasted, and topsoil is needed at the end for landscaping and stabilisation.

**Why stabilisation is last but planned first:** an area that is finished and left bare generates sediment. Progressive stabilisation as areas are completed is far more effective than doing it all at the end.

### Identifying Dependencies

Before starting, work out:

- **What must be complete before I start?** Survey set-out, service location, permits, hold point releases
- **What am I holding up?** If another trade is waiting on my work, they need to know my actual timing, not my optimistic timing
- **What will damage my work if it happens after?** Newly compacted subgrade damaged by delivery trucks; fresh kerb damaged by plant
- **What will trap my work if it happens before?** Anything that needs to go under something else

### Resources

| Resource | Plan for |
|---|---|
| **Plant** | Right machine for the task, available when needed, with the right attachment |
| **Labour** | Numbers and competencies — including licences and tickets |
| **Materials** | Quantity (including bulking and compaction allowances), delivery timing, storage |
| **Survey** | Set-out before you need it, not when you need it |
| **Testing** | Booked. Laboratory turnaround affects the programme |
| **Permits** | Obtained before the work, and current |
| **Traffic control** | Accredited controllers arranged |

**Testing turnaround is the resource most often overlooked in planning.** A layer that needs a density result before the next layer goes on is a layer that stops the job if the test was not arranged.

## Executing the Work

### Set-Out

Work is built to survey control. Common methods:

| Method | Use |
|---|---|
| **Pegs and offsets** | Alignment and position; offset pegs placed clear of the work |
| **Profiles / batter boards** | Carry a string line at a known level over an excavation |
| **String line** | Alignment and level over a run — kerb, pipe, formwork |
| **Boning rods / travellers** | Transferring a level down from a string line to the work — traditional and still effective for drainage |
| **Laser level** | Level over distance |
| **Machine guidance / GPS** | Plant guided to design surface directly |

**Protect the set-out.** Pegs get destroyed by plant, and re-establishing survey control costs time and money. Offset pegs are placed clear of the work for exactly this reason.

**Check the set-out before you build to it.** A set-out error discovered after the work is built is expensive; discovered before, it is a phone call.

### Working to Line, Level and Grade

- **Check frequently, not at the end.** Every pipe, every few metres of kerb, every layer. An error caught after one pipe costs one pipe
- **Cross-check independently** where the consequence is high — a level checked twice by two methods before a drainage line is laid
- **Understand the tolerance** and work to the design, not the tolerance edge (see RIICWD302E)

### Coordinating With Plant

- **Communicate the task clearly** to the operator before the movement, not during
- **One person directing.** Multiple people signalling one machine is how conflicting instructions get followed
- **Positive acknowledgement** before approaching (see RIICWD201D)
- **Tell the operator what you are trying to achieve**, not only what to do. An operator who understands the objective will produce a better result and will flag a problem you have not seen

### When Something Goes Wrong

Something will differ from the documents on almost every job.

**The response is the same regardless of what it is:**

1. **Stop the affected work**
2. **Make it safe** — the condition may be a hazard as well as a problem
3. **Do not resolve a document conflict or a design question yourself**
4. **Record it** — photographs, measurements, survey, diary, time
5. **Report it immediately** to the supervisor
6. **Check the notice implications** — a changed condition may carry a contractual notice period (see RIICWM201D)
7. **Work elsewhere** while it is resolved

**"Stop and escalate" is not passivity.** It is the correct response to a problem that is outside your authority to decide, and it protects both the work and the commercial position.

## Environmental Obligations

Civil construction operates under environmental legislation with real penalties, and enforcement is active.

| Obligation | Practical requirement |
|---|---|
| **Sediment control** | Controls installed before disturbance, maintained, inspected after rain |
| **Concrete washout** | Contained. **Highly alkaline — never to a drain or waterway** |
| **Fuel and chemical** | Bunded storage, spill kit available, spills contained and reported |
| **Dust** | Water suppression, stabilisation, covered loads |
| **Noise and vibration** | Work within permitted hours; monitoring where required |
| **Waste** | Segregated and disposed of correctly; contaminated material tracked |
| **Vegetation** | Protect what is designated for retention — tree protection zones are enforceable |
| **Watercourses** | No unauthorised works, no discharge |
| **Heritage and cultural** | **Stop work immediately on discovery of artefacts or remains and notify.** Do not disturb further |

**The heritage obligation is absolute and it is the one that catches people out.** Discovery of Aboriginal cultural material or human remains requires work to stop and the relevant authority to be notified. Continuing to work through a discovery is a serious offence, and the obligation applies regardless of the programme.

**[VERIFY: environmental and heritage obligations are set by state legislation and project approvals. Confirm the requirements applying to your project, including who must be notified on a heritage discovery.]**

## Working With Others

### Other Trades and Utilities

- **Coordinate at the interfaces.** Most problems occur where one party's work meets another's
- **Raise clashes rather than working around them** — a clash between your work and another party's is a coordination or documentation problem for the supervisor, not something to improvise past
- **Leave the site workable for the next trade.** Debris, trip hazards and blocked access transfer your problem to them
- **Do not direct another employer's workers**, and do not take direction from them without your supervisor's knowledge

### The Public

Civil work happens where people live. See RIICOM201D.

- Maintain access to properties wherever practicable, and notify in advance when it cannot be
- Be civil; refer enquiries to the supervisor or community contact
- **Reinstate properly.** Reinstatement is what the public judges the whole job by

## Finishing the Work

### Before Calling It Complete

- **Survey as-constructed** — levels, alignment, invert levels, service locations
- **All testing complete and passing**, with records
- **All hold points released**
- **NCRs closed** or dispositioned
- **Records compiled** — ITP sign-offs, test results, measurement, diary, photographs
- **Reinstatement complete** and matching the surrounding surface
- **Site cleaned** — all material, plant, spoil and rubbish removed
- **Temporary works removed** — traffic control, fencing, sediment controls once the area is stabilised
- **Stabilisation complete** — no bare disturbed areas left generating sediment

**Sediment controls come out last**, after the ground is stabilised. Removing them when the work finishes but before the surface has established is a common and penalised error.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are given a section of subdivision road to deliver: 180m of new road, including 140m of 375mm stormwater with three pits, subgrade preparation, 150mm subbase, 150mm base course, kerb and channel both sides, and asphalt surfacing.*

*The drawings show the road in cut for the first 60m (up to 1.4m deep) and in fill for the remaining 120m (up to 0.9m). Services drawings show a water main crossing the alignment at Ch 95.*

*Set out your sequence, identify the hold points and dependencies, and state what you would confirm before starting.*

---

### Exemplary Response

**I would work the sequence backwards from what must not be disturbed, then forwards from what must physically come first — and I would resolve the water main and the earthworks balance before anyone turns a wheel.**

---

### Before starting — what I confirm

**1. The water main at Ch 95 — this is the first thing I deal with.**

The services drawing shows it crossing the alignment. What I need to know before excavating anywhere near it:

- **Has it been physically located and marked**, not just shown on a plan? Service drawings show approximate positions and are frequently wrong (see RIIWHS201E)
- **What depth is it, and does it clash with the design?** The stormwater invert at Ch 95 versus the water main level determines whether this is a simple crossing or a design problem. **If the stormwater needs to pass through the water main's position, that is a clash requiring resolution before I get there**
- **Has the asset owner been notified**, and are there approach conditions or a permit?
- **What is the exclusion distance** for mechanical excavation, and is vacuum excavation or hand digging required within it?

**I would want this resolved before the drainage reaches Ch 95, not when the excavator is sitting on top of it.** A clash discovered at that point stops the job with an open trench.

**2. Earthworks balance.**

60m in cut to 1.4m and 120m in fill to 0.9m. **Does the cut material suit reuse as fill, and does the volume balance?**

- If cut material is suitable and volumes roughly balance, cut can be hauled directly to fill — cheapest option, minimal cartage
- If cut is unsuitable, it goes off site and fill is imported. **That is a completely different cost and programme**
- Rough check: cut volume from cross-sections against fill volume, remembering that **compaction shrinkage means I need more bank volume than the compacted fill volume** (see RIICCM201D)

**This determines the plant, the cartage, and the programme**, so it is a planning question, not something to discover at day three.

**3. Survey set-out** established and protected, with offset pegs clear of the work.

**4. Testing arranged** — laboratory booked, and I know the turnaround. **The subgrade hold point cannot be released without a density result**, so a slow turnaround directly delays the base course.

**5. Traffic management** — approved TMP, permit, accredited controllers.

**6. Confirm the ITP** — where the hold points are, who releases them, and the notice required.

---

### The sequence

**Stage 1 — Establishment**
1. Traffic management installed per the TGS
2. **Erosion and sediment controls before any ground disturbance** — silt fence on the downslope side, stabilised site access, drain inlet protection, clean water diversion
3. Service location and marking — including physical location of the water main at Ch 95
4. Survey set-out with protected offset pegs
5. Site facilities, spill kit, muster point

**Stage 2 — Clearing and earthworks**
6. Clear and grub
7. **Strip topsoil and stockpile separately** for reuse in landscaping. Protect the stockpile with a bund or cover
8. **Bulk earthworks** — cut the first 60m, haul to fill for the remaining 120m if the material is suitable
9. **Fill placed in specified layers** with moisture control and compaction to specification. Test each layer

**Stage 3 — Drainage (before pavement)**
10. Stormwater trench, working **from the downstream end upstream**, laying pipe with sockets facing upstream
11. **Vacuum excavation or hand dig approaching Ch 95**; expose and protect the water main before mechanical excavation nearby
12. Bedding, lay, joint, **haunch and side fill**, overlay before plant tracks over
13. Construct the three pits
14. Backfill in layers, compacted and tested
15. **Test the line** — CCTV or air test as specified

**Stage 4 — Subgrade**
16. Trim subgrade to design level and shape
17. Moisture condition and compact
18. Test — density and level
19. ⛔ **HOLD POINT — subgrade inspection and release before any pavement material is placed**

**Stage 5 — Pavement**
20. Subbase 150mm — place, moisture condition, compact, test level and density
21. ⛔ **Likely hold point or witness point at subbase level** — confirm from the ITP
22. Base course 150mm — place, compact, test
23. ⛔ **Likely hold point before sealing**

**Stage 6 — Kerb and surfacing**
24. Kerb and channel both sides, to line and level
25. Allow kerb to cure before adjacent trafficking
26. Asphalt surfacing
27. Line marking, signs

**Stage 7 — Finishing**
28. Topsoil from the stockpile, landscaping, **progressive stabilisation**
29. As-constructed survey
30. Reinstatement and clean up
31. **Remove sediment controls only once the surface is stabilised**
32. Compile and submit records

---

### Dependencies and why the order matters

**Drainage before pavement.** Non-negotiable. Trenching through completed pavement destroys it and the reinstatement never matches.

**Topsoil stripped before earthworks.** Topsoil mixed into fill is both a wasted resource and unsuitable fill material.

**Fill tested layer by layer, not at the end.** A failed layer under three more layers means removing all of them.

**Subgrade hold point before any pavement.** Proceeding past it risks being directed to remove the pavement so the subgrade can be inspected — the worst commercial outcome (see RIICWD302E).

**Kerb after base, before asphalt.** The kerb provides the edge restraint and the level reference for the asphalt.

**Pipe overlay before plant crosses.** The specified cover exists to protect the pipe from construction loading.

**Sediment controls out last.** Removing them when the work finishes but before the surface has established generates sediment and is a penalised error.

---

### What I would flag to the supervisor on day one

**Three things, because each has a lead time:**

1. **The water main at Ch 95** — needs physical location and a clash check against the stormwater invert **before** the drainage reaches it. If there is a clash, resolving it takes days
2. **Earthworks balance and material suitability** — determines plant, cartage and programme. Needs the geotechnical assessment of the cut material
3. **Testing turnaround** — the subgrade hold point sits on the critical path, and a slow laboratory turnaround delays the entire pavement sequence

**All three are things that stop the job if they are discovered late and are straightforward if they are dealt with early.** That is the point of planning the sequence before starting rather than working it out as I go.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student plans from constraints rather than reciting a generic sequence, and the three pre-start confirmations are exactly the right ones. The water main analysis is the strongest element: recognising that the service drawing shows an approximate position requiring physical location, and — critically — identifying that the stormwater invert may clash with the main and that this must be resolved before the trench arrives rather than when the excavator is on top of it. The earthworks balance question, including the recognition that compaction shrinkage means more bank volume is needed than compacted fill volume, connects the planning to the calculation unit correctly. Identifying testing turnaround as sitting on the critical path through the subgrade hold point is a scheduling insight that demonstrates genuine understanding of how quality processes interact with programme. The sequence is complete and correctly ordered, with the hold point marked and the ITP correctly cited as the authority for the others rather than assumed. Every dependency stated is justified with a consequence. The closing identification of the three long-lead items, framed as things that stop the job if found late, is precisely what planning a section of work is for. Strong Competent.
