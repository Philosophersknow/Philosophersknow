## What This Unit Is About

Roadworks put workers on foot within metres of vehicles travelling at speed, driven by people who are not inducted, not trained, not supervised, and frequently not paying attention.

Traffic management is what keeps those two things apart. It is one of the few civil construction controls where the hazard is entirely outside the site's control — you cannot instruct a driver, you can only design an environment that guides them safely and gives yourself room when they fail to follow it.

RIIWHS302D asks you to read and implement a Traffic Management Plan, install and maintain traffic control devices correctly, and work safely within the arrangement.

## The Regulatory Framework

### Governing Documents

| Document | Role |
|---|---|
| **AS 1742.3** | Manual of Uniform Traffic Control Devices — Part 3: Traffic Control for Works on Roads. The primary technical standard |
| **State road authority manuals** | Each state road authority publishes its own supplement or manual which takes precedence in that jurisdiction |
| **Local council requirements** | Apply on council-controlled roads |
| **WHS Act and Regulations** | General duties; traffic is a workplace hazard |

**[VERIFY: AS 1742.3 and state road authority requirements are periodically revised, and several jurisdictions have moved to updated frameworks and terminology. Always work from your state road authority's current manual and the project's approved TMP — this guide is not a substitute for either.]**

### Permits and Approvals

Work on a public road generally requires a **road occupancy permit** or equivalent from the road authority or council. The TMP is usually submitted as part of that application and approved before work begins.

**Working without an approved permit and TMP is unlawful**, uninsured, and exposes the contractor to prosecution if anything goes wrong.

### Competency

| Role | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Traffic Controller** | Accredited traffic control card / ticket; state-specific |
| **Implement a TMP** | This unit, plus state-specific requirements |
| **Prepare/design a TMP** | Higher-level qualification and authorisation |

**Holding this unit does not authorise you to design a TMP.** It authorises you to implement one that a qualified person has prepared.

**[VERIFY: Traffic controller accreditation and TMP designer qualifications vary by state and are periodically changed. Confirm with your state road authority.]**

## The Traffic Management Plan

### What a TMP Contains

- **Site location and description** of the works
- **Road environment** — speed limit, lane configuration, traffic volume, sight distance
- **Hours of work** and any restrictions (peak hour, school times, night work)
- **Traffic guidance scheme (TGS)** — a diagram showing the layout of every sign, device and delineator, with dimensions
- **Sign and device schedule** — what is required and how many
- **Speed limit** arrangements, including any reduced speed zones
- **Pedestrian and cyclist provisions**
- **Property and business access** arrangements
- **Public transport** provisions
- **Emergency vehicle access**
- **Contingency** — incident response, what to do if the arrangement fails
- **Monitoring and inspection** requirements
- **Contact details** and responsible person

**The TGS is the working document on site.** It is the diagram you implement and check against.

### Your Obligation

**Implement the TMP exactly as approved.** Do not vary distances, omit signs, or improvise a different layout because the approved one is inconvenient.

**If conditions differ from what the TMP assumes** — a road closure elsewhere has changed traffic volumes, a business access is not shown, sight distance is worse than assumed — **stop and escalate**. The TMP must be varied by a qualified person, not adjusted on site.

## Anatomy of a Worksite Layout

A standard traffic management arrangement has defined zones. Understanding what each is for is the core knowledge of this unit.

| Zone | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Advance warning area** | Signs warning approaching drivers of the works ahead, in sequence, at specified spacings |
| **Transition (taper) area** | Where traffic is moved laterally out of the closed lane |
| **Buffer / safety zone** | **Empty space between the traffic and the work area.** Contains no workers, plant or materials |
| **Work area** | Where the work is performed |
| **Termination area** | Where traffic returns to normal flow |

### The Buffer Zone — The Most Important Concept

**The buffer zone is deliberately empty.**

Its purpose is to absorb an errant vehicle. Drivers misjudge, are distracted, are fatigued, are impaired, or fail to see the taper. When a vehicle intrudes, the buffer is the distance that gives it space to stop or the workers time to move before it reaches them.

**Storing materials, parking vehicles, or working in the buffer zone defeats it entirely.** This is a very common site failure — the buffer looks like unused space, and unused space on a constrained road job is tempting.

**Longitudinal buffer** separates traffic from the work area along the road. **Lateral buffer** separates traffic from the work area sideways.

### The Taper

The taper guides traffic out of the closed lane. **Taper length is determined by the approach speed** — the faster the traffic, the longer the taper must be, because drivers need more distance and a gentler angle to change lanes safely.

**A taper that is too short** presents drivers with an abrupt lane change, causing late braking, sudden swerving, and vehicles clipping the delineators — or the work area.

Taper lengths, device spacings and advance sign distances are all **specified in the TGS and derived from the standard.** They are not estimated on site.

## Traffic Control Devices

### Signs

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Regulatory** | Impose a legal requirement — speed limits, stop/give way, no entry |
| **Warning** | Advise of a hazard — roadworks ahead, workers on road, loose stones, uneven surface |
| **Guidance** | Direct traffic — detour, keep left, lane status |

**Sign requirements:**
- **Correct sign for the situation** — signs are prescribed, not discretionary
- **Clean, undamaged and legible.** A faded or dirty sign is not compliant
- **Correctly mounted** — at the specified height, facing traffic squarely, stable against wind
- **Correctly positioned** at the spacing shown in the TGS
- **Removed or covered when not applicable.** A "ROADWORKS AHEAD" sign left up on a Sunday when nobody is working trains drivers to ignore it — and it can be a legal issue where a reduced speed limit is displayed with no works in progress

**Reduced speed limit signs are regulatory and legally enforceable.** They must only be displayed while the conditions warranting them exist.

### Delineation Devices

| Device | Use |
|---|---|
| **Cones** | Short-term delineation; lightweight, easily displaced |
| **Bollards** | Higher visibility, more stable than cones |
| **Barrier boards** | Longer-term delineation |
| **Water-filled or concrete barriers** | **Physical protection** — the only devices that actually stop a vehicle |
| **Flashing arrow boards** | Lane closure indication, high visibility |
| **Variable message signs (VMS)** | Advance information |

**The critical distinction:**

**Cones, bollards and barrier boards delineate. They do not protect.** A vehicle passes through them without slowing meaningfully.

**Only rigid or water-filled barriers provide physical protection.** Where the risk assessment identifies a significant intrusion risk — high speed, high volume, long duration, poor sight distance, night work — physical barriers should be specified, not cones.

**Treating cones as protection is a fundamental and dangerous misunderstanding.**

### Night and Low Visibility

- Devices must be **retroreflective** and in good condition — reflectivity degrades with age, dirt and damage
- Additional lighting for the work area, positioned so it does not dazzle approaching drivers
- **Higher class hi-vis clothing** is required at night than in daylight
- Increased advance warning distances
- Night work has substantially higher risk — fatigue affects both workers and drivers, and impaired drivers are more prevalent

## Traffic Controllers

Where the TMP requires manual traffic control, it is performed by an **accredited traffic controller**.

**Positioning:**
- Stand where you are **clearly visible** to approaching traffic with adequate sight distance
- Stand **out of the traffic lane** and clear of the path of vehicles
- Have an **escape route** identified — somewhere to move if a vehicle does not stop. This is not optional; it is the personal control of last resort
- Never stand between the traffic and a fixed object with no escape

**Equipment:** stop/slow bat, high-visibility clothing to the required class, and radio communication where controllers are working as a pair out of line of sight.

**Communication between paired controllers is essential** — two controllers releasing traffic simultaneously from both ends of a single-lane arrangement causes a head-on conflict.

**Traffic controllers must remain alert and focused.** No phone use, no distraction. The role looks passive and is not — a controller is continuously reading approaching traffic for vehicles that are not slowing.

## Pedestrians and Vulnerable Road Users

**Pedestrians are frequently the weakest element of a traffic management arrangement.**

- **Provide a safe alternative route.** Closing a footpath and leaving pedestrians to walk in the traffic lane is not acceptable
- The alternative route must be **continuous, delineated, and accessible** — including for people using wheelchairs, mobility aids, prams, and people who are blind or have low vision
- **Tactile and detectable barriers** matter — a person who is blind navigating by a cane needs a continuous, detectable edge, not a line of cones with gaps
- **Adequate width and firm surface**
- **Cyclists** need to be considered — an arrangement that forces cyclists into a narrowed traffic lane creates a serious conflict
- **Schools and aged care facilities** nearby warrant additional consideration and timing adjustments

**Property and business access** must be maintained wherever practicable, and affected residents and businesses notified in advance.

## Installing and Removing the Arrangement

### Installation Sequence

**Install in the direction of travel — starting furthest from the work area and working toward it.**

1. Advance warning signs first, in sequence
2. Then the taper
3. Then the work area delineation

**Why:** drivers are warned before they encounter the change. Installing the work area delineation first means the crew is working in a live traffic lane with no warning to approaching drivers.

**Installation and removal are the highest-risk phases of the whole operation**, because the crew is exposed in the traffic environment with the arrangement incomplete. Use a shadow vehicle where the TMP specifies one, and work quickly and deliberately.

### Removal Sequence

**Remove in the reverse order — work area first, advance warning last.**

The advance warning stays up until the crew and equipment are clear of the road.

### During Operation — Monitoring

The arrangement must be **inspected regularly** throughout the shift and after any incident:

- Are all devices still in position? **Cones get knocked over and displaced constantly**
- Are signs still upright, visible and legible?
- Has wind moved anything?
- Is the buffer zone still clear?
- Have conditions changed — weather, light, traffic volume?
- Is the arrangement still appropriate for the work being done?

**Record the inspections.** Most TMPs specify an inspection frequency and a record requirement.

## Incident Response

**If a vehicle intrudes into the work area:**
1. **Alert workers** — a designated warning signal or air horn
2. **Move to safety** via the identified escape route
3. Once safe, assess for injuries and call **000** if required
4. Do not attempt to direct or confront the driver
5. Secure the scene
6. **Notify the supervisor immediately**
7. Preserve the scene — this is likely a **notifiable dangerous incident** to the WHS regulator
8. Document everything, including vehicle details and witness contacts

**If the arrangement is damaged or compromised** — a sign knocked down, a barrier displaced — restore it immediately, or **stop work** if it cannot be safely restored.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You arrive at a job to replace a section of kerb on a suburban street. The approved TMP and TGS specify: a 60 km/h approach with a reduced 40 km/h work zone, a lane closure with a taper, advance warning signs at specified spacings, a 15m longitudinal buffer, and a signed pedestrian detour across to the opposite footpath at a nominated point.*

*On arrival you find:*

*- The crew has already set up, but the advance warning signs are placed much closer to the taper than the TGS shows*
*- The 15m buffer contains the crew's ute and a stack of kerb sections*
*- The pedestrian detour signs are up, but the crossing point is blocked by the excavator*
*- Cones are being used to separate traffic from the work area*
*- Two cones near the taper are lying flat, knocked over*

*It is 8:40am and a primary school 200m along the street starts at 9:00am.*

*Identify the problems and describe what you would do.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

There are five non-conformances here, and the timing makes two of them urgent. I would stop work on the kerb and fix the arrangement before 9:00am, because in twenty minutes this street fills with children.

---

**Problem 1 — Materials and vehicle in the buffer zone (most serious)**

**The buffer zone is deliberately empty. That is its entire function.**

It exists to absorb an errant vehicle — a driver who misjudges the taper, is distracted, or does not slow. The 15m gives that vehicle distance to stop, or gives the crew time to move, before it reaches anyone.

**Filling it with a ute and a stack of kerb sections destroys it completely.** Worse than simply removing the protection, it creates new hazards:
- A vehicle striking the stacked kerb sends heavy debris into the work area
- Striking the parked ute sends the ute into the work area
- The stack blocks sight lines and the crew's escape route

The buffer looks like wasted space on a constrained suburban job, which is exactly why it gets used. **Clear it immediately** — the ute goes outside the arrangement entirely, and the kerb sections go into the work area, not the buffer.

---

**Problem 2 — Blocked pedestrian detour, twenty minutes before school (most urgent)**

The detour signs direct pedestrians to cross at a nominated point. **The excavator is sitting on it.**

Pedestrians following the signage arrive at a blocked crossing and then improvise — which on a 60 km/h road means stepping out somewhere unsignalised and unexpected by drivers.

**In twenty minutes those pedestrians include primary school children**, who are shorter and therefore less visible, less predictable, and less able to judge vehicle speed and distance.

**This is the one I fix first.** Either move the excavator clear of the crossing point, or — if it must stay — **stop and escalate**, because relocating a nominated pedestrian crossing point is a change to the approved TMP and not something I can decide on site.

I would also consider whether the school timing warrants raising with the supervisor more broadly. A TMP approved without regard to school pick-up and drop-off times may need reviewing, and a spotter at the crossing during those windows is a sensible additional control.

---

**Problem 3 — Advance warning signs too close to the taper**

Advance warning distances are calculated from approach speed. They give drivers time to perceive the sign, react, and slow before reaching the taper.

**Signs placed too close mean drivers arrive at the taper still doing 60 km/h**, having had insufficient distance to slow to the 40 km/h work zone speed. The result is late braking, abrupt lane changes, and vehicles clipping the delineation — or entering the work area.

**Relocate them to the distances shown on the TGS.** These are not approximate — they come from the standard and were approved.

If the crew shortened them because of a driveway, a bend, or an obstruction, that is a genuine constraint but it is a **TMP variation question**, not a site decision.

---

**Problem 4 — Two cones knocked flat**

Cones are constantly displaced by wind, passing vehicles, and turning traffic. **A flattened cone at the taper is a gap in the delineation exactly where drivers are being asked to change lanes.**

**Reinstate them immediately** — this takes seconds.

More importantly, this indicates the **arrangement is not being monitored**. The TMP will specify an inspection frequency. Someone needs to be checking the layout through the shift, not just at set-up, and recording it.

---

**Problem 5 — Cones as the only separation from traffic**

This may be compliant with the approved TGS, and if the TGS specifies cones then cones are what we use.

But it is worth understanding and, where the risk warrants it, raising: **cones delineate. They do not protect.** A vehicle passes through a line of cones without meaningfully slowing.

Given a 60 km/h approach, a suburban street with turning traffic, and workers on foot at kerb level, I would ask whether physical barriers were considered. If conditions on site differ from what the TMP assumed — higher traffic volume, worse sight distance, longer duration — that is a legitimate basis for requesting a review.

**I would not substitute barriers on my own initiative** — that is also a variation.

---

**My sequence of actions:**

**Immediately, before 9:00am:**

1. **Clear the pedestrian crossing point.** Move the excavator or stop and escalate. This is the item with a hard deadline
2. **Stand the two cones back up.** Ten seconds
3. **Clear the buffer zone.** Ute out of the arrangement, kerb sections into the work area
4. **Relocate the advance warning signs** to the TGS distances

**Then:**

5. **Walk the whole arrangement against the TGS**, from the first advance sign through to the termination, checking every device, position and spacing. If four things were wrong, there may be a fifth I have not spotted
6. **Raise it with the supervisor** — not to blame the crew, but because a set-up with this many departures from the approved TGS suggests the TGS was not being worked from at all, and that is a process problem that will recur
7. **Establish an inspection routine** for the shift, with a record, per the TMP requirement
8. **Brief the crew** — particularly on the buffer zone, since that is the one that will be re-used the moment I turn my back unless people understand why it is empty

---

**How I would raise it with the crew:**

Not as a lecture. Most of this was done under time pressure by people trying to get set up and get on with the job.

*"Give us a hand for ten minutes before the school kicks out — we've got the excavator sitting on the pedestrian crossing point and the school goes in at nine. And I need the ute and the kerb out of the buffer, that's meant to stay empty in case someone comes through the taper."*

Practical, explains the reason, and gets it fixed.

---

**If told to leave it and get on with the kerb:**

I would decline and escalate. The pedestrian crossing issue alone, twenty minutes before a primary school starts, is a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk — and the people at risk are children who have no involvement in this job and no ability to protect themselves from it.

I would exercise the **right to cease unsafe work** if necessary, notify the supervisor, and document the non-conformances and the direction I was given.

---

**The underlying point:**

Every one of these five departures happened for an understandable reason — the buffer was convenient space, the excavator needed to be somewhere, the signs were probably placed where there was room, the cones just fell over.

**None of those reasons survive contact with a driver who does not see the taper, or a seven-year-old following a detour sign to a blocked crossing.** The TGS was approved for this site by someone who assessed it. Implementing it as approved is the whole job.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student identifies all five non-conformances and — critically — prioritises correctly by combining severity with the hard deadline of the school start time, rather than simply listing problems in the order given. The buffer zone analysis is excellent: the student explains not just that it must be empty but that filling it with a vehicle and stacked kerb creates additional projectile and sight-line hazards, which is a level of reasoning beyond the standard answer. The pedestrian issue is correctly identified as most urgent, with sound reasoning about why children are more vulnerable and about improvised crossing behaviour when signed routes are blocked. Throughout, the student maintains the correct boundary between what can be fixed on site and what constitutes a TMP variation requiring a qualified person — reinstating cones and clearing the buffer are site fixes; relocating a nominated crossing point or substituting barriers are not. The delineation-versus-protection distinction is correctly raised without overstepping into unauthorised substitution. Recognising that four departures suggests the TGS was not being worked from at all, and that this is a recurring process problem rather than a one-off, demonstrates systems thinking. The approach to the crew is practical and non-confrontational, and the closing observation — that every departure had an understandable reason and none of them survive contact with the actual hazard — is exactly the right professional framing.
