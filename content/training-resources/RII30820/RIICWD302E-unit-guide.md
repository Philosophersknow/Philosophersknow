## What This Unit Is About

Quality in civil construction is not a matter of doing careful work. It is a matter of doing work that **conforms to a specification** and being able to **demonstrate** that it does.

Those are two separate obligations, and the second one catches people out. Work can be genuinely good and still fail a quality audit because nobody recorded the evidence — and once a layer is buried, the record is the only remaining proof that it was built correctly.

RIICWD302E asks you to work to specification, identify and report non-conformance, and produce the records that make conformance verifiable.

---

## Where the Requirements Come From

Quality requirements are not invented on site. They come from a documented hierarchy.

| Source | What it provides |
|---|---|
| **The contract specification** | The governing document — materials, methods, tolerances, testing, hold points |
| **Drawings** | Geometry, levels, dimensions, details |
| **Australian Standards** | Referenced test methods and material requirements (AS 1289 soil, AS 1141 aggregate, AS 1012 concrete, AS 2870, etc.) |
| **Road authority standards** | State road authority specifications, frequently incorporated by reference |
| **Manufacturer requirements** | Installation methods for proprietary products — pipe, geotextile, membranes |
| **The Inspection and Test Plan (ITP)** | What is inspected and tested, when, by whom, and to what criteria |
| **Approved method statements / SWMS** | The agreed way the work will be done |

**Where documents conflict, there is usually a stated precedence order in the contract.** If there is not, or if the conflict is material, **stop and escalate** — do not resolve a document conflict yourself. See CPCCCM1012 and the drawing conflict example in the trades assessment bank.

**[VERIFY: specific Australian Standards, road authority specification references and precedence provisions vary by project and jurisdiction. Always work from the project's own specification and ITP.]**

## The Inspection and Test Plan

The ITP is the practical quality document. It sets out, for each activity:

- **What is inspected or tested**
- **When** — at what stage
- **By whom** — contractor, superintendent, client, third-party laboratory
- **Against what criteria** — the specification clause and the acceptance limits
- **What record is produced**
- **Whether it is a hold point or a witness point**

### Hold Points and Witness Points

**This distinction is directly assessed and frequently confused.**

| | **Hold point** | **Witness point** |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Work **must not proceed** past this stage until released | The other party has the **opportunity to attend**; work may proceed if they do not |
| **Notice** | Notification required, and release obtained | Notification required |
| **If they don't turn up** | **Work stops.** You wait for release | Work may proceed after the notice period |
| **Typical examples** | Subgrade before base course; reinforcement before pour; excavation before backfill | Routine compaction testing; pipe jointing |

**Proceeding past a hold point without release is a serious non-conformance.** It removes the client's ability to verify work that is about to become permanently concealed — which is the entire purpose of the hold point.

**The commercial consequence is significant:** work built past an unreleased hold point may be required to be exposed for inspection, at the contractor's cost, or rejected outright.

## Tolerances

Specifications state permitted deviation. Work outside tolerance is non-conforming even if it looks fine.

**Typical categories of tolerance:**

| Tolerance type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Level** | Finished surface within ±X mm of design |
| **Line / alignment** | Horizontal position within ±X mm |
| **Grade** | Drainage fall within a stated tolerance of design |
| **Thickness** | Layer thickness not less than specified |
| **Density** | Percentage of MDD achieved |
| **Dimension** | Width, depth, cross-section |
| **Surface finish** | Deviation under a straight edge; ride quality |

**Two things about tolerances that matter practically:**

**1. Some tolerances are one-sided.** A layer thickness "not less than 150mm" is not a ±tolerance — thinner fails, thicker generally does not. A level tolerance of ±10mm is two-sided. **Read which one applies.**

**2. Tolerances do not stack.** If a subgrade is built to the low end of its tolerance and the base course is also built to the low end of its tolerance, the finished surface may be outside its own tolerance even though each layer individually conformed. **Working consistently to one edge of a tolerance band is how a job drifts out of specification while every individual test passes.**

Work to the design, not to the edge of the tolerance.

## Non-Conformance

### What It Is

**A non-conformance is work, material, or a process that does not meet a specified requirement.**

It is not a moral failure and it is not automatically someone's fault. Material arrives out of specification, ground conditions differ from the geotechnical report, drawings conflict, plant breaks down mid-operation. Non-conformance is a normal part of construction — **what matters is that it is identified, reported and resolved rather than concealed.**

### Identifying It

Non-conformance is found through:
- Inspection against the ITP
- Testing — a failed density, moisture, slump or grading result
- Survey — level or alignment outside tolerance
- Material inspection at delivery
- Observation during work
- **Anyone noticing.** Quality is not solely the quality manager's job

### The Process

**1. Stop the affected work.** Do not continue building on or covering non-conforming work. **This is the step that determines whether the fix is cheap or expensive.**

**2. Identify the extent.** Is it one lot, one delivery, one location — or has the same issue affected work already completed? A material problem or a systematic method error usually extends further than the point where it was noticed.

**3. Report it.** To the supervisor, and through to the quality representative, superintendent or client as the contract requires. **Within the timeframe the contract specifies** — many contracts have notification periods.

**4. Raise a Non-Conformance Report (NCR).** Typically containing:
- Identification and date
- What the non-conformance is
- Where — chainage, lot, layer, location
- The specification requirement not met, by clause
- The extent
- The apparent cause
- Proposed disposition
- Who identified it and when

**5. Determine the disposition.** This is not the site crew's decision alone; it usually requires the superintendent or designer.

| Disposition | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Rework** | Bring it into conformance — recompact, relay, regrade |
| **Repair** | Make it fit for purpose by an approved method, without fully meeting the original specification |
| **Accept as-is (concession)** | The designer or superintendent formally accepts the deviation. **Requires written approval** |
| **Reject / remove** | Take it out and rebuild |

**"Accept as-is" is a formal decision by the party entitled to make it, recorded in writing.** It is not a site-level judgement that something is "close enough".

**6. Implement and verify.** Do the rework and **retest**. Both the original failure and the verification result are retained.

**7. Close out.** The NCR is formally closed with evidence attached.

**8. Corrective action — the step that matters most.** Prevent recurrence. If a layer failed because the roller was making insufficient passes, the fix is not just recompacting that layer; it is changing the compaction procedure.

**Rework fixes the instance. Corrective action fixes the cause.**

## Records

### Why Records Are the Deliverable

On a civil project, a substantial part of what the contractor hands over is **evidence that the work conforms**. Buried work cannot be inspected later. The records are the only remaining proof.

**Practical consequences of poor records:**
- Payment claims disputed or withheld
- Practical completion delayed
- Work required to be exposed for inspection at the contractor's cost
- Defects liability disputes with no evidence of what was built
- An adverse audit finding

### What Records Are Kept

| Record | Content |
|---|---|
| **ITP with sign-offs** | Each inspection and test signed as completed |
| **Test records** | Raw data and calculated results, method, equipment calibration |
| **Survey records** | As-constructed levels and alignment |
| **Material conformance** | Delivery dockets, supplier test certificates, batch records |
| **Hold point releases** | Written release before proceeding |
| **NCRs** | Raised, dispositioned and closed |
| **Site diary** | Daily record of work, conditions, plant, personnel, events |
| **Photographs** | Particularly of work about to be concealed |
| **Calibration records** | For all measuring and test equipment |
| **As-constructed drawings** | What was actually built, where it differs from design |

**Photograph anything that is about to be buried.** It costs nothing and it has resolved a very large number of disputes.

### Traceability

For significant materials, the record must connect the material to the location it was placed — which delivery, which batch, which lot, which chainage.

**If a batch of material is later found to be defective, traceability determines whether you replace one lot or the whole job.** Without it, the conservative answer applies.

---

## Calibration

**A measurement from uncalibrated equipment is not evidence.**

- Test and measuring equipment requires calibration at specified intervals, by an appropriate provider
- Calibration certificates are retained and current
- **Equipment that has been dropped or damaged is recalibrated before use** — regardless of its certificate date
- **Field checks** between calibrations: a level's two-peg test, a gas detector's bump test, a scale checked against a known mass

**An uncalibrated instrument does not produce obviously wrong readings.** It produces plausible readings that are consistently and invisibly wrong, which is worse.

## Your Role

At Certificate III level, quality obligations are practical rather than managerial:

- **Know the specification requirements for your task** before you start. "I didn't know the tolerance" is not a defence and it is avoidable
- **Work to the design**, not to the edge of the tolerance
- **Check your own work** before it is inspected
- **Do not proceed past a hold point.** Ever
- **Identify and report non-conformance** — including your own. **Reporting your own error early is professional; concealing it is not, and it is always found**
- **Complete records accurately and at the time**
- **Do not sign for work you did not do or did not check**
- **Never alter a test result or a record**

**Signing an ITP item or a test record is a statement of fact.** Signing something you did not verify is falsification, and it exposes you personally.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are working on a subdivision road. The ITP specifies a **hold point** at subgrade level: the subgrade must be inspected and released by the superintendent before base course is placed.*

*Subgrade is complete and tested. Density passed at 96% against a 95% requirement. Levels are within tolerance.*

*The superintendent was notified yesterday but has not attended and has not issued a release. The base course material is on site, the trucks are booked, and rain is forecast for tonight.*

*Your leading hand says: "It passed all the tests, he's had his notice, and if we don't get the base on tonight the rain will wreck the subgrade and we'll have to redo the lot. Get it on."*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**I don't place the base course, and I escalate immediately — because there is a real problem here and covering the subgrade is not the way to solve it.**

I want to be clear that the leading hand's concern is legitimate. Rain on an exposed subgrade genuinely can destroy it, and if that happens the subgrade does have to be rebuilt. **He is identifying a real risk. He has reached the wrong solution.**

---

**Why I cannot proceed**

**A hold point means work must not proceed until release is issued.** That is the definition, and it is the specific reason a hold point exists rather than a witness point.

**The distinction matters exactly here:**

- If this were a **witness point**, the superintendent would have had the opportunity to attend, and having been given notice and not attended, we could proceed
- It is a **hold point**. Notice is necessary but not sufficient. **Release is required**

**Why the tests passing does not change it:** the hold point is not only about the test results. It exists so the superintendent can **verify the subgrade before it is permanently concealed** — its condition, uniformity, any soft spots, evidence of the surface being disturbed, whether it matches what the test locations suggest. A density result at a handful of test points does not describe the whole lot, and that visual verification is precisely what cannot be done afterwards.

**The consequence if I proceed:**
- It is a **non-conformance** in itself
- The superintendent may direct that the base course be **removed at our cost** so the subgrade can be inspected
- Payment for the lot may be withheld
- It damages the contractor's standing on a project where every subsequent hold point will now be scrutinised harder

**Covering it to protect it, and then being told to uncover it, is the worst of all outcomes** — we would have spent the base course material, the trucks, and the labour, and still have to expose the subgrade.

---

**What I actually do — and this is the useful part**

The rain risk is real, so I do not simply refuse and go home. I try to solve the actual problem.

**1. Escalate right now, urgently — not tomorrow.**

To my supervisor, and I would press for direct contact with the superintendent **today**. Not an email into the void.

The message is specific and it makes the consequence clear to the person who can act:

*"Subgrade at [lot/chainage] is complete, tested and passing — 96% against 95%, levels within tolerance. We notified yesterday. Rain is forecast tonight. We need a release or a decision, because if the subgrade gets wet it will need rebuilding and that's a cost and a delay to the programme."*

**A superintendent who understands that the choice is release-now or rebuild-later will usually find a way to respond.** They may inspect immediately, they may release on the basis of the test results and photographs, or they may give a written instruction. Any of those solves it.

**2. Ask for a documented alternative if he cannot attend.**

Options that are legitimate and that I would put to the supervisor to seek:
- **Release based on submitted evidence** — test results, survey, and photographs, with a written release
- **A written instruction** to proceed, accepting the risk. **In writing.** A verbal "go ahead" from anyone is not a hold point release
- **Delegated inspection** — someone authorised to inspect on the superintendent's behalf

**3. Protect the subgrade instead of covering it.**

This is the practical answer to the actual risk, and it does not require the hold point to be breached.

- **Seal the surface** — a light roll with a smooth drum to close the surface and shed water, if the specification permits it
- **Plastic sheeting** or a temporary membrane over the lot
- **Ensure the drainage works** — check that surface water can run off rather than pond, and that the diversion drains and bunds are functioning. **Ponding is what destroys a subgrade; runoff is survivable**
- **Cut a temporary diversion** to keep upslope water off the area

**Protecting an exposed subgrade from forecast rain is a normal civil construction problem with normal civil construction solutions.** Placing an unreleased base course is not one of them.

**4. Document all of it.**

- Date and time of the original notification, and how it was given
- Test results and survey confirming the subgrade conforms
- Rain forecast and the risk it presents
- Every attempt to obtain a release, with times
- The protection measures implemented
- The direction I received and my response

**This documentation does two things.** It supports a delay or variation claim if the rain does damage the subgrade despite protection — because we notified, we chased, and we mitigated. And it protects me personally if the work is later built past the hold point by someone else.

---

**If I am directed to place the base course anyway**

I decline to place it, and I escalate above the leading hand — to the site supervisor, project manager, or the contractor's quality representative.

**I would not sign any ITP item or record stating the hold point was released when it was not.** That is falsification of a quality record, and unlike a judgement call about programme, it is unambiguous.

I would put my position in writing the same day.

---

**The distinction I would want understood**

The leading hand and I agree on the risk. We disagree on the response.

**His approach solves tonight's problem and creates a larger one that surfaces in a week.** Mine solves tonight's problem by protecting the subgrade and getting a decision from the person entitled to make it.

**The hold point is not an obstacle to managing the rain risk.** It just means the base course is not the thing we use to manage it.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies the hold point versus witness point distinction as decisive and — importantly — explains why notice alone is insufficient for a hold point, which is the precise technical point being assessed. The explanation of *why* the hold point exists beyond the test results, that it permits visual verification of uniformity and condition across the whole lot before permanent concealment, demonstrates understanding rather than rule-following. The observation that covering the subgrade and then being directed to expose it is the worst possible outcome is commercially astute and is the argument most likely to persuade the leading hand. Crucially, the student treats the rain risk as a real problem requiring a real solution rather than dismissing it, and proposes legitimate protection measures with the correct emphasis that ponding rather than rainfall is what destroys a subgrade. Seeking a documented release based on submitted evidence, or a written instruction, shows understanding that there are lawful paths through the problem. The documentation rationale correctly identifies both the variation claim value and personal protection. Refusing to sign a false hold point release, and distinguishing that from a judgement call about programme, is exactly the right line. Strong Competent.
