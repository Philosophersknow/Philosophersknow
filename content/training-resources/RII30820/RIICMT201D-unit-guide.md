## What This Unit Is About

Civil construction is built on materials that look identical and behave completely differently.

Two loads of gravel from the same quarry can have different grading. The same soil at two different moisture contents compacts to entirely different densities. A concrete truck can arrive within specification or well outside it, and you cannot tell by looking.

Materials testing is how you find out before it is buried under a road.

RIICMT201D asks you to sample correctly, conduct basic field tests, record results accurately, and understand what the results mean for whether work can proceed.

---

## Why Testing Matters

**A pavement fails from the bottom up.** A road with an inadequately compacted subgrade will fail regardless of how good the asphalt is, and the failure appears years later when the cause is buried under three layers of material.

**Testing is the only point at which that is preventable**, which is why specifications mandate it and why falsifying a test result is treated so seriously.

| Test failure consequence | Cost |
|---|---|
| Inadequate compaction | Settlement, pavement cracking, premature failure |
| Excessive moisture at placement | Cannot achieve density; instability |
| Out-of-spec concrete | Reduced strength; potential structural failure |
| Incorrect grading | Poor interlock, low strength, drainage failure |

## Sampling — The Foundation

**A test result describes the sample. If the sample does not represent the material, the result is worse than useless — it is misleading with an official number attached.**

### Principles of Representative Sampling

- **Sample from multiple locations**, not one convenient spot
- **Sample at the correct stage** — from the stockpile, the truck, or the placed layer, as the specification directs. These are not interchangeable
- **Avoid segregated material.** Stockpiles segregate: coarse particles roll to the bottom and outside, fines concentrate at the top and centre. Sampling from the toe of a stockpile gives you a coarse-biased sample every time
- **Sample the full depth** of a layer where relevant, not just the surface
- **Combine and reduce properly** — sub-sampling by quartering or with a riffle splitter, not by scooping off the top
- **Label immediately** — location, date, time, chainage, layer, sampled by
- **Seal moisture samples** at once. Moisture content changes from the moment the sample is exposed, and a sample left open in the sun is no longer the material you sampled

### Sampling Frequency

Specified in the project documentation — typically per volume placed, per area, per layer, or per delivery.

**[VERIFY: sampling frequencies and test methods are specified by the project specification and the relevant AS 1289 (soil), AS 1141 (aggregate) and AS 1012 (concrete) test method series. Always work from the project specification and the nominated test method.]**

## Moisture Content

Moisture content governs compaction. It is the single most influential variable in earthworks.

### Why It Matters

Soil compacts best at a specific moisture content — the **Optimum Moisture Content (OMC)**.

- **Too dry** — particles do not lubricate and reposition; the required density cannot be achieved regardless of compactive effort
- **Too wet** — water occupies the void space and cannot be expelled; density cannot be achieved and the material becomes unstable and may pump under plant

**Adding compactive effort does not fix incorrect moisture.** More roller passes on material that is too wet will not achieve density; it will produce a soft, pumping subgrade.

### Determining Moisture Content — Oven Drying

The reference method.

1. Weigh the wet sample and container — record the **wet mass**
2. Dry to constant mass at the specified temperature
3. Weigh again — record the **dry mass**

**Moisture content (%) = (Wet mass − Dry mass) ÷ Dry mass × 100**

**Note the denominator: dry mass, not wet mass.** Using wet mass is the most common calculation error in this unit and it always understates the result.

### Worked Calculation

A sample has a wet mass of 542g and a dry mass of 487g. The container mass has already been deducted.

Mass of water = 542 − 487 = **55g**

Moisture content = 55 ÷ 487 × 100 = **11.3%**

If the OMC for this material is 9.5% with a permitted range of ±2%, then 11.3% is within range and compaction can proceed.

If the OMC were 8.0% with the same tolerance, 11.3% would be **too wet** — the material would need to be aerated and dried before compaction.

### Field Methods

| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Oven drying** | Reference method; slow (hours) |
| **Microwave drying** | Faster; risk of overheating and altering the material |
| **Speedy moisture meter** | Calcium carbide reaction producing gas pressure; rapid field result |
| **Nuclear density/moisture gauge** | Rapid, non-destructive; **requires a licence and radiation safety controls** |

**The nuclear gauge is common on civil sites and carries specific obligations** — licensing, transport requirements, secure storage, personal monitoring, and exclusion distances. It is not a tool anyone picks up.

**[VERIFY: nuclear gauge licensing, transport and storage requirements are set by state radiation safety legislation. Confirm requirements with your state radiation regulator.]**

## Compaction and Density Testing

### The Concepts

**Maximum Dry Density (MDD)** — the highest dry density achievable for a material under a specified laboratory compactive effort, determined at the OMC. Established in a laboratory, not in the field.

- **Standard compaction** — lower compactive effort
- **Modified compaction** — higher compactive effort, used for heavily trafficked pavements

**Field Dry Density (FDD)** — the dry density actually achieved in the placed material.

**Relative compaction** is the comparison:

**Relative compaction (%) = Field Dry Density ÷ Maximum Dry Density × 100**

### Worked Calculation

A subgrade layer is tested. Field dry density measures **1.82 t/m³**. The laboratory MDD for this material is **1.95 t/m³**. The specification requires **95% of modified MDD**.

Relative compaction = 1.82 ÷ 1.95 × 100 = **93.3%**

**93.3% is below the specified 95%. The layer fails and cannot be covered.**

**What happens next:** the layer requires additional compaction, and the moisture content should be checked — if the material is outside the moisture range, further rolling will not achieve density and the moisture must be corrected first.

**Retest after remediation.** The original failing result stays on the record; it is not deleted or replaced.

### Field Density Test Methods

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Sand replacement (sand cone)** | A hole is excavated, the material weighed, and the hole volume measured by filling with calibrated sand |
| **Nuclear density gauge** | Gamma radiation backscatter measures density and moisture. Fast; licensed |
| **Core cutter** | A known-volume cylinder driven into cohesive soil |
| **Balloon densometer** | Hole volume measured with a water-filled membrane |

**Sand replacement remains the reference method** where a dispute arises, because it is a direct physical measurement rather than an inferred one.

## Aggregate Testing

### Particle Size Distribution (Grading)

Determined by **sieve analysis** — passing a dried sample through a nested stack of sieves of decreasing aperture and weighing what is retained on each.

Results are expressed as **percentage passing** each sieve and plotted as a grading curve against the specification envelope.

**Why grading matters:**
- **Well graded** — a continuous range of particle sizes. Smaller particles fill the voids between larger ones, producing good interlock, high density and strength
- **Uniformly (poorly) graded** — particles of similar size. High void content, lower density, less stable
- **Gap graded** — a size range missing, producing a discontinuous curve and unpredictable behaviour

**A grading curve falling outside the specification envelope means the material is not what was ordered**, regardless of what the delivery docket says.

### Worked Calculation — Percentage Passing

A 2000g dried sample is sieved. Mass retained on the 9.5mm sieve is 340g, cumulative retained above it is 260g.

Cumulative retained at 9.5mm = 260 + 340 = **600g**

Percentage retained = 600 ÷ 2000 × 100 = 30%

**Percentage passing 9.5mm = 100 − 30 = 70%**

**Check:** the sum of all retained masses plus the pan should equal the original sample mass, within a small tolerance. If it does not, material has been lost and the test is invalid.

### Other Aggregate Tests

| Test | Measures |
|---|---|
| **Los Angeles abrasion** | Resistance to wear and degradation |
| **Aggregate crushing value** | Resistance to crushing under load |
| **Flakiness index** | Proportion of flat particles — flaky aggregate breaks down and interlocks poorly |
| **Plasticity index (Atterberg limits)** | Behaviour of the fine fraction with moisture. High plasticity fines swell and shrink |
| **CBR (California Bearing Ratio)** | Bearing strength of subgrade or base — used to design pavement thickness |

## Concrete Testing

### Slump Test

Measures the **consistency and workability** of fresh concrete — not its strength.

**Method:**
1. Dampen the slump cone and place it on a level, rigid, non-absorbent base
2. Fill in three approximately equal layers by volume
3. Rod each layer **25 times** with the standard rod, distributed over the surface
4. Strike off level
5. Lift the cone **vertically, smoothly, without twisting**, over 2–3 seconds
6. Measure the slump — the vertical distance from the top of the cone to the displaced centre of the specimen

**Result:** e.g. specified 80mm ± 15mm. A measured slump of 110mm is **outside tolerance** and the load should be rejected.

**What a high slump usually means:** water has been added. Adding water to a concrete load increases workability and **reduces strength** — the water-cement ratio is the primary determinant of concrete strength.

**Never permit water to be added to a load to make it easier to place.** If additional workability is genuinely required, it is achieved with an admixture, by the supplier, with the mix designed accordingly.

### Test Cylinders

Cast for compressive strength testing at specified ages — commonly 7 and 28 days.

- Cast in accordance with the method, rodded or vibrated as specified
- **Cured correctly** — this is critical. Cylinders left in the sun on site do not represent the concrete and will underperform
- Labelled with the date, location in the pour, truck docket and mix
- Transported without disturbance
- Tested at the specified age

**Concrete strength is specified as a grade** — N20, N25, N32, N40 — being the characteristic compressive strength in MPa at 28 days.

### Other Concrete Checks

- **Delivery docket** — mix, grade, volume, batch time. **Concrete has a maximum time from batching to placement.** A load outside that window is rejected
- **Air content** where specified
- **Temperature** — hot and cold weather concreting have specific requirements

## Recording and Reporting

### What a Test Record Must Show

- **Date and time**
- **Location** — chainage, layer, lot, offset
- **Material** identification and source
- **Test method** used, by reference
- **Equipment** used, including calibration status
- **Raw measurements**, not only the calculated result
- **Calculated result**
- **Specification requirement**
- **Pass or fail**
- **Who conducted the test**

**Record the raw data.** A result without the underlying measurements cannot be checked, and an arithmetic error becomes undetectable.

### Test Failure — What You Do

1. **Record the failure.** It stays on the record permanently
2. **Report it immediately** — the supervisor and, depending on the project, the superintendent or client representative
3. **Do not cover the layer.** Once a failing layer is buried, remediation means removing everything above it
4. **Identify the cause** — moisture, insufficient compactive effort, unsuitable material, inadequate layer thickness
5. **Remediate and retest**
6. **Retain both results**

### Falsification

**Falsifying a test result is fraud.** It exposes the individual and the contractor to prosecution, and it has caused structural failures.

The pressure to do it is real — a failing test costs time and money, and it usually arrives at the worst moment. **The pressure does not change the physics.** A layer at 93% compaction will settle whether or not the record says 96%.

If asked to alter a result, decline, document the request, and escalate.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are testing a base course layer on a road construction project. The specification requires 98% of modified maximum dry density.*

*Your results:*
- *Field dry density: 2.14 t/m³*
- *Laboratory modified MDD for this material: 2.21 t/m³*
- *Moisture content: 8.9%*
- *Specified OMC: 6.5%, permitted range ±1.5%*

*The asphalt crew is booked for tomorrow morning. Your leading hand asks whether the layer is "good to go."*

*Calculate the results, state whether the layer passes, and describe what you would do.*

---

### Exemplary Response

**Both tests fail. The layer is not good to go, and the moisture result explains why the density result failed.**

---

**Calculation 1 — Relative compaction**

Relative compaction = Field dry density ÷ Maximum dry density × 100

= 2.14 ÷ 2.21 × 100
= **96.8%**

**Specification requires 98%. The layer fails by 1.2 percentage points.**

---

**Calculation 2 — Moisture content against the permitted range**

Specified OMC is 6.5% with a permitted range of ±1.5%, giving an acceptable band of **5.0% to 8.0%**.

Measured moisture content is **8.9%**.

**That is 0.9% above the upper limit. The material is too wet.**

---

**Why these two results are connected — and this is the important part**

The moisture failure is not a separate problem. **It is the cause of the density failure.**

At 8.9% moisture, the voids in the material are partially occupied by water. Water is effectively incompressible over the timescale of a roller pass, so the compactive effort cannot expel it and cannot bring the particles into closer contact. The material has reached the maximum density achievable **at that moisture content**, which is below the specified density.

**This means additional rolling will not fix it.** More passes on material that is too wet will:
- Fail to increase density meaningfully
- Risk **pumping** — the layer becoming unstable and losing what strength it has
- Potentially damage the layer below

**The moisture has to come down first.** Then compaction can achieve the specified density.

---

**What I actually do**

**1. Record both results as failures, with the raw data.**

Field dry density, laboratory MDD, the calculated relative compaction, the moisture content, the specified OMC and range, the test methods, equipment and calibration status, chainage and layer, date and time, and my name.

**The failing results stay on the record.** They are not deleted when the retest passes — both results are retained.

**2. Report immediately — today, not tomorrow morning.**

To my leading hand first, then ensure it reaches the supervisor and, depending on the project's arrangements, the superintendent or client representative.

**Immediately matters because of the asphalt booking.** There is a day to fix this. If it is raised tomorrow when the asphalt crew arrives, the options collapse to postponing at short notice or — much worse — someone deciding to proceed.

**3. Answer the leading hand's question directly.**

*"No — it's at 96.8% and we need 98. And the moisture's at 8.9 against a range that tops out at 8.0, which is why it's not making density. Rolling it more won't get us there while it's that wet. We need to aerate and dry it back into range, then re-roll and retest. Do you want me to let the supervisor know about the asphalt booking now so there's time to move it?"*

**Giving him the cause and a path forward, plus flagging the schedule consequence, is more useful than just reporting a failure.**

**4. Remediation.**

The material needs to be dried into the moisture range. Depending on conditions and the project method:
- **Aerate** — scarify or turn the layer to expose it and allow moisture loss
- Allow drying time, weather permitting
- Then **recompact** and **retest**

**If the weather is against us**, drying may not be achievable in the timeframe, in which case the asphalt is postponed. That is a genuine possibility and it needs to be visible to the people managing the programme today.

**5. Retest and record.**

Retest both moisture and density after remediation. Both must pass. Record the retest alongside the original failure.

**6. Consider whether there is a wider problem.**

One failing lot may be local. But it is worth asking:
- Is the whole layer this wet, or just this location? Additional test locations would establish that
- Did the material arrive wet, or has it been rained on since placement?
- Is the layer thickness correct? An over-thick layer will not compact through its full depth even with correct moisture
- Is the roller achieving the required passes, and is it the right roller for this material?

**If the material arrived outside the moisture range, that is a supply issue** worth raising, because the next load will be the same.

---

**What I will not do**

**Sign the layer off.** Not because of a rule, but because I know what happens next. A base course at 96.8% under new asphalt will settle differentially. The pavement will crack, and the failure will appear in a year or two when the cause is buried under the surfacing and nobody can economically fix it.

**And if I am asked to adjust the number** — I decline, document the request, and escalate. **Falsifying a test result is fraud**, it exposes both me and the contractor, and the settlement happens regardless of what the record says.

The asphalt booking is a scheduling cost. A failed pavement is a rehabilitation cost with an order of magnitude between them.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. Both calculations are correct and clearly set out, including deriving the acceptable moisture band from the OMC and tolerance rather than comparing to the OMC alone. The critical insight — that the moisture failure is the *cause* of the density failure, not a coincidental second problem — is exactly what this unit is assessing, and the explanation of why (water occupying void space and being effectively incompressible under a roller) is technically sound. Correctly identifying that additional rolling will not achieve density and risks pumping demonstrates practical understanding beyond the arithmetic. The reporting is appropriately urgent and the student recognises specifically that raising it today rather than tomorrow preserves the options. The response to the leading hand delivers the result, the cause, the remediation and the schedule consequence in a few sentences, which is how this conversation actually needs to go on site. The wider-cause investigation — additional test locations, layer thickness, roller adequacy, and whether the material arrived wet — shows the student thinking about whether this is one lot or a systemic issue. The closing refusal to falsify, framed around the eventual settlement rather than the rule, is the correct professional position. Strong Competent.
