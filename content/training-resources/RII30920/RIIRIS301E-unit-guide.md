## What This Unit Is About

RIIWHS201E covers following the site's WHS system. RIIRIS301E covers **building** part of it — identifying hazards, assessing risk, selecting controls, and reviewing whether they worked.

This is a step up in responsibility. It is the unit that moves you from complying with a risk assessment someone else wrote to contributing to one, and eventually leading one.

---

## Risk Management Terminology

Getting these terms right matters, because assessors mark on them and because they are used precisely in the industry.

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hazard** | A source or situation with the potential to cause harm | An unshored 2m trench |
| **Risk** | The likelihood and consequence of harm occurring from the hazard | The chance a worker is buried, and how badly |
| **Consequence** | The severity of the outcome if it occurs | Fatality |
| **Likelihood** | The probability of it occurring | Possible |
| **Risk rating** | Likelihood combined with consequence | Extreme |
| **Control** | A measure that eliminates or reduces the risk | Trench shield |
| **Residual risk** | The risk remaining after controls are applied | Low |
| **Inherent risk** | The risk before controls are applied | Extreme |
| **Risk appetite** | The level of risk an organisation is willing to accept | Set by the organisation |

**The most commonly marked error is using "hazard" and "risk" interchangeably.** A hazard is a thing. A risk is a probability and a consequence attached to that thing. Write them precisely.

## The Risk Management Process

The standard cycle, based on the approach in **AS/NZS ISO 31000** and reflected in WHS regulation:

### 1. Establish the Context
What is the scope? What work, what location, what people, what timeframe? What legal and site requirements apply?

### 2. Identify Hazards
Find everything that could cause harm.

### 3. Analyse and Evaluate Risk
Determine likelihood and consequence; assign a rating; decide whether it is acceptable.

### 4. Treat the Risk
Select and implement controls, using the hierarchy.

### 5. Monitor and Review
Check the controls are in place, being used, and working. Revisit when anything changes.

### 6. Communicate and Consult — Throughout
Not a step at the end. **Consultation with workers is a legal requirement**, not good practice.

## Hazard Identification

### Methods

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Workplace inspection** | Walking the site systematically, looking |
| **Task analysis** | Breaking a job into steps and identifying hazards at each step |
| **Consultation** | Asking the workers who do the job — they know hazards that are invisible from an office |
| **Incident and near-miss review** | What has already gone wrong, or nearly gone wrong, here or elsewhere |
| **Reviewing SDS** | Hazards of chemicals in use |
| **Plant and equipment manuals** | Manufacturer-identified hazards |
| **Industry guidance and codes of practice** | Known hazards for this type of work |
| **Checking legislation and standards** | Mandated requirements |

**Consultation is the most underrated method.** The plant operator knows which corner has the blind spot. The pipelayer knows which ground collapses. Risk assessments written without asking them miss things.

### Hazard Categories in Civil Construction

Working through categories systematically prevents omissions:

| Category | Civil construction examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical** | Noise, vibration, heat, UV, radiation |
| **Mechanical** | Mobile plant, moving parts, crush and entanglement |
| **Gravitational** | Falls, falling objects, trench collapse, load failure |
| **Electrical** | Overhead lines, underground cables, site power, plant electrics |
| **Chemical** | Fuels, solvents, concrete (alkaline burns), contaminated ground |
| **Respirable** | Silica dust, diesel particulate, welding fume, asbestos in ground |
| **Biological** | Sewage, contaminated water, Leptospirosis, snakes and spiders |
| **Ergonomic** | Manual handling, repetitive work, sustained postures |
| **Psychosocial** | Fatigue, workload, bullying, isolation, remote work |
| **Environmental** | Weather, heat stress, storm, flooding, poor visibility |
| **Traffic** | Public traffic, site traffic, pedestrian interaction |

## Risk Assessment

### The Risk Matrix

Risk rating combines **likelihood** and **consequence**.

**Consequence scale (typical):**

| Level | Descriptor | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insignificant | No injury, minor inconvenience |
| 2 | Minor | First aid injury |
| 3 | Moderate | Medical treatment injury, lost time |
| 4 | Major | Serious injury, permanent disability |
| 5 | Catastrophic | Fatality or multiple fatalities |

**Likelihood scale (typical):**

| Level | Descriptor | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| A | Almost certain | Expected to occur in most circumstances |
| B | Likely | Will probably occur |
| C | Possible | Might occur at some time |
| D | Unlikely | Could occur but not expected |
| E | Rare | May occur only in exceptional circumstances |

**Matrix:**

| | 1 Insignificant | 2 Minor | 3 Moderate | 4 Major | 5 Catastrophic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A Almost certain** | Medium | High | High | **Extreme** | **Extreme** |
| **B Likely** | Medium | Medium | High | High | **Extreme** |
| **C Possible** | Low | Medium | High | **Extreme** | **Extreme** |
| **D Unlikely** | Low | Low | Medium | High | **Extreme** |
| **E Rare** | Low | Low | Medium | High | High |

**[VERIFY: Matrix structure, scale descriptors, and rating bands vary between organisations. Always use your site's matrix — do not import one from elsewhere.]**

### Typical Response by Rating

| Rating | Response |
|---|---|
| **Extreme** | **Stop work.** Immediate senior management attention. Work does not proceed until risk is reduced |
| **High** | Senior management attention required; detailed controls and documented plan before proceeding |
| **Medium** | Management responsibility specified; controls implemented and monitored |
| **Low** | Manage by routine procedures |

### Assessing Consequence Honestly

**Assess consequence on the realistic worst case, not the average case.**

A worker in an unshored 2m trench: the average outcome of a minor wall slump might be a bruise. The **realistic worst case** is burial and asphyxiation — catastrophic.

**Under-rating consequence is the most common failure in workplace risk assessment**, and it is usually driven — consciously or not — by the fact that a lower rating permits the work to continue.

**Note that a catastrophic consequence rates as Extreme or High at every likelihood level, including Rare.** This is deliberate. Fatality risks cannot be rated away by arguing they are unlikely.

## The Hierarchy of Controls

Controls are applied in order. A lower-level control is only acceptable where the higher levels are not reasonably practicable.

| Level | Control | Civil construction example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Elimination** | Remove the hazard entirely | Directional boring instead of open trenching — removes the excavation and entry hazard completely |
| **2. Substitution** | Replace with something less hazardous | Vacuum excavation instead of hand digging near services; lower-silica products |
| **3. Isolation** | Separate people from the hazard | Trench shields; hard barriers between traffic and workers; plant exclusion zones |
| **4. Engineering** | Physical modification | Shoring and battering; reversing cameras and proximity detection; on-tool dust extraction; water suppression |
| **5. Administrative** | Procedures, training, signage | Permits; spotters; SWMS; job rotation to limit vibration exposure; traffic management plans |
| **6. PPE** | Worn by the worker | Hard hat, hi-vis, boots, eye and hearing protection, P2 respirator |

**Levels 1–4 are more reliable than 5–6** because they do not depend on human behaviour. A trench shield protects a worker who is tired, distracted, or new. A procedure only protects a worker who follows it.

### Reasonably Practicable

The WHS Act requires controls "so far as is reasonably practicable." This weighs:

1. The **likelihood** of the hazard or risk occurring
2. The **degree of harm** that might result
3. What the person **knows, or ought reasonably to know**, about the hazard and ways to eliminate or minimise it
4. The **availability and suitability** of ways to eliminate or minimise it
5. **After** assessing the above — the **cost**, including whether it is grossly disproportionate to the risk

**Cost is considered last, and only after the risk is understood.** It cannot be the starting point, and a control cannot be rejected simply because it costs money — only where the cost is **grossly disproportionate** to the risk. For a fatality risk, that bar is very high.

**"We can't afford scaffold" is not a lawful reason to work at height without fall protection.**

### Combining Controls

Rarely does a single control suffice. Most significant risks require layers:

**Excavation near services:**
- Elimination — bore instead of trench where possible
- Substitution — vacuum excavation to expose services
- Isolation — exclusion zone; trench shield
- Engineering — shoring; edge protection
- Administrative — Before You Dig enquiry, service locating, permit, SWMS, spotter
- PPE — hard hat, hi-vis, boots, gloves

## Documenting Risk

### SWMS — Safe Work Method Statement

Required for **high risk construction work**. A SWMS must:
- Identify the high risk construction work
- State the hazards and risks
- Describe the control measures
- Describe how the controls will be implemented, monitored, and reviewed
- Be developed **in consultation with the workers** who will do the work
- Be available at the workplace and provided to workers before work starts
- Be **reviewed and revised** when the work method, conditions, or controls change

**A SWMS is a living document.** If ground conditions change, the plan changes, or a new hazard appears, the SWMS must be revised — not ignored.

### Risk Register

A documented list of identified risks with their ratings, controls, responsible person, and review dates. Maintained at project level.

### Take 5 / Step Back 5

A short personal pre-task assessment. Typically:
1. Stop and think about the task
2. Look for hazards
3. Assess the risks
4. Control the hazards
5. Proceed safely — or stop and escalate

**The value of a Take 5 is that it catches what changed since the SWMS was written** — the weather, the ground, the adjacent work, the equipment available today.

## Monitoring and Review

Controls fail. They get removed, damaged, worked around, or become inadequate as conditions change.

**Review triggers:**
- An incident or near miss
- A change in work method, plant, or materials
- A change in conditions — weather, ground, adjacent activity
- New information — a safety alert, a recall, a regulator notice
- Worker concern raised
- Scheduled periodic review
- New legislation or codes of practice

**Monitoring methods:**
- Site inspections and audits
- Observing whether controls are actually being used
- Incident and near-miss data
- Health monitoring where required — noise, silica, hazardous chemicals
- Consultation and toolbox feedback

**A control that exists on paper but is not in place on site is not a control.**

---

## Consultation — A Legal Duty

Under the WHS Act, a PCBU **must consult** workers who are, or are likely to be, directly affected by a health and safety matter. This includes:
- Identifying hazards and assessing risks
- Making decisions about control measures
- Making decisions about facilities, procedures, training, and monitoring
- Proposing changes that may affect health and safety

**Consultation means workers get to contribute before decisions are made**, not be informed afterwards.

**Mechanisms:** Health and Safety Representatives (HSRs), Health and Safety Committees, toolbox talks, prestart meetings, SWMS development, and direct discussion.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*Your crew is to replace a section of failed stormwater pipe in a suburban street. The excavation will be approximately 2.4m deep and 1.2m wide, in a road reserve. The street carries moderate traffic and has a footpath on both sides used by pedestrians including school children in the afternoon. Services in the area include water, gas, and communications. The work will take three days and includes a concrete cut through the road surface.*

*Conduct a risk assessment: identify the significant hazards, assess them, and select controls using the hierarchy.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I would work through this by hazard category to avoid missing anything, then assess and control each.

**Note on method:** I am assessing **inherent risk** (before controls) to determine how much control is required, then stating the **residual risk** after controls. Assessing only the controlled risk hides how much the controls are doing.

---

### Hazard 1 — Excavation collapse

**Hazard:** A 2.4m deep, 1.2m wide unsupported excavation with workers inside.

**Consequence:** **Catastrophic (5).** A cubic metre of soil weighs roughly 1.5–2 tonnes. Burial to chest height causes fatal crush asphyxiation. Workers do not self-rescue from this.

**Likelihood (inherent, unsupported):** **Possible (C)** — and higher here than in undisturbed ground, because a road reserve containing existing services is **previously disturbed backfill**, which is substantially less stable than virgin ground. Traffic vibration adjacent to the excavation is a further destabilising factor.

**Inherent rating: EXTREME.**

**Controls:**
- **Isolation (L3):** **Trench shield / shoring box** — the primary control. It protects workers inside even if the walls fail. Battering is impractical here: at 2.4m depth, achieving a safe batter angle would require a far wider excavation than the road reserve allows
- **Engineering (L4):** Shoring designed for the ground conditions and assessed by a competent person; edge protection
- **Administrative (L5):** Spoil placed **minimum 1m back from the edge** — spoil at the edge surcharges the wall and is a leading cause of collapse; safe access and egress with a ladder within the required distance of any worker; daily inspection of the excavation by a competent person, and after rain; SWMS; no-entry rule when the shield is not in position
- **PPE (L6):** Hard hat, hi-vis, boots

**Residual: Medium.**

**Note:** excavation with risk of a person being buried is **high risk construction work — a SWMS is legally required** before work starts.

---

### Hazard 2 — Striking underground services (gas, electrical, water, comms)

**Hazard:** Excavating in a road reserve containing live services.

**Consequence:** **Catastrophic (5).** A struck gas main risks explosion and fire affecting the crew and surrounding residents. A struck HV cable risks electrocution and arc flash.

**Likelihood (inherent, plans only):** **Likely (B).** Service plans show approximate positions and are frequently inaccurate — services get relocated and as-built records are often wrong.

**Inherent rating: EXTREME.**

**Controls:**
- **Elimination (L1):** Consider whether any section can be relined or bored rather than open-cut, removing the excavation entirely
- **Substitution (L2):** **Vacuum excavation / potholing** to positively expose services before any mechanical excavation near them — this replaces the high-energy method with a non-destructive one
- **Administrative (L5):** **Before You Dig enquiry** lodged and plans obtained; **qualified service locator** engaged to physically locate and **mark** services on the ground (yellow gas, red electrical, blue water, white comms); permit to excavate; exclusion distance for mechanical excavation near located services, with hand or vacuum digging inside it; asset owner notified where required; emergency contacts for each asset owner on site
- **PPE (L6):** As above; appropriate for arc flash where relevant

**Residual: Medium.**

**Critical point:** plans are step one of four — **enquire, locate, mark, then excavate carefully.** Plans alone are never authority to dig.

---

### Hazard 3 — Public traffic

**Hazard:** Work in a live road reserve with moving traffic adjacent.

**Consequence:** **Catastrophic (5).** A vehicle intrusion into the work area, or into an open excavation, can kill multiple workers.

**Likelihood (inherent, no traffic management):** **Likely (B).**

**Inherent rating: EXTREME.**

**Controls:**
- **Isolation (L3):** **Hard physical barriers** between the traffic lane and the work area — not cones alone. Cones delineate; they do not stop a vehicle. A **buffer zone** between the traffic lane and the work area
- **Administrative (L5):** **Traffic Management Plan** prepared by a qualified person, compliant with **AS 1742.3** and the road authority requirements; **accredited traffic controllers**; advance warning signage and correct taper lengths; road authority permit; consider partial or full road closure — for a three-day job with a 2.4m excavation, full closure may be both safer and simpler than managing live traffic; reduced speed limit through the works
- **PPE (L6):** Hi-vis to the required class — note that **night or low-light work requires a higher hi-vis class**

**Residual: Medium.**

---

### Hazard 4 — Pedestrians, including school children

**Hazard:** Open excavation and plant movement adjacent to footpaths used by children in the afternoon.

**Consequence:** **Catastrophic (5).** A child falling into a 2.4m excavation, or being struck by plant.

**Likelihood (inherent):** **Possible (C)** — and I would rate this higher than for adult pedestrians. Children are less predictable, less visible to plant operators, and may be attracted to the site rather than avoiding it.

**Inherent rating: EXTREME.**

**Controls:**
- **Isolation (L3):** **Continuous physical fencing** around the excavation — not bunting or tape, which children walk through. Fencing must be secure and stable
- **Engineering (L4):** **Excavation covered or securely fenced overnight and whenever unattended** — this is the highest-risk period, with no crew present. Plates or a locked barrier
- **Administrative (L5):** Signed **pedestrian detour** with a safe, compliant alternative route — not simply "footpath closed" leaving pedestrians to walk on the road; scheduling the noisiest and highest-risk activity outside school pick-up times where practicable; a spotter during plant movement at peak pedestrian times; notification to the adjacent school and residents before work starts
- **PPE (L6):** N/A for public — the control must protect people who have no PPE and no induction

**Residual: Low–Medium.**

**Key principle:** the public are unprotected, uninducted, and include children. **Controls that rely on the public behaving sensibly are not controls.**

---

### Hazard 5 — Respirable crystalline silica from concrete cutting

**Hazard:** Cutting the road surface releases respirable crystalline silica.

**Consequence:** **Major (4)** — silicosis is irreversible, progressive, and can be fatal; also linked to lung cancer. Delayed onset, but permanent.

**Likelihood (inherent, dry cutting):** **Almost certain (A).**

**Inherent rating: EXTREME.**

**Controls:**
- **Elimination (L1):** Have the surface cut off-site or use pre-cut sections where the design allows — rarely available for road work
- **Substitution (L2):** Lower-silica products where any reinstatement material is being selected
- **Engineering (L4):** **Wet cutting with water suppression at the blade** — the primary control; **on-tool dust extraction with H-class vacuum**; enclosed cab with filtered positive-pressure air for plant operators
- **Administrative (L5):** Exclusion zone downwind; **no dry sweeping** — wet methods or vacuum only; limit exposure duration; **health monitoring** where required for silica-exposed workers
- **PPE (L6):** **P2 minimum respirator, fit-tested.** Facial hair compromises the seal

**Residual: Low–Medium.**

**Never dry cut.** Workplace exposure standards for RCS have been substantially tightened in Australia.

---

### Hazard 6 — Mobile plant / worker interaction

**Hazard:** Excavator and truck movements in a confined work area with workers on foot.

**Consequence:** **Catastrophic (5).** Being struck or crushed by plant is a leading cause of civil construction fatalities.

**Likelihood (inherent):** **Possible (C).**

**Inherent rating: EXTREME.**

**Controls:**
- **Isolation (L3):** **Exclusion zones** around operating plant; physically segregated pedestrian routes through the site where practicable
- **Engineering (L4):** Reversing cameras, alarms, proximity detection
- **Administrative (L5):** **Spotter** for reversing and confined movements; **positive communication** — eye contact with the operator and acknowledgement before approaching, never assuming you have been seen; defined traffic flow for trucks; competent and licensed operators; pre-start plant inspections
- **PPE (L6):** Hi-vis

**Residual: Medium.**

**Note:** hi-vis does not make a worker visible in a blind spot. Standing in a blind spot in hi-vis offers no protection.

---

### Other hazards to include

| Hazard | Key controls |
|---|---|
| **Noise** (saw, breaker, plant) | Quieter methods and plant, barriers, distance, exposure rotation; hearing protection; audiometric monitoring |
| **Vibration** (breaker, compactor) | Low-vibration tools, job rotation to limit exposure duration |
| **Manual handling** (pipe, plates, shoring) | Mechanical lifting, two-person lifts, correct technique |
| **Contaminated ground / sewage** | Ground assessment before work; hygiene facilities; gloves; vaccination status |
| **Heat stress** | Hydration, shade, rotation, rescheduling to cooler hours, monitoring |
| **Fatigue** | Shift limits, adequate turnaround, monitoring — three consecutive days of physical work |
| **Confined space** (if entering a pit or manhole) | **Permit, atmospheric testing, standby person, rescue plan** — never enter to rescue without BA |

### Consultation

I would develop this assessment **with the crew**, not for them — through the SWMS development process and prestart meetings. The plant operator knows the blind spots on that machine. The pipelayer has seen how this ground behaves in this suburb. That knowledge does not come from a desk.

### Monitoring and Review

**Review triggers for this job specifically:**
- **Rain** — significantly changes excavation stability and must trigger reinspection before entry
- Ground conditions differing from expectation once opened
- A service found in an unexpected position
- Any incident or near miss
- A change in the work method or plant
- Public complaint or observed pedestrian behaviour indicating the barriers are inadequate

**Daily:** excavation inspection by a competent person before entry; Take 5 before each task; prestart briefing.

### Summary

| Hazard | Inherent | Primary control | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Excavation collapse | Extreme | Trench shield | Medium |
| Underground services | Extreme | Locate, mark, vacuum excavate | Medium |
| Public traffic | Extreme | TMP, hard barriers, closure | Medium |
| Pedestrians / children | Extreme | Continuous fencing, covered overnight | Low–Medium |
| Silica dust | Extreme | Wet cutting, extraction, P2 | Low–Medium |
| Plant interaction | Extreme | Exclusion zones, spotter | Medium |

**Every one of these rates Extreme inherently, and five of the six have a catastrophic consequence.** That is not unusual for civil work in a live road reserve — it is why the controls are extensive and why a SWMS is legally mandatory.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student works systematically by hazard rather than impressionistically, and the decision to assess inherent risk before residual — with an explicit statement of why — demonstrates genuine understanding of what a risk assessment is for. Consequences are rated honestly on realistic worst case throughout, with no evidence of the under-rating that commonly creeps in to permit work to proceed. Several judgements show real field knowledge: recognising that road reserve backfill is previously disturbed and less stable than virgin ground, that traffic vibration destabilises the excavation, that battering is geometrically impossible in the available width, and that overnight and unattended periods are the highest-risk window for public exposure. The pedestrian assessment correctly rates children as a higher likelihood than adults and states the governing principle that the public are unprotected and uninducted. Controls are correctly ordered by hierarchy, with vacuum excavation properly identified as substitution rather than lumped in with administrative controls. The distinction between cones as delineation and hard barriers as protection is correct and frequently confused. The consultation section correctly frames it as developing the assessment with the crew, with concrete examples of what they know that a desk-based assessor does not. Rain as a review trigger is exactly the kind of specific, job-relevant trigger the unit is looking for. Strong Competent.
