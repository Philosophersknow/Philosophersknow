## What This Unit Is About

On a civil site, communication failure is a safety hazard.

A spotter who cannot reach a plant operator, a level instruction misheard by 100mm, a hazard nobody reported, a shift handover that omitted the failed shoring — these are not administrative problems. They are the mechanism by which most site incidents actually occur.

RIICOM201D asks you to communicate clearly in a noisy, dispersed, high-risk environment, use the site's systems and terminology correctly, and document what needs documenting.

---

## Why Civil Sites Are Communication-Hostile

Understanding the constraints explains why the protocols exist.

| Constraint | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Noise** | Speech is frequently inaudible. Hearing protection compounds it |
| **Distance and dispersal** | Crew spread across hundreds of metres, out of line of sight |
| **Plant** | Operators are enclosed, cannot hear, and have restricted vision |
| **PPE** | Hard hats, glasses, hearing protection and masks obscure face and voice |
| **Multiple employers** | Principal contractor, subcontractors, utilities, traffic control — different systems and terminology |
| **Rotating personnel** | Casual and labour hire crew who may not know the site or the people |
| **Language diversity** | A significant proportion of the civil workforce speaks English as an additional language |
| **Time pressure** | Rushed communication is truncated communication |

**None of these are solved by telling people to communicate better.** They are solved by protocols that work despite the conditions.

## Verbal Communication on Site

### Being Understood the First Time

- **Get attention before giving information.** Say the person's name, wait for acknowledgement, *then* speak. Information delivered to someone who was not listening has not been communicated
- **Face the person.** Lip reading and expression carry a substantial share of comprehension in noise
- **Short sentences, one instruction at a time.** "Move the excavator back two metres" lands; a three-part instruction in noise does not
- **Use plain terms and avoid idiom.** Idiom is the first thing to fail with a speaker of English as an additional language
- **Specify, don't approximate.** "Two metres" not "back a bit"
- **Confirm understanding by asking for it back**, not by asking "got that?" — to which everyone says yes

### Read-Back Confirmation

For anything safety-critical or numerical, the receiver **repeats the instruction back**.

> "Set the invert at 24.275."
> "Invert 24.275, confirmed."

**This is not bureaucracy.** It catches the difference between 24.275 and 24.375 before a drainage line is laid to the wrong level, and it catches "two metres" heard as "two feet".

### Standard Site Calls

Common calls vary between sites, but the function is universal — a short, unambiguous, loud signal that means one thing.

| Call | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **"Clear!"** | The area is clear; safe to proceed |
| **"Hold!" / "Stop!"** | Cease immediately |
| **"Behind you"** | Someone or something is approaching from behind |
| **"Below!"** | Something is falling or being lowered |
| **"All stop"** | Everything ceases — used for an incident or emergency |

**Anyone can call a stop.** This is worth stating explicitly: on a well-run site, the most junior person present can call "stop" and everything stops, no questions asked in the moment. Questions come afterwards.

## Radio Communication

Radio is the primary tool for dispersed and out-of-sight communication.

### Discipline

- **Listen before transmitting.** Transmitting over someone else blocks both messages
- **Identify yourself and who you are calling** — "Excavator one, this is spotter"
- **Wait for acknowledgement** before delivering the message
- **Keep it short.** Radio is not a conversation medium
- **Say "over"** when you finish a transmission and expect a reply; **"out"** when the exchange is complete
- **Use the correct channel.** Cross-channel traffic causes confusion and blocks operational communication
- **No non-operational chat on an operational channel.** It masks real traffic and desensitises people to the radio

### The Phonetic Alphabet

Used for anything where a single letter matters — chainage references, lot numbers, plate numbers, drawing references.

Alpha · Bravo · Charlie · Delta · Echo · Foxtrot · Golf · Hotel · India · Juliett · Kilo · Lima · Mike · November · Oscar · Papa · Quebec · Romeo · Sierra · Tango · Uniform · Victor · Whiskey · X-ray · Yankee · Zulu

**Numbers are read individually** — "one four zero", not "a hundred and forty" — because grouped numbers are misheard.

### Radio Silence and Emergency Traffic

If an emergency is declared on the channel, **all other traffic ceases** until it is cleared. An emergency transmission blocked by routine chatter is a genuine and recurring problem.

## Hand Signals

Where noise prevents voice communication and plant is operating, **hand signals are the primary method.**

### Principles

- **Signals must be agreed before work starts** — at the prestart, so everyone is using the same set
- **One signaller per machine.** Multiple people signalling one operator is how conflicting instructions get followed
- **The signaller must be visible to the operator** and in a position of safety with an escape route
- **The operator stops if the signaller disappears from view.** Losing sight of the signaller is a stop condition, always
- **Signals must be unambiguous** — clear, deliberate, held long enough to be seen

### Common Signals

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Arm raised, palm forward | **Stop** |
| Both arms raised, palms forward | **Emergency stop** |
| Hand beckoning toward the body | Come toward me |
| Hand pushing away | Move away |
| Finger pointing down, circular motion | Lower |
| Finger pointing up, circular motion | Raise |
| Hands indicating a gap, narrowing | Slow / approaching position |
| Hands clasped | Secure / hold position |

**[VERIFY: standard hand signals for plant and lifting operations are specified in AS 2550 for cranes and in various codes of practice. Confirm the signal set applicable to the plant and jurisdiction, and use the set agreed at the site prestart.]**

**Everyone on site can signal stop.** Only the designated signaller directs movement.

## Site Meetings and Briefings

### Prestart / Toolbox

Held at the start of the shift or before a new phase of work.

**What it covers:**
- The day's work and sequence
- Hazards specific to today and to this location
- Controls in place and who is responsible
- Changes since yesterday — weather, conditions, other trades, plant
- **Anything raised by the crew**
- Permits in force
- Emergency arrangements

**Your obligations:**
- **Attend and pay attention.** A prestart you sat through without listening is a prestart you did not attend
- **Raise hazards and concerns.** You see things the supervisor does not
- **Ask if anything is unclear.** A clarifying question takes ten seconds; a misunderstanding takes an incident
- **Sign the record** — which means you attended and understood, not that you agreed with everything

**A prestart where nobody says anything is not a good prestart.** It usually means people have learned that raising things is unwelcome.

### Pre-Task Assessment (Take 5 / Step Back 5)

A brief individual assessment before starting a task, particularly when something has changed. Its value is that it catches what changed since the prestart or since the SWMS was written — the weather, the ground, the equipment available today.

## Shift Handover

**Handover is one of the highest-risk moments on any site**, because information that exists in one person's head has to survive a transfer.

**What must be handed over:**

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| **Work completed** and to what point | So the incoming crew knows the state |
| **Work in progress** and its current condition | Particularly anything left in a temporary state |
| **Anything left unsafe or incomplete** | **The single most important item** — an unbraced structure, an open excavation, a partially installed control |
| **Plant status** — faults, fuel, servicing | So a defective machine is not started |
| **Permits in force or expired** | A permit does not carry over automatically |
| **Materials** — delivered, outstanding, short | |
| **Changed conditions** | Weather, ground, services found, other trades |
| **Incidents, near misses, hazards** raised | |
| **Instructions received** from the supervisor or client | |

**Handover should be face to face wherever possible, and written as well.** A verbal handover with no record fails when the receiving person is interrupted between hearing it and acting on it.

**Do not hand over by leaving a note and going home** on anything that matters.

## Written Communication and Records

### Common Site Documents

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Daily diary / site log** | Work done, hours, plant, weather, deliveries, visitors, issues |
| **Prestart and toolbox records** | Attendance and topics |
| **Hazard report** | Hazard identified, location, risk, action taken or required |
| **Incident report** | What happened, when, who, what was done |
| **Take 5 / pre-task record** | Individual pre-task assessment |
| **Test records** | Materials testing results with raw data |
| **Delivery dockets** | Material received, quantity, quality |
| **Plant prestart checklist** | Daily plant inspection |
| **Permits** | Confined space, excavation, hot work, isolation |
| **Non-conformance report** | Work not meeting specification and the proposed rectification |
| **Timesheet** | Hours worked |

### Writing Records Properly

**Factual.** What you observed, measured or did — not what you concluded.

**Objective.** "Trench wall showing 200mm of material loss at 1.4m depth, water seeping from sand layer" is objective. "Trench looked dodgy" is not.

**Specific.** Times, locations, chainages, measurements, names.

**Timely.** Written at the time or as close as practicable. Memory degrades and reconstructed records are less reliable and less defensible.

**Legible and unambiguous.**

**Signed and dated.**

### Why Records Matter Beyond Compliance

- **A variation claim requires a contemporaneous record.** Changed ground conditions, a drawing conflict, a client instruction — if it is not recorded when it happens, the commercial claim is much weaker
- **An incident investigation relies on what was recorded**, and gaps are interpreted unfavourably
- **A test record without raw data cannot be checked**
- **Your own protection.** If you raised a hazard, refused unsafe work, or declined an improper instruction, the record is what establishes it

**"If it isn't written down, it didn't happen" is a slightly unfair summary of a substantially true position.**

## Communicating With People Outside the Crew

### The Public

Civil work happens in and around where people live and travel.

- **Be civil.** You represent the contractor and the client, and a complaint about crew conduct travels a long way
- **Do not argue.** A resident angry about noise, access or dust is not going to be persuaded and the interaction will be reported
- **Refer enquiries** to the supervisor or the designated community contact. Do not speculate about the programme, the design, or how long the work will take
- **Never discuss** cost, fault, other parties' performance, or anything about an incident
- **Safety first.** If a member of the public is in a hazardous position, address the safety issue immediately and politely, then refer them on

### Other Contractors and Utilities

- Coordinate at the interfaces — where your work meets theirs
- **Raise clashes rather than working around them.** A conflict between your work and another party's is a documentation or coordination issue that belongs with the supervisor
- Do not direct another employer's workers, and do not accept direction from them without your supervisor's knowledge

### Cultural and Language Diversity

- **Plain language, no idiom.** Idiom fails first
- **Confirm understanding** by having the instruction repeated back, not by asking whether it was understood
- **Use demonstration** — showing is more reliable than telling across a language barrier
- **Do not raise your voice.** Volume does not aid comprehension and reads as aggression
- **Written and visual support** where available — diagrams, marked-up drawings, translated safety material
- **Never assume that silence means agreement or understanding.** In several cultural contexts, questioning a supervisor is not done, and an unclear instruction will be acknowledged rather than queried

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are finishing a night shift on a drainage job. During your shift the following occurred:*

*- A 40m section of pipe was laid and backfilled to subgrade level*
*- The next 12m of trench is open, 1.9m deep, with the trench shield still in position*
*- The excavator developed a hydraulic leak at about 2am and was shut down and tagged out*
*- A telecommunications conduit was found at 1.1m depth, not shown on the service plans, at approximately chainage 340. Work stopped in that area and it was left exposed and protected*
*- Rain started around 4am and is continuing*
*- A confined space permit for the downstream pit was issued at 10pm and expired at 6am*

*The day shift leading hand arrives. Give your handover.*

---

### Exemplary Response

**I would give this face to face, walk the site with him where practicable, and write it in the diary. Several of these items cannot safely be left as a note.**

---

**The handover, in order of what could hurt someone:**

---

**1. Open trench, 1.9m deep, 12m length, shield in position — and it's been raining since 4am.**

*"The trench from about 352 to the end is still open — 1.9 deep with the shield in. But it's been raining for a couple of hours and nobody's been in there since. That needs a proper look by a competent person before anyone goes down, because the water changes the wall stability and we don't know what it's done overnight."*

**Why this is first:** a 1.9m excavation is a fatality risk, rain materially changes wall stability, and the shield being in position does not mean the trench has been assessed since the rain started. **Rain is an explicit trigger to reinspect an excavation before re-entry.** If I hand over "trench's open, shield's in" without flagging the rain, the day crew may reasonably treat it as ready.

**2. Unidentified telecommunications conduit at chainage 340, 1.1m depth, not on the plans, left exposed and protected.**

*"We found a comms conduit at about 340, 1.1 down, and it's not on any of the plans we've got. We stopped in that area, it's exposed and protected. Nothing goes in there with a machine until it's been identified and the asset owner's been contacted — and it needs to go back to the supervisor because the plans are wrong, which means they might be wrong further along too."*

**Why this matters beyond the one conduit:** an unmarked service means the service location information for this job is unreliable. **That has implications for the rest of the alignment**, not just chainage 340. The day crew needs to know they cannot rely on the plans.

**3. Confined space permit expired at 6am.**

*"The permit for the downstream pit was issued at 10 last night and expired at 6. Nobody's in there and it's not been re-tested. If you need to go in, it's a new permit — fresh gas test, the lot."*

**Permits do not carry over.** This is a specific and easily missed item, and an expired permit treated as current is a serious matter — the atmosphere has not been tested for hours and conditions may have changed, particularly with rain entering the system.

**4. Excavator tagged out — hydraulic leak.**

*"The excavator's down. Hydraulic leak started about 2am, I shut it down and tagged it. Don't untag it — it needs a fitter. And there's fluid on the ground where it stopped, around 348, so that needs cleaning up and the spill kit's been used."*

**Two parts here that get separated and shouldn't be:** the machine is out of service **and** there is an environmental issue on the ground that needs remediation. A tagged machine with unmentioned spilled fluid means the spill sits there.

**5. Rain — ongoing, and its wider effect.**

Beyond the trench: ground conditions for plant, whether the backfilled section will hold moisture and affect compaction testing, and whether the day's programme is achievable.

*"It's still raining. Apart from the trench, the backfilled section's going to be wet — if you're testing compaction today, moisture's likely to be out of range and you won't make density. Might be worth checking before booking anything."*

**This is genuinely useful information rather than just a weather report** — it connects the rain to a specific consequence the day shift can act on.

**6. Work completed.**

*"We got 40 metres in and backfilled to subgrade, from about 312 to 352. That's not been density tested yet."*

**Flagging that it is untested matters** — otherwise the day crew may assume the layer is signed off.

---

**What I write in the diary:**

- Shift times, crew, weather including the time rain started
- Work completed: 40m pipe laid and backfilled to subgrade, chainage 312–352, **not yet tested**
- Open excavation: chainage 352 onward, 12m, 1.9m depth, shield in position, **reinspection required before entry due to rain**
- **Unidentified telecommunications conduit, chainage ~340, 1.1m depth, not shown on service plans. Exposed and protected. Work suspended in that area. Asset owner notification required. Service plan reliability queried for the balance of the alignment**
- Excavator [ID]: hydraulic leak identified approx. 0200, shut down and tagged out, fitter required. Hydraulic fluid spill at approx. chainage 348, spill kit deployed, remediation outstanding
- Confined space permit [number], downstream pit, issued 2200, **expired 0600. Space not re-tested. New permit required for any entry**
- Handover given verbally to [name] at [time], site walked

---

**What I do beyond talking:**

**Walk the site with him** for the trench, the exposed conduit and the tagged machine. Three items on this list are things you point at rather than describe — the location of an exposed service, the state of a trench wall, and where the fluid is on the ground.

**Confirm he's got it** — not by asking "all good?" but by having the critical items repeated back. Specifically the conduit chainage and the trench reinspection requirement.

**Escalate the conduit myself** rather than leaving it entirely to the handover. An unmarked service is a notifiable matter to the supervisor and potentially to the asset owner, and it should not wait on a handover conversation to be actioned.

---

**Why I would not just leave a note:**

Four of these six items are things where a misunderstanding causes an injury or a serious incident:

- Someone entering the trench without reinspection after rain
- Someone excavating near an unmarked live comms conduit
- Someone entering the pit on an expired permit
- Someone untagging the excavator

**A written record is necessary and it is not sufficient.** The written diary is what survives; the face-to-face walk is what ensures it was actually received and understood before anyone starts work.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student orders the handover by potential for harm rather than by chronology, which is the correct professional judgement and is what distinguishes a competent handover from a recitation. Every high-risk item is identified and, more importantly, the *implication* is drawn for each: rain as a trigger for excavation reinspection; the unmarked conduit as evidence that the service plans are unreliable for the whole alignment rather than a single localised finding; the expired permit requiring a fresh gas test rather than a renewal; and the rain's effect on moisture content and therefore on compaction testing, which connects the weather to something the day shift can act on. The identification of the unmentioned hydraulic spill as a separate outstanding item alongside the tagged machine catches something that would otherwise sit unremediated. The written record is factual, specific, and includes chainages, times and the explicit outstanding actions. The insistence on walking the site for the three items that must be pointed at rather than described, and on read-back confirmation of the two most critical items, demonstrates understanding that handover is a transfer of understanding rather than a transfer of information. Escalating the conduit independently rather than relying on the handover chain is correct. Strong Competent.
