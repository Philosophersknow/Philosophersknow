## What This Unit Is About

RIICCM201D covers the **arithmetic** — how to calculate an area, a volume, a load count. This unit covers the **process**: measuring completed work for payment, recording it in a form the client will accept, and understanding how quantities move money.

Civil contracts are frequently paid on measured quantities. Work that was done and not measured is work that was not paid for. Work measured incorrectly is a dispute, and disputes are resolved on records rather than recollection.

---

## How Civil Contracts Pay

Understanding the payment model explains why measurement matters and what is being measured.

| Contract type | How payment works | Measurement role |
|---|---|---|
| **Schedule of rates / measure and value** | A rate per unit for each item; paid on quantity actually completed | **Critical.** Quantity directly determines payment |
| **Lump sum** | Fixed price for defined scope | Measurement supports progress claims and variations |
| **Cost plus** | Actual cost plus margin | Records of resources used |
| **Bill of quantities** | Priced quantities; adjusted for actual | Measurement confirms or adjusts |

**On a schedule of rates contract, measurement is the payment mechanism.** If 840 metres of pipe were laid and 820 were measured, the contractor is paid for 820.

**[VERIFY: contract type, measurement rules and payment provisions are project-specific. Always work from the contract and the method of measurement it nominates.]**

---

## Units of Measurement

Each work item has a specified unit, and using the wrong one produces a meaningless claim.

| Item | Typical unit |
|---|---|
| Excavation, fill, concrete | **m³** (cubic metre) |
| Pavement layers, sealing, topsoil, geotextile | **m²** (square metre) |
| Pipe, kerb, fencing, line marking | **m** (linear metre) |
| Pits, manholes, headwalls, signs, trees | **each / No.** (item count) |
| Reinforcement, structural steel | **t or kg** (mass) |
| Bitumen, sealant | **L or t** |
| Traffic control, dewatering, site establishment | **day / week / lump sum** |

**Read the schedule item wording carefully.** "Excavate and dispose" is a different item from "excavate" — one includes cartage and disposal and one does not. Claiming the wrong item is either underclaiming or a disputed overclaim.

---

## Methods of Measurement

**A method of measurement defines the rules** — what is included in an item, what is measured separately, how deductions are handled, and how quantities are computed.

**The two most consequential rules:**

**1. What the rate includes.** A rate for pipe laying may be deemed to include bedding, jointing, and backfill — or those may be separate items. Claiming separately for something the rate is deemed to include is an overclaim; failing to claim a separate item is lost revenue.

**2. Deductions.** Whether voids, openings and inserted items are deducted, and above what size. A method of measurement typically specifies a threshold below which no deduction is made.

**Never assume the measurement rules.** They are stated in the contract, and different projects use different ones.

## Measuring in Practice

### Linear Items

Measured along the item as constructed — along the centreline for pipe, along the face for kerb.

- **Measure along the actual line**, including curves. A curved kerb measured as a straight chord underclaims
- **Confirm whether pits and structures are deducted** from pipe length. Commonly pipe is measured between the inside faces of pits, but this is a rules question
- **Record chainages** — from and to. This is what makes the measurement checkable

### Area Items

- Measured on the **plan (horizontal) area**, not the slope area, unless the rules state otherwise
- Break irregular shapes into rectangles and triangles
- **Record the dimensions used**, not just the total. A total with no dimensions cannot be verified

### Volume Items

- **Cross-sections at regular chainages**, with volume computed between them by the **average end area method** (see RIICCM201D)
- **State the measurement basis** — bank, loose, or compacted. **These are different quantities for the same work** and a claim that does not state the basis is ambiguous
- For excavation, whether measured to the design line or to the actual excavated line is a rules question. **Over-excavation is usually not paid for**

### Item Counts

Straightforward but easy to get wrong through omission. Count systematically, record locations, and reconcile against the drawings.

## Survey and As-Constructed

**Survey is the authoritative measurement** for anything geometric.

- **Pre-construction survey** establishes existing surface levels. Without it, cut and fill volumes cannot be computed and the contractor cannot prove what was there
- **As-constructed survey** records what was actually built — levels, alignment, invert levels, service locations
- **Set-out records** connect the work to the survey control

**Pre-construction survey is the one that gets skipped and the one whose absence is most expensive.** If existing levels were never recorded, there is no basis to demonstrate the volume of material moved, and the claim reduces to argument.

**As-constructed information is a contract deliverable in most civil contracts** — it is what the asset owner needs to maintain the asset, and practical completion may depend on it.

## Progress Claims

Measurement feeds the periodic payment claim.

**The cycle:**
1. Measure work completed during the period
2. Compile the claim by schedule item, with supporting measurement records
3. Submit within the contractual timeframe
4. The superintendent assesses and certifies
5. Payment follows within the contractual period

**Two things that cost contractors money:**

**Underclaiming.** Work completed but not measured and claimed in the period. It can usually be claimed later, but it delays cash flow, and on a long job the cumulative effect is significant.

**Overclaiming.** Claiming work not completed, or claiming items the rate is deemed to include. This gets rejected, and a pattern of it damages credibility so that subsequent legitimate claims are scrutinised harder.

**Claim what was done, measured properly, with the records attached.**

**[VERIFY: progress claim timeframes and security of payment provisions are governed by state legislation and the contract. Confirm the applicable requirements.]**

## Variations

A variation is a change to the contracted scope — additional work, deleted work, or changed work.

**Common triggers in civil construction:**
- Changed ground conditions differing from the geotechnical information
- Unidentified services requiring relocation or a changed method
- Design changes and drawing revisions
- Client-directed additional work
- Suspension or delay outside the contractor's control

**The critical requirement: contemporaneous records.**

A variation claim made three months later, from memory, with no record of when the condition was encountered, what was done, or what resources were used, is a weak claim. The same claim supported by a dated diary entry, photographs, survey, plant hours and labour records is a strong one.

**What to record when something changes:**

| Record | Why |
|---|---|
| **Date and time** the condition was encountered | Establishes when the entitlement arose and supports notice compliance |
| **Photographs** | Unarguable, and free |
| **Survey** of the actual condition | Quantifies it |
| **Description** of how it differs from the documented condition | Establishes the basis |
| **Plant and labour** deployed, with hours | Quantifies cost |
| **Materials** used | Quantifies cost |
| **Delay** caused | Supports a time claim |
| **Instructions received**, from whom and when | Establishes direction |
| **Notice given**, when and to whom | Contracts have notice periods — **missing a notice period can extinguish an otherwise valid claim** |

**The notice requirement is where valid claims die.** Many contracts require notice of a variation or a delay within a stated number of days. **The work being genuinely additional does not matter if the notice was not given in time.**

**Your role at this level:** record accurately and immediately, and tell your supervisor the moment something differs from the documents. You are frequently the only person who sees the changed condition, and the claim depends on your diary entry.

## Records for Measurement

| Record | Content |
|---|---|
| **Measurement sheet** | Item, location, dimensions used, calculation, quantity, date, measured by |
| **Site diary** | Daily work, location, plant, labour, weather, deliveries, events |
| **Survey records** | Pre-construction, set-out, as-constructed |
| **Plant hours** | Machine, operator, hours, task, location |
| **Labour records** | Personnel, hours, task |
| **Delivery dockets** | Material received |
| **Photographs** | Dated, located, and particularly of work about to be concealed |
| **Instructions register** | Verbal and written instructions received |

### Writing a Measurement Record Properly

**Show the dimensions and the calculation, not just the answer.**

Poor:
> *Excavation — 218 m³*

Good:
> *Excavation, stormwater trench, Ch 312–397 (85m)*
> *Trapezoidal section: base 1.0m, top 2.2m, depth 1.6m*
> *CSA = ½ × (1.0 + 2.2) × 1.6 = 2.56 m²*
> *Volume = 2.56 × 85 = **217.6 m³** (bank measure)*
> *Measured [date] by [name]. Survey ref [x].*

**The second version is checkable.** The first is an assertion. When a claim is assessed, a checkable measurement is accepted and an assertion is queried — which delays payment even when the number is right.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are working on a schedule of rates contract. During excavation of a drainage trench you encounter rock at 1.2m depth, continuing to the design invert at 1.8m, over a length of 26m. The geotechnical report indicated no rock in this area.*

*The schedule of rates includes separate items for "Excavation in material other than rock" and "Excavation in rock", with the rock rate approximately four times the soil rate.*

*The trench is 1.1m wide. Your leading hand tells you to "just keep digging, we'll sort out the paperwork later."*

*What do you do, and what do you record?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**"Sort out the paperwork later" is how a legitimate claim gets lost.** I record it now, and I make sure it is reported today — because the entitlement depends on evidence that has to be captured before the rock is removed and the trench is backfilled.

---

**Why this matters commercially**

The rock rate is approximately **four times** the soil rate. This is not a minor administrative distinction.

**Rough quantification of what is at stake:**

Rock volume = length × width × depth of rock
= 26 × 1.1 × (1.8 − 1.2)
= 26 × 1.1 × 0.6
= **17.16 m³**

If the soil rate were, say, $80/m³ and the rock rate $320/m³, the difference is 17.16 × $240 = **roughly $4,100** on this section alone.

**And the rock may continue.** If it extends further along the alignment, the exposure grows — and the geotechnical report indicating no rock means nobody priced for it.

**More significantly, there may be a variation entitlement beyond the rate difference** — changed ground conditions differing from the documented information can give rise to a claim for the changed method, additional plant (a rock breaker or hammer attachment), reduced production rate, and delay.

**None of that survives without contemporaneous records.**

---

**The two things that will kill the claim if I do nothing**

**1. Notice.** The contract almost certainly requires notice of a changed condition or a variation **within a stated period** — commonly days, not weeks.

**Missing the notice period can extinguish an otherwise entirely valid claim.** The rock being genuinely there and genuinely unexpected does not matter if notice was not given in time. This is the single most common way civil contractors lose money they were entitled to.

**2. Evidence, captured before the condition is destroyed.** Once the rock is broken out and carted away and the trench is backfilled, the only remaining proof is what was recorded. **You cannot photograph rock that is no longer there.**

---

**What I do — today, in this order**

**1. Tell my supervisor now, not at the end of the shift.**

*"We've hit rock at 1.2 in the trench from about [chainage], running down to invert at 1.8. It's not on the geotech. It's a different schedule item and the rate's about four times, and there may be a variation on top for the changed method. I'm recording it and photographing it now — but someone needs to check the notice period today, because if we miss that we lose it."*

**Flagging the notice period explicitly is the most valuable thing I can say.** The supervisor may not have it front of mind, and it is the one element with a hard deadline.

**2. Photograph it before anything is removed.**

- The exposed rock face in the trench, with a **scale reference** — a survey staff, a tape, or a marked rod showing the depth at which rock begins
- Wide shots establishing the location, with recognisable features
- The chainage marker or an identifiable reference in frame
- Photographs at **intervals along the 26m**, not one shot — to establish the extent
- The plant and attachment being used

**Dated, and with the location recorded.** Photographs with no location reference are much weaker evidence.

**3. Get it surveyed if at all possible.**

A survey of the rock surface level along the section quantifies it properly and is far stronger than a tape measurement. I would request this through the supervisor.

If survey is not available immediately, I measure and record: the depth at which rock was encountered at multiple points along the 26m, the trench width, and the design invert level.

**4. Record it in the site diary, immediately.**

> *[Date, time]. Rock encountered in stormwater trench, Ch [x] to Ch [y], length 26m. Rock surface at approx. 1.2m below existing surface, continuing to design invert 1.8m. Trench width 1.1m. Rock not indicated in geotechnical report [ref]. Photographs [numbers] taken before excavation. Depth measured at [x]m intervals — readings recorded on measurement sheet [ref]. Reported to [supervisor name] at [time]. Excavation method changed from bucket to hydraulic hammer attachment at [time]. Production rate reduced from approx. [x]m/hr to [y]m/hr.*

**The production rate note matters** — it quantifies the delay component of a claim, and it is a detail almost nobody records at the time.

**5. Record the resources.**

- **Plant** — machine, attachment, hours on rock excavation specifically, separated from soil excavation
- **Labour** — personnel and hours
- **Any additional plant** brought in, with mobilisation

**Separate the rock excavation hours from the soil excavation hours.** If they are recorded as one block, the additional cost cannot be substantiated.

**6. Measure the quantities separately.**

Two schedule items now apply to this section:

> *Excavation in material other than rock: Ch [x]–[y], 26m × 1.1m × 1.2m depth = **34.32 m³***
> *Excavation in rock: Ch [x]–[y], 26m × 1.1m × 0.6m depth = **17.16 m³***
> *Total = 51.48 m³, consistent with full-depth trench volume 26 × 1.1 × 1.8 = 51.48 m³* ✓

**The cross-check matters** — the two items must sum to the total trench volume. If they do not, one of them is wrong.

Dimensions and calculation shown, so the claim is checkable rather than asserted.

---

**Why I do not just keep digging quietly**

The work has to continue — I am not suggesting we stop excavating. **The point is that recording it costs almost nothing and takes minutes, and not recording it costs thousands and cannot be recovered.**

The photographs take five minutes. The diary entry takes five minutes. The notice, if the supervisor acts on it today, takes a phone call. **Against approximately $4,100 on this section alone, plus whatever the variation is worth, plus whatever the rock does further along.**

There is also a straightforward point about who bears the loss. If this is not claimed, the contractor absorbs the cost of excavating rock at a soil rate. On a schedule of rates contract that is exactly the risk the two separate items exist to allocate — and the allocation only works if the rock is measured as rock.

---

**One thing I would raise beyond this section**

**The geotechnical report was wrong here, which means it may be wrong elsewhere.**

That has two implications worth flagging:
- **Commercially** — there may be more rock on the alignment, and the contractor should know that its pricing basis is unreliable
- **Practically** — the excavation method, plant requirements and programme for the remainder need reviewing

That is a supervisor and project manager matter, but the observation comes from me because I am the one who found it.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student immediately identifies that the instruction to defer paperwork is the risk, and correctly isolates the two mechanisms by which the claim would be lost — the contractual notice period and the destruction of the evidence once the rock is removed. Flagging the notice period explicitly to the supervisor, as the element with a hard deadline, is the single most commercially valuable action available and most candidates miss it entirely. The quantification is correct and, importantly, the student cross-checks that the two schedule items sum to the total trench volume — a verification that would catch a dimensional error before it reached a claim. The evidence-gathering is thorough and practical: scale references in photographs, multiple shots along the extent rather than one, and separating rock excavation plant hours from soil excavation hours so the additional cost is substantiable. The production rate note is a detail that quantifies delay and is almost never recorded contemporaneously. Recognising that a wrong geotechnical report has implications for the remainder of the alignment, both commercially and for method, demonstrates thinking beyond the immediate section. The measurement record shows dimensions and calculation, making it checkable rather than asserted. Strong Competent.
