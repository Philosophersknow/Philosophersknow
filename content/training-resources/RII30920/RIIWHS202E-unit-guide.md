## What This Unit Is About

Confined space entry kills people who were trying to help.

That is the defining fact of this unit. A substantial proportion of confined space fatalities are **would-be rescuers** — a worker collapses, a colleague goes in to pull them out without breathing apparatus, and both die. It happens repeatedly, it is entirely predictable, and it is why the procedural requirements around confined spaces are stricter than almost anything else in civil construction.

RIIWHS202E asks you to identify a confined space, understand the hazards, work within a permit system, and understand why you never enter to rescue.

## What Is a Confined Space

A confined space is an enclosed or partially enclosed space that:

- Is **not designed or intended primarily for human occupancy**
- Has a **restricted means of entry and exit**
- And **is or may be**:
  - At atmospheric pressure while occupied, **and**
  - At risk of containing an atmosphere with **harmful contaminant** levels, an **oxygen deficiency or excess**, or a risk of **engulfment**

**All parts of that definition matter.** A space can be cramped and awkward without being a confined space, and a space can be large and open-topped and still be one.

### Civil Construction Examples

| Confined space | Why |
|---|---|
| **Stormwater and sewer pits** | Restricted access, gas accumulation, engulfment risk |
| **Manholes and access chambers** | Restricted access, oxygen deficiency, hydrogen sulphide |
| **Culverts** | Restricted entry, poor ventilation |
| **Tanks and vessels** | Residual contents, oxygen displacement |
| **Shafts and caissons** | Depth, gas accumulation, restricted egress |
| **Pipes over ~600mm diameter** | Restricted entry and egress, atmosphere |
| **Deep unventilated excavations and trenches** | **In some circumstances** — where gas may accumulate or oxygen may be displaced. Depth alone does not make a trench a confined space, but a deep trench in contaminated ground or near a gas main may qualify |

**The trench case is the one most often got wrong.** A trench is not automatically a confined space, but it can become one. Where there is any doubt, it is assessed as one.

## The Hazards

### 1. Oxygen Deficiency

Normal air is approximately **20.9% oxygen**.

| Oxygen level | Effect |
|---|---|
| 19.5% | **Minimum safe level for entry** |
| 16–19% | Impaired judgement and coordination — and the person does not notice |
| 12–16% | Rapid fatigue, faulty judgement, poor coordination |
| 10–12% | Nausea, unconsciousness |
| Below 10% | Unconsciousness within minutes; death |
| Below 6% | Immediate collapse; death |

**[VERIFY: oxygen threshold values and entry limits against your state WHS regulations and the confined spaces code of practice. 19.5% is the commonly specified minimum but confirm the applicable figure.]**

**The critical point: oxygen deficiency gives no warning.** There is no smell, no taste, no visible sign. The impairment of judgement begins before the person recognises anything is wrong, which is exactly why they do not climb out.

**How oxygen gets displaced:**
- **Rust and oxidation** consuming oxygen in a steel structure over time
- **Biological decomposition** in a sewer or wet pit
- **Displacement by heavier gases** — carbon dioxide, argon, nitrogen, or gas from purging
- **Combustion** — a running engine or a hot work operation consuming oxygen
- **Absorption** by damp soil or concrete curing

### 2. Oxygen Enrichment

Above approximately **23.5%** oxygen, the fire risk increases dramatically. Materials that will not normally burn readily become highly flammable, and clothing saturated with oxygen can ignite from a spark and burn ferociously.

**Cause:** leaking oxygen cylinder or hose, commonly from cutting equipment. **Never use oxygen to ventilate a confined space** — this is a recurring fatal error.

### 3. Toxic Atmosphere

| Gas | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)** | Sewers, wet pits, decomposing organic matter | **Smells of rotten eggs at low concentrations, then deadens the sense of smell entirely at higher ones.** Losing the smell is a sign of increasing danger, not decreasing. Rapidly fatal |
| **Carbon monoxide (CO)** | Engines, generators, combustion | Colourless, odourless. Binds haemoglobin far more readily than oxygen |
| **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** | Decomposition, combustion, fermentation | Heavier than air — accumulates at the bottom of a space |
| **Methane (CH₄)** | Sewers, decomposing matter, landfill, natural | Flammable and asphyxiant. Lighter than air |
| **Solvent and fuel vapours** | Paints, adhesives, coatings, fuel residue | Flammable and toxic. Heavier than air |
| **Welding fume** | Hot work inside the space | Accumulates rapidly in poor ventilation |

**Gas stratification matters.** Heavier-than-air gases pool at the bottom; lighter-than-air gases collect at the top. **Atmospheric testing must be conducted at multiple levels** — top, middle and bottom — because a clear reading at the entry point tells you nothing about conditions at the base.

### 4. Flammable Atmosphere

Measured as a percentage of the **Lower Explosive Limit (LEL)** — the concentration below which a gas will not ignite.

Entry is typically prohibited above a specified fraction of the LEL — commonly **5% or 10% LEL**, depending on the jurisdiction and the work being performed.

**[VERIFY: permissible LEL thresholds for entry and for hot work against your state regulations.]**

### 5. Engulfment

Free-flowing solids or liquids entering the space and burying or drowning the occupant.

- Water surge in a stormwater system — **a storm kilometres upstream can fill a pit with no warning at the work site**
- Sand, grain, or gravel flowing from a hopper or stockpile
- Sludge in a wet well
- Backflow from a connected system

**Isolation of connected systems is essential**, not optional.

### 6. Other Hazards

- **Restricted egress** — an injured person cannot self-extract from a manhole
- **Temperature extremes** — heat stress in an unventilated space is rapid and severe
- **Mechanical** — plant, agitators, or valves that could operate while someone is inside
- **Electrical** — often wet, often earthed metal structures
- **Noise** — amplified in a reflective enclosed space
- **Biological** — sewage, contaminated water, leptospirosis, needlestick risk
- **Poor lighting**
- **Psychological** — claustrophobia and panic, which impair judgement

## The Confined Space Entry Permit

**Entry to a confined space requires a written permit.** This is not administrative preference; it is a regulatory requirement.

### What a Permit Contains

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Identification of the space** | Which specific space, located precisely |
| **The work to be performed** | Including whether hot work is involved |
| **Period of validity** | Permits are time-limited. A permit does not carry over to the next shift |
| **Hazard identification and risk assessment** | Specific to this space and this task |
| **Isolation** | What has been isolated, how, and verified by whom — **lock-out/tag-out** |
| **Atmospheric test results** | Recorded values at multiple levels, with time and instrument details |
| **Ventilation** | Method and confirmation it is operating |
| **Required PPE and equipment** | Including respiratory protection if required |
| **Names of authorised entrants** | Only named persons may enter |
| **Name of the standby person** | And confirmation they are in position |
| **The rescue plan** | Documented, resourced and achievable |
| **Communication method** | How the entrant and standby will maintain contact |
| **Signatures** | Permit issuer, entrants, standby |
| **Cancellation** | Signed off when the work is complete and everyone is out |

**A permit signed without reading it is worthless — and it is evidence that you were informed of the controls.**

### Permit Conditions Change → Permit Is Void

If **anything** changes — a gas reading shifts, ventilation fails, the work method alters, a new hazard appears, the time expires — **everyone exits and the permit is reassessed.** You do not "finish up quickly."

## Before Entry — The Sequence

### 1. Eliminate the Need to Enter

**This is the first control and it is skipped constantly.**

Can the work be done from outside?
- Remote inspection with a camera or borescope
- Long-handled tools
- Vacuum extraction rather than manual removal
- Remote sampling
- Relocating the task

**Most confined space fatalities occur during work that could have been avoided or performed differently.** If entry can be eliminated, the entire hazard profile disappears.

### 2. Isolate

All connected energy sources and systems must be isolated and **locked out**:

- **Mechanical** — agitators, pumps, conveyors physically locked out
- **Electrical** — isolated, locked, tagged, and **tested dead**
- **Hydraulic and pneumatic** — de-pressurised and locked
- **Process lines** — blanked, spaded, or physically disconnected. **A closed valve is not an isolation** — valves leak and valves get opened by someone who does not know you are in there
- **Upstream flow** — for a stormwater or sewer system, the connected network

Isolation must be **verified**, not assumed, and the verification recorded on the permit.

### 3. Purge and Ventilate

- **Purge** to remove contaminants — with air or an appropriate inert gas, never with oxygen
- **Ventilate** continuously, mechanically, throughout the entry
- Position the ventilation intake in clean air, away from exhaust or plant
- **Ventilation must continue while the space is occupied** — an initial clearance does not persist

### 4. Test the Atmosphere

**Testing is done before entry and monitored continuously during occupancy.**

**Test in this order:**
1. **Oxygen** — because the other sensors may not read correctly in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere
2. **Flammable gases** (LEL)
3. **Toxic gases** — H₂S, CO, and any specific contaminant expected

**Test at multiple levels** — top, middle, bottom — because gases stratify.

**Test from outside first**, lowering the probe, before anyone enters.

**The gas detector must be:**
- **Calibrated** and within its calibration date
- **Bump tested** before use — a functional check confirming the sensors respond to gas. A calibrated instrument with a dead sensor reads a comfortable zero
- Appropriate for the contaminants expected

**A gas detector reading zero because it is faulty is indistinguishable from a safe space.** The bump test is what tells them apart.

### 5. The Standby Person

A standby person is stationed **outside** the space for the entire duration.

**Their role:**
- Maintain continuous communication with the entrant
- Monitor the atmosphere and the conditions
- Control access — nobody enters who is not on the permit
- **Initiate the rescue procedure**
- Account for everyone

**The standby person does not enter the space. Ever.** Not to help, not briefly, not to hand something down. The moment the standby enters, there is nobody outside and a single incident becomes a double fatality.

**The standby person must not have other duties.** Standing by is the job.

### 6. The Rescue Plan

**A documented, resourced, rehearsed rescue plan must exist before entry.**

**It must be achievable within minutes.** Emergency service response time will generally exceed the survival window in an oxygen-deficient or toxic atmosphere.

**"Call 000" is not a rescue plan.**

**Non-entry rescue is the preferred method** — retrieving the person without anyone entering:
- **Full body harness** worn by the entrant, attached to a retrieval line
- **Tripod or davit with a mechanical winch** at the entry point
- The line managed by the standby person

**Where entry rescue is unavoidable**, it requires:
- Trained rescuers with **self-contained breathing apparatus**
- Their own standby arrangements
- Equipment in position before the original entry begins

## During Entry

- **Continuous atmospheric monitoring** — personal gas detector worn by the entrant, and monitoring at the entry
- **Continuous communication** — voice, radio, or an agreed signal system, checked at intervals
- Harness and retrieval line attached
- Lighting appropriate to the space, and **intrinsically safe** where a flammable atmosphere is possible
- **Exit immediately** on any alarm, any change in conditions, or any instruction from the standby person

**Do not investigate an alarm. Exit, then investigate from outside.**

## Respiratory Protection

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| **Filtering respirator (P2, cartridge)** | **Never sufficient for oxygen deficiency.** A filter removes contaminants from air; it does not supply oxygen |
| **Supplied air respirator (airline)** | Air supplied from outside the space via a hose |
| **Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus (SCBA)** | Cylinder carried by the wearer. Required for rescue and for immediately dangerous atmospheres |

**The most dangerous misunderstanding in this area is that a dust mask or cartridge respirator offers protection in a low-oxygen space. It offers none.**

## Training and Competency

Confined space entry requires **specific competency** — this unit, plus the practical training and, in many workplaces, additional certification.

**Roles requiring competency:**
- Entrant
- Standby person
- Permit issuer
- Rescuer

**Nobody performs any of these roles without the relevant training.** Being told to stand at the top of a manhole and keep an eye on someone is being placed in a role with statutory responsibilities.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*Your crew is working on a stormwater upgrade. A blockage needs clearing from a pit approximately 3.5m deep, accessed through a 600mm opening.*

*Your leading hand has set up a ladder into the pit and asks you to climb down and clear the blockage by hand. He says he'll "stand up here and keep an eye on you."*

*There is no permit. Nobody has tested the atmosphere. There is no harness, tripod or winch on site. It has been raining intermittently upstream during the morning.*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**I do not enter that pit.** Nothing about this entry is safe and several of the missing controls are the ones that kill people.

---

**This is a confined space.**

A 3.5m deep stormwater pit with a 600mm opening meets every element of the definition: not designed for human occupancy, restricted entry and egress, and a genuine risk of atmospheric hazard and engulfment.

**That means a permit is legally required.** There is no permit, so the entry cannot lawfully occur regardless of everything else.

---

**What is specifically wrong, in order of how likely it is to kill me:**

**1. No atmospheric testing.**

Stormwater and sewer pits accumulate gas from decomposing organic matter. The two I would be most concerned about:

- **Hydrogen sulphide** — heavier than air, so it pools at the bottom of a 3.5m pit exactly where I would be working. It smells of rotten eggs at low concentration and then **deadens the sense of smell** at higher concentration. If I climbed down and noticed a smell that then went away, I would be more likely to interpret that as safe when in fact it is the opposite
- **Oxygen deficiency** — decomposition consumes oxygen, and CO₂ displaces it. **This gives no warning at all.** Impaired judgement begins before I would notice anything wrong, which is precisely why people in low-oxygen spaces do not climb out

A clear reading at the opening would tell me nothing about conditions at the base. **Gases stratify** — testing must be at multiple levels, lowering the probe from outside before anyone enters.

**2. Rain upstream — engulfment risk.**

This is the one specific to today and it is serious.

A stormwater system is a connected network. Rain falling kilometres upstream produces a surge that arrives at this pit **with no warning at the work site**. I would be at the bottom of a 3.5m pit, through a 600mm opening, with no harness and no mechanical retrieval.

**The upstream system has not been isolated.** For a live stormwater network, isolation may not even be achievable — which is a reason not to enter, not a reason to proceed and hope.

**3. No harness, tripod or winch — so there is no rescue capability.**

If I lost consciousness at the bottom of that pit, there would be no way to get me out.

A 600mm opening is too small for a rescuer to descend with breathing apparatus and manoeuvre an unconscious adult. Without a harness and mechanical retrieval, extraction requires someone to enter — and that is how the second fatality happens.

**Non-entry rescue is the required method for a space like this**, and it needs the harness, the tripod or davit, and the winch **in position before the first entry**, not sourced afterwards.

**4. "I'll keep an eye on you" is not a standby person.**

A standby person has statutory responsibilities: continuous communication, atmospheric monitoring, access control, and initiating a rescue procedure that actually exists. It requires training in the role.

**And critically — the standby person must never enter.** The most likely outcome of this arrangement is that if I collapsed, my leading hand would climb down to help me, and there would be two casualties instead of one. That is the single most common confined space fatality pattern in the world, and it is what this setup is arranged to produce.

**5. No isolation, no ventilation, no gas detector, no competency check.**

Nobody has established whether I am even trained for confined space entry.

---

**What I actually say:**

I would be direct, and I would not soften it into a question:

*"I'm not going down there. That's a confined space — it needs a permit, gas testing at multiple levels, isolation, a harness on a tripod, and a trained standby with a rescue plan. And with the rain upstream this morning there's a real surge risk. If I go down there and something happens, there's no way to get me out — and if you come down after me we've got two of us."*

**Then I would offer the alternative, because the blockage still needs clearing:**

*"Can we do it without entry? Vacuum truck, jetter, or long-handled tools from the surface? That's probably quicker than getting a permit and a full confined space setup organised anyway."*

**Elimination is the first control and it is the right answer here.** A blockage in a stormwater pit is very commonly cleared by a jetting or vacuum unit from the surface. That removes the entire hazard profile rather than managing it.

---

**If directed to enter anyway:**

I exercise the **right to cease unsafe work**. I have a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk, and that right is legally protected — I cannot be lawfully disadvantaged for exercising it.

I would notify the leading hand, escalate to the site supervisor and the principal contractor, and **document** the direction I was given, the time, and my response.

**I would also say it to the rest of the crew.** If I refuse and walk away, the next person asked may not know enough to refuse — and they are the one who will be at the bottom of the pit.

---

**The thing that makes this scenario dangerous:**

Nothing about it looks dramatic. It is a pit, a ladder, a blockage and a five-minute job. There is no visible hazard, no smell at the surface, and the leading hand is not being reckless in his own mind — he is solving a practical problem the way it has probably been solved on that site before.

**That is exactly the profile of a confined space fatality.** They do not happen during obviously dangerous work. They happen during routine tasks in spaces that looked fine from the top.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies the space against the definition and recognises that the absence of a permit alone prohibits entry. The hazard analysis is prioritised by lethality rather than listed, and the technical detail is accurate and specific: H₂S stratifying to the bottom of the pit where the work occurs, the smell-deadening characteristic being misread as safety, and oxygen deficiency impairing judgement before it is noticed. The upstream rain is correctly identified as a live engulfment risk with no warning at the work site — the detail most candidates overlook. The observation that a 600mm opening precludes an entry rescue with breathing apparatus is a practical insight that demonstrates genuine understanding of why non-entry rescue must be arranged in advance. Most importantly, the student names the double-fatality pattern explicitly and identifies that this specific setup is arranged to produce it. Elimination is proposed as the first control with a realistic method. Telling the rest of the crew, not just refusing personally, recognises that a refusal which leaves the hazard in place for the next person is incomplete. Strong Competent.
