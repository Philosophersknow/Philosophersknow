## What This Unit Is About

An automotive workshop concentrates an unusual number of serious hazards into a small space: multi-tonne vehicles suspended overhead, flammable fuels, pressurised systems, corrosive fluids, hot components, carbon monoxide, and — increasingly — high-voltage electrical systems capable of killing instantly.

AURAFA004 asks you to demonstrate that you understand these hazards and can work safely within the workshop's WHS system. In automotive work, the consequences of a shortcut are frequently immediate and severe.

---

## The Legal Framework

### Model WHS Act — Duties

**PCBU (the workshop owner/operator) must** ensure so far as reasonably practicable: safe plant and equipment, safe systems of work, adequate facilities, information, training, instruction and supervision, and consultation with workers.

**Workers must:**
- Take reasonable care for their own health and safety
- Take reasonable care not to adversely affect others
- Comply with reasonable instructions
- Cooperate with WHS policies and procedures

**Workers may** cease unsafe work where there is a reasonable concern of exposure to serious risk, and are protected from discriminatory conduct for doing so.

### Apprentice-Specific Considerations

Apprentices are workers with full WHS rights and protections. Additionally:
- Apprentices must be **supervised** appropriately to their level of competence
- An apprentice should not be directed to perform work beyond their demonstrated competence without supervision
- "Just have a go" is not adequate instruction for hazardous work
- An apprentice has the same right to refuse unsafe work as any other worker — and inexperience is a *reason* to ask, not a reason to stay silent

## Vehicle Lifting and Support — The Critical Hazard

Being crushed by a vehicle is a recurring cause of death in automotive workshops. The rules are absolute.

### Hoists (Vehicle Lifts)

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Training** | Only operate a hoist you have been trained and authorised to use |
| **Inspection** | Visually inspect before each use — arms, pads, locks, hydraulic lines, cables, controls |
| **Load rating** | Never exceed the hoist's rated capacity. Check the vehicle's weight, especially for EVs (battery packs make EVs substantially heavier than equivalent ICE vehicles) |
| **Lifting points** | Use manufacturer-specified lifting points only. Incorrect points can damage the vehicle, crush sills, rupture battery cases on EVs, or allow the vehicle to slip |
| **Balance** | Check the vehicle's centre of gravity. Removing a heavy component (engine, gearbox, battery pack) while raised changes the balance and can cause the vehicle to tip |
| **Safety locks** | Raise the vehicle, then **lower onto the mechanical safety locks**. Never work under a vehicle supported only by hydraulics |
| **Clear zone** | Ensure no person or obstruction is under or around the vehicle before raising or lowering |
| **Statutory inspection** | Hoists require periodic inspection by a competent person; records must be maintained |

### Jacks and Vehicle Stands

**A jack is a lifting device, not a supporting device.**

- Never work under a vehicle supported only by a jack — hydraulic seals fail, jacks slip, and ground can subside
- Always lower onto **rated vehicle support stands** after lifting
- Use stands in matched pairs at manufacturer-specified support points
- Chock the wheels remaining on the ground
- Apply the park brake and select park/gear
- Check stability by pushing the vehicle firmly before going underneath

### Working Under a Vehicle
- Never rely on a single point of support
- Never use bricks, blocks of wood, or improvised supports
- Ensure adequate lighting
- Keep the area clear so you can exit quickly
- Where practical, do not work alone under a vehicle

## Electrical Hazards

### 12V Systems
While 12V will not typically cause electrocution, the **current** available from a vehicle battery is very high. Risks include:
- **Arc flash and burns** — a spanner bridging a battery terminal to chassis can glow red hot in seconds and cause severe burns
- **Battery explosion** — lead-acid batteries emit hydrogen; a spark can ignite it, spraying sulphuric acid
- **Fire** — shorted wiring can ignite

**Controls:** Remove the **negative terminal first** and reconnect it **last** (so that if the spanner touches chassis while on the negative, there is no circuit). Remove rings and watches. Wear eye protection near batteries. Never lean over a battery while jump-starting or charging.

### High-Voltage Systems (Hybrid and Electric Vehicles)

**This is the most serious electrical hazard in a modern workshop.** EV and hybrid traction systems commonly operate at **300–800V DC**. This is lethal, and DC at these voltages causes sustained muscular contraction that can prevent release.

**Absolute rules:**
- **Do not work on HV systems without specific HV training and authorisation.** The base AUR30620 qualification does **not** confer HV competence — additional units and, in many workplaces, specific licensing or accreditation are required
- HV cabling and components are **orange** — orange means do not touch without authorisation
- HV systems must be formally **de-energised, isolated, and verified as isolated** using a rated tester before any work near HV components
- Isolation must be **locked out and tagged out (LOTO)** so it cannot be re-energised while you are working
- Use **insulated tools rated for the voltage** and appropriate HV PPE (Class 0 insulating gloves with leather overgloves, tested and in date)
- After isolation, allow the specified **capacitor discharge time** before touching HV components — inverters store dangerous energy after isolation
- Damaged EV batteries can undergo **thermal runaway** — a self-sustaining fire that reignites and cannot be extinguished by conventional means. Damaged HV batteries require specialist handling and quarantine

**[VERIFY: HV training requirements, competency units, and any state licensing requirements for HV automotive work — this area is evolving rapidly. Check with your state regulator and the current AUR training package.]**

## Chemical and Substance Hazards

### Safety Data Sheets (SDS)

Every hazardous chemical must have a current SDS, accessible to all workers. An SDS provides 16 standardised sections including hazard identification, composition, first aid, firefighting, handling and storage, exposure controls and PPE, and disposal.

**Your obligations:** Know where the SDS register is. Read the SDS before first using a chemical. Follow the PPE and handling requirements it specifies.

### Common Automotive Chemical Hazards

| Substance | Hazard | Controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Petrol** | Highly flammable; vapour is heavier than air and travels; carcinogenic (benzene) | No ignition sources; ventilation; bonding when transferring; nitrile gloves; never siphon by mouth |
| **Diesel** | Flammable; skin irritant and sensitiser | Gloves; skin protection; avoid prolonged contact |
| **Brake fluid** | Corrosive to skin and eyes; damages paint; hygroscopic | Gloves, eye protection; wash spills immediately |
| **Battery acid (sulphuric)** | Severely corrosive; causes burns and blindness | Face shield and chemical gloves; eyewash station accessible; neutralise spills with bicarbonate |
| **Coolant (ethylene glycol)** | Toxic if ingested; sweet taste attracts animals and children | Gloves; contain spills; correct disposal — never to drain |
| **Refrigerant (R134a, R1234yf)** | Asphyxiant in confined space; severe cold burns; R1234yf is mildly flammable | ARC licence required to handle; recovery equipment; gloves and eye protection |
| **Solvents / brake cleaner** | Respiratory irritant; some are neurotoxic; flammable | Ventilation; respirator where required; never use near hot work |
| **Used engine oil** | Carcinogenic on prolonged skin contact | Gloves; barrier cream; wash thoroughly; correct disposal |
| **Asbestos (older brake/clutch linings)** | Carcinogenic; causes mesothelioma and asbestosis | **Never dry-brush or blow out brake assemblies.** Assume pre-2004 friction materials may contain asbestos. Use wet methods or a H-class vacuum; licensed removal where required |

**[VERIFY: Asbestos in Australian vehicles — asbestos was banned nationally from 31 December 2003, but imported parts and older vehicles remain a risk. Check current Safe Work Australia guidance.]**

### Carbon Monoxide

Running an engine indoors produces **carbon monoxide** — colourless, odourless, and fatal. It binds to haemoglobin far more readily than oxygen, causing asphyxiation without warning.

**Controls:**
- Never run an engine indoors without **exhaust extraction connected**
- Ensure workshop ventilation is adequate and operating
- Be alert to symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion — get to fresh air immediately and report
- CO detectors in enclosed workshop areas

## Mechanical and Physical Hazards

### Hot Components
Exhaust systems, radiators, engine blocks, and turbochargers retain dangerous heat long after shutdown.

**Cooling systems are pressurised** — removing a radiator cap on a hot engine releases superheated coolant that flashes to steam, causing severe scalding to the face and arms. Allow the system to cool, and release pressure gradually using a cloth.

### Pressurised Systems
- **Air conditioning:** High pressure refrigerant; requires ARC licence and recovery equipment; never vent to atmosphere (illegal and environmentally damaging)
- **Fuel systems:** Modern direct injection systems operate at very high pressure; depressurise per manufacturer procedure before disconnecting
- **Hydraulics:** Assume pressurised; a pinhole leak can inject fluid through skin — a medical emergency requiring immediate surgical treatment
- **Tyres:** Inflation cages for split-rim and high-pressure applications; never stand over a tyre being inflated
- **Airbags (SRS):** Pyrotechnic devices. Follow manufacturer procedures for disabling; observe the specified waiting period after battery disconnection; handle and store deployed and undeployed units per procedure

### Rotating and Moving Parts
Fans (including electric fans that can start without warning after shutdown), belts, pulleys, and driveshafts. Keep hands, tools, loose clothing, jewellery, and long hair clear. Never reach into an engine bay with the engine running unless the task specifically requires it and you have identified all moving parts.

### Manual Handling
Wheels, batteries, gearboxes, cylinder heads, and EV battery modules are heavy and awkward. Use lifting equipment (engine crane, transmission jack, wheel dolly). Assess before lifting. Get assistance. Batteries in particular are heavy, awkward, and contain acid.

### Noise
Air tools, impact wrenches, grinders, and running engines routinely exceed 85 dB(A). Hearing protection required; hearing loss is permanent and cumulative.

## Workshop Housekeeping

Housekeeping in an automotive workshop is a direct safety control, not cosmetic:

- **Spills:** Oil, coolant, and fuel on the floor are slip hazards and, for fuel, fire hazards. Clean immediately using absorbent; dispose of contaminated absorbent correctly
- **Rags:** Oily rags can spontaneously combust. Store used rags in a **closed metal container**
- **Tools:** Return to the board or box. Tools left on a bench or in an engine bay get lost, cause damage, or fall
- **Leads and hoses:** Air lines and power leads across the floor are trip hazards. Route overhead or along walls
- **Waste segregation:** Used oil, coolant, filters, batteries, tyres, and refrigerant all have specific disposal requirements. None may go to general waste or to drain
- **Access:** Keep walkways, exits, and access to firefighting equipment and the switchboard clear at all times

## Fire Safety

Automotive workshops contain fuel, solvents, oils, oxygen and acetylene, and ignition sources.

### Fire Extinguisher Types

| Type | Colour Band | Use On | Do NOT Use On |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water** | Plain red | Wood, paper, fabric (Class A) | Electrical, flammable liquids |
| **Foam** | Blue band | Flammable liquids (Class B), Class A | Electrical |
| **Dry chemical powder (ABE)** | White band | Class A, B, E — the general workshop extinguisher | — (messy but broadly usable) |
| **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** | Black band | Electrical (Class E), flammable liquids | Confined spaces without ventilation (asphyxiation) |
| **Wet chemical** | Oatmeal band | Cooking oils and fats (Class F) | Electrical |

**EV battery fires** cannot be extinguished by conventional extinguishers. Lithium-ion thermal runaway is self-sustaining, produces its own oxygen, and reignites. The response is to evacuate, call emergency services, advise them it is an EV, and where possible apply large volumes of water for cooling from a safe distance — this is a fire service decision, not a workshop one.

### Hot Work
Welding, cutting, and grinding near fuel systems require a hot work procedure: remove or purge fuel sources, screen the area, have an extinguisher immediately available, and maintain a fire watch after work ceases.

## PPE in the Automotive Workshop

| PPE | When |
|---|---|
| **Safety footwear** | At all times in the workshop |
| **Eye protection** | Under vehicles, using compressed air, grinding, working near batteries, handling chemicals |
| **Face shield** | Battery servicing, high-pressure spray, grinding |
| **Gloves — nitrile** | Handling oils, fuels, solvents |
| **Gloves — chemical resistant** | Handling acids, strong solvents |
| **Gloves — insulating Class 0 (+ overgloves)** | HV work only, by authorised personnel, tested and in date |
| **Hearing protection** | Air tools, grinding, running engines, workshop noise above 85 dB(A) |
| **Respirator (P2 minimum)** | Brake dust (potential asbestos), spray, sanding, solvent vapour |
| **Overalls** | General workshop work; protects skin from oils and hot components |

**Do not wear** rings, watches, bracelets, neck chains, or loose clothing. Tie back long hair.

## Incident Reporting

Report all injuries (including first aid only), near misses, property damage, spills, and hazards.

**Notifiable incidents** — reported to the WHS regulator immediately — include death, serious injury requiring immediate hospital treatment, and **dangerous incidents** such as:
- Uncontrolled escape of a substance (fuel or refrigerant release)
- Uncontrolled explosion or fire
- **Electric shock**
- Fall or release from height of any plant or thing (a vehicle falling from a hoist)
- Damage to or malfunction of major plant

The incident scene must be preserved until the regulator permits disturbance, except to make safe or assist the injured.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are a second-year apprentice. A hybrid vehicle comes in with a fault the customer describes as "a warning light and it won't start." Your supervisor is on a road test. The workshop is busy. A third-year apprentice tells you to "get it up on the hoist and pull the inverter cover off so we can have a look — it's just a plastic cover, four bolts."*

*You notice the vehicle has orange cabling visible in the engine bay. You have not completed any high-voltage training.*

*What do you do, and why?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I do not remove the inverter cover. I stop and escalate.

---

**Why — the HV hazard:**

The orange cabling identifies this as a high-voltage system. Hybrid traction systems typically operate at **200–650V DC**. That is lethal. DC at these voltages causes sustained muscular contraction, which means a person in contact may be unable to let go.

The inverter is one of the most dangerous components in the vehicle. Beyond the traction voltage itself, inverters contain **DC link capacitors that store dangerous energy after the system is isolated**. Even after correct shutdown and isolation, the manufacturer specifies a mandatory discharge waiting period — commonly five to ten minutes — before the inverter may be touched. Removing the cover immediately after shutdown can expose a live, charged component.

The framing "it's just a plastic cover, four bolts" is exactly the reasoning that gets people killed. The mechanical difficulty of the task is irrelevant. The hazard is behind the cover, not in the bolts.

---

**Why I am not authorised:**

I have not completed HV training. Working on or near HV components without that training is:
- Outside my competence
- Contrary to my duty under the WHS Act to take reasonable care for my own safety and that of others
- Almost certainly contrary to my workshop's own procedures

The base light vehicle qualification does **not** confer HV competence. That requires specific additional training and workplace authorisation.

A third-year apprentice is not a supervisor and cannot authorise me to perform HV work. Neither could my supervisor, unless I held the training.

---

**What must happen before anyone works on this:**

An HV-authorised technician must:
1. Follow the manufacturer's shutdown procedure
2. Remove the **service disconnect / HV isolation plug**
3. Apply **lock-out/tag-out** so the system cannot be re-energised while work proceeds
4. **Verify isolation** with a tester rated for the voltage — testing the tester on a known live source before and after (the "live–dead–live" check), because a faulty tester reading zero on a live system is a fatal failure mode
5. Observe the manufacturer's **capacitor discharge waiting period**
6. Use insulated tools rated for the voltage and in-date Class 0 insulating gloves with leather overgloves
7. Establish an exclusion zone with signage so others do not approach

---

**What I actually do:**

**1. I decline, clearly and without hedging.**

*"I can't touch that one — it's a hybrid and I haven't done the HV training. That inverter needs someone who's HV authorised and it needs to be properly isolated first."*

I say it plainly. I do not need to be aggressive, and I do not need to apologise for it either.

**2. I do something useful instead.**

Declining is not the same as refusing to help. I can contribute to the diagnosis without going near HV:
- Read the fault codes with a scan tool
- Check the 12V auxiliary battery — a flat or failing 12V battery is an extremely common cause of "warning light and won't start" on hybrids, because the 12V system powers the control electronics that close the HV contactors. The fault may have nothing to do with the inverter at all
- Record the customer's description, warning lamps displayed, and service history
- Have the vehicle ready for the HV technician

That way, when my supervisor returns, they have information rather than an untouched car — and possibly a resolved fault.

**3. I wait for my supervisor and raise it with them.**

When my supervisor returns, I tell them what was asked and what I did. Not to get the third-year in trouble, but because:
- The vehicle still needs an HV-authorised technician
- If a third-year is directing apprentices to open inverter covers, that is a systemic issue the supervisor needs to know about — the next apprentice asked may not know to say no

**4. If I were pressured to proceed:**

I would exercise my **right to cease unsafe work**. I have a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk, and that right is legally protected — I cannot lawfully be disadvantaged for exercising it. I would escalate to the supervisor, the workshop manager, or the HSR.

---

**The point I would hold onto:**

Being an apprentice means I am *expected* not to know things yet. Asking, or saying "I'm not trained for that," is exactly what a second-year should do — it is not weakness and it is not being difficult. The genuinely dangerous apprentice is the one who does not want to look inexperienced and opens the cover anyway.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies orange cabling as the HV indicator, understands the specific inverter hazard including stored capacitor energy after isolation — a detail many qualified technicians overlook — and knows the full isolation sequence including LOTO and the live–dead–live verification check. The response correctly identifies that a third-year apprentice has no authority to direct HV work. Critically, the student does not simply refuse and stop: they identify genuinely useful non-HV diagnostic work, including the insight that a failing 12V auxiliary battery is a very common cause of this exact symptom on hybrids. The escalation is framed as a systemic safety concern rather than as reporting a colleague. The closing reflection on apprentice culture demonstrates exactly the professional judgment this unit exists to develop. Strong Competent.
