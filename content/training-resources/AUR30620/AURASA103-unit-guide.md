## What This Unit Is About

A safety inspection determines whether a vehicle is fit to be on a public road with other people around it.

This is one of the few things a mechanic does where the consequence of a poor job falls primarily on **strangers** — the other drivers, passengers, cyclists and pedestrians who share the road with a vehicle you passed.

AURASA103 asks you to inspect systematically against defined criteria, measure rather than estimate, and report accurately.

---

## The Regulatory Context

### What a Safety Inspection Is

Australian states and territories operate roadworthy or vehicle inspection schemes under different names — **Roadworthy Certificate (VIC)**, **Safety Certificate (QLD)**, **eSafety / Pink Slip (NSW)**, and equivalents elsewhere. Requirements, triggers, and validity periods differ.

**Certification requires authorisation.** Being qualified as a mechanic does not automatically authorise you to issue a roadworthy or safety certificate — that requires separate approval as an authorised inspector or an approved inspection station, with its own application, training and audit requirements.

**[VERIFY: Inspection scheme names, criteria, authorisation requirements and validity periods vary substantially by state and are periodically amended. Always work from your state road authority's current inspection manual — it is the governing document, not a general guide such as this one.]**

### Your Position as a Non-Authorised Technician

At Certificate III level you will commonly:
- **Conduct inspections** as part of a service, identifying defects for a customer
- **Assist** an authorised inspector
- **Not sign or issue** a certificate unless you personally hold that authorisation

**Never sign an inspection you did not perform, and never allow your inspection to be certified by someone who did not check your work.** Certificate fraud is a serious offence, carries personal liability, and has been prosecuted.

## Inspection Principles

### Systematic, Not Selective

**Follow the same sequence every time.** An inspection done in a consistent order does not skip items; an inspection done by wandering around the vehicle does.

A typical sequence:
1. Documentation and identification
2. Exterior — body, glass, lights, tyres
3. Interior — seats, belts, controls, warning lamps
4. Under bonnet
5. Under vehicle (raised)
6. Brake and road test

### Measure, Do Not Estimate

The defining discipline of this unit. **"It looks about right" is not an inspection.**

- Tyre tread — measure with a depth gauge
- Brake pad and lining thickness — measure
- Disc thickness — measure with a micrometer at several points
- Disc runout — measure with a DTI
- Play in steering and suspension — assess against specification

### Compare Against the Standard

Every measurement is compared against either the **manufacturer's service limit** or the **regulatory minimum** — whichever is more stringent. A component can be above the legal minimum and still below the manufacturer's service limit.

### Record What You Find

Record measurements, not just outcomes. "Front pads 3mm" is useful information for the customer and evidence of what you did. "Brakes OK" is neither.

## The Inspection

### 1. Identification and Documentation

- **VIN** matches the registration papers
- Engine number where required
- Compliance plate present and legible
- Check for evidence of tampering with identifiers
- Note odometer reading
- Note any **modifications** — modified vehicles may require engineering certification

### 2. Tyres and Wheels

**Tread depth:**

The minimum legal tread depth across Australia is **1.5mm**, measured across the tread width in the principal grooves.

**Measure at multiple points** — inner, centre and outer, and at several points around the circumference. A tyre can be legal in the centre and bald on one edge.

**Tread wear indicators (TWI)** are moulded into the grooves at 1.5mm. When the tread is level with the indicator, the tyre is at the limit.

**Also check:**

| Item | Reject if |
|---|---|
| **Sidewall** | Cuts, bulges, cracking, exposed cord |
| **Tread** | Uneven wear, exposed cord or belt, penetrations, repairs outside the repairable area |
| **Age** | Perishing and cracking; check the DOT date code — tyres degrade with age regardless of tread |
| **Matching** | Different sizes, construction types, or tread patterns across an axle |
| **Speed and load rating** | Below the vehicle's requirement |
| **Pressure** | Set to placard pressure |
| **Wheels** | Cracks, buckles, damaged stud holes, incorrect offset, missing or damaged nuts |

**Wear patterns are diagnostic:**

| Pattern | Cause |
|---|---|
| Both edges worn | Under-inflation |
| Centre worn | Over-inflation |
| One edge worn | Camber misalignment |
| Feathering across the tread | Toe misalignment |
| Cupping / scalloping | Worn suspension components |
| Flat spots | Locked braking or a stationary period |

**[VERIFY: Minimum tread depth and tyre requirements against your state's current vehicle standards. 1.5mm is the general Australian minimum but requirements for repairs, matching and age should be confirmed.]**

### 3. Brakes

**Friction material:**
- Measure **pad and lining thickness** and compare against the service limit
- Check for uneven wear between inboard and outboard pads — indicates a **seized caliper slide pin**
- Check for contamination — oil or brake fluid on friction material requires replacement, not cleaning

**Discs:**
- **Thickness** — measure with a micrometer at **several points around the disc**, since the fault of interest is thickness *variation*. Compare against the minimum thickness, which is usually cast or stamped on the disc
- **Runout** — measure with a DTI
- Check for scoring, heat cracking, blueing (indicating overheating), and lip formation

**Drums:**
- Internal diameter measured against the maximum
- Check for scoring, ovality and cracking

**Hydraulics:**
- Fluid level and condition — dark or contaminated fluid indicates it needs replacement. Brake fluid is **hygroscopic** (absorbs moisture), which lowers its boiling point and can cause brake fade
- **Flexible hoses** — cracking, perishing, bulging, chafing, twisting. A perished hose can act as a one-way valve, causing a brake to drag or a car to pull
- Rigid lines — corrosion, damage, secure mounting
- **Leaks** — any leak is a fail
- Master cylinder and caliper condition

**Operation:**
- Pedal feel — spongy suggests air or a failing hose; hard suggests a booster fault
- Pedal travel and reserve
- **Park brake** — travel, holding ability, cable condition and adjustment
- Brake performance on a road test or brake tester — balance across axles and stopping performance

### 4. Steering and Suspension

- **Free play** at the steering wheel measured against specification
- **Ball joints and tie rod ends** — check for play; many have a specified maximum movement
- Rack or box condition, boots intact, no leaks
- **Bushes** — perished, split, or excessively worn
- **Shock absorbers / struts** — leaking, damaged, or ineffective. Leaking dampers are a fail
- **Springs** — broken, sagging, corroded
- Wheel bearings — play and noise
- CV boots — split boots admit dirt and destroy the joint
- Vehicle sits level, correct ride height

### 5. Lights and Electrical

| Item | Check |
|---|---|
| **Headlights** | Both beams operate, correct colour, **aim within specification**, lenses not clouded or cracked |
| **Tail and stop lights** | Operate, correct colour and intensity |
| **Indicators** | Operate at the correct flash rate, front and rear, both sides |
| **Reverse lights** | Operate |
| **Number plate light** | Operates |
| **Hazard lights** | Operate |
| **Horn** | Operates and is audible |
| **Wipers and washers** | Effective clearing, blades not perished, washers function |
| **Warning lamps** | Bulb check on ignition, then extinguish. **An illuminated airbag, ABS or brake warning lamp is a defect** |
| **Wiring** | Secure, insulated, no chafing or exposed conductors |

**Headlight aim matters more than most people assume.** Misaimed headlights either fail to illuminate the road or dazzle oncoming drivers.

### 6. Body, Glass and Restraints

**Structure:**
- Corrosion — **structural corrosion is a fail**; surface corrosion is generally not. The distinction is whether it affects structural integrity, mounting points, or occupant protection
- Evidence of accident damage or poor repair
- Doors, bonnet and boot latch securely
- Mudguards and body panels — no sharp edges, adequate tyre coverage
- Exhaust — secure, no leaks, no excessive noise

**Glass:**
- **Windscreen** — cracks and chips within the driver's primary vision area are generally a fail; requirements vary by location and size on the screen
- Windows operate; tinting within legal limits
- Mirrors — present, secure, not cracked, adequate field of view

**Seats and restraints:**
- Seats secure to the floor, adjusters and locks function
- **Seatbelts** — webbing not frayed, cut, faded or contaminated; buckles latch and release; **retractors retract and inertia reels lock when pulled sharply**; anchorages secure and not corroded
- Airbag warning lamp behaviour correct
- Child restraint anchorage points present and sound

**Seatbelt inspection is frequently rushed and it should not be.** A belt that looks fine but has a failed retractor or a corroded anchorage will not restrain an occupant in a crash.

### 7. Under Bonnet and Under Vehicle

- **Fluid leaks** — engine oil, coolant, transmission, brake, power steering, fuel. Identify the source; fluid tracks downward from where it originates
- **Fuel system** — no leaks, lines secure and not chafing, filler cap seals
- Engine and transmission mounts
- Drive shafts, universal joints, CV joints
- **Exhaust system** — leaks are a carbon monoxide risk to occupants, not just a noise issue
- Battery secure, terminals sound, no leakage
- Belts and hoses
- Chassis and subframe corrosion

## Reporting

### The Report Must Be Accurate

Record:
- Every item inspected
- **Measurements taken**, with values
- Items that pass
- Items that fail, with the reason and the measured value
- Items that are serviceable now but approaching a limit — **advisories**

**Advisories matter.** A pad at 4mm passes, but the customer benefits enormously from knowing it will need replacing within a few months. That is service, and it is also honest.

### Talking to the Customer

- Explain findings in **plain language**
- **Distinguish safety-critical from advisory.** A customer who cannot tell which items are urgent may defer the wrong one
- Show them where practicable — a customer who sees a split CV boot understands it
- Provide the measurement and the limit: "the pads are at 2mm and the minimum is 3mm" is far more credible than "your brakes need doing"
- **Do not oversell.** Recommending unnecessary work is dishonest, damages trust, and in some circumstances breaches the Australian Consumer Law

### Failing a Vehicle

If a vehicle fails, the report states **what failed and why**. Where a defect makes the vehicle unsafe to drive, **advise the customer clearly** and record that you did so.

## The Ethical Dimension

This unit carries genuine ethical weight, and it is commonly assessed.

**Two failure modes, both serious:**

**1. Passing a vehicle that should fail.** Because the customer cannot afford the repair, because they are a friend, because it is nearly closing time, or because they applied pressure. The consequence is a defective vehicle on a public road, and it can kill people who had no part in the decision.

**2. Failing items that do not warrant it, or recommending unnecessary work.** This is fraud against the customer and it is illegal under consumer law.

**The measurement is the defence against both.** A recorded value compared against a published limit is objective. It removes the argument, and it protects you.

**If pressured to pass a defective vehicle:** decline, record the defect, and escalate. Your authorisation, your employment, and potentially your liberty are attached to that decision — and so is somebody else's safety.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are conducting a safety inspection on a customer's vehicle. Your findings include:*

*- Front brake pads: 2mm (manufacturer minimum 3mm)*
*- Front discs: 21.6mm at the thinnest point (minimum stamped on disc 22.0mm)*
*- Right front tyre: 1.4mm tread on the inner edge, 4mm centre and outer*
*- Left front CV boot: split, grease visible on the surrounding components*
*- All lights, seatbelts and steering within specification*

*The customer arrives and tells you she is a single parent, money is very tight, she needs the car for work tomorrow, and asks whether you can "just pass it this once and I'll get it all done next month."*

*What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I cannot pass this vehicle. I would tell her that clearly and without hedging, and then I would spend my effort on helping her find a way forward — because her problem is real even though the answer to her question is no.

---

**Why it fails — the measurements:**

| Item | Measured | Limit | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Front pads | 2mm | 3mm | **FAIL** — below minimum |
| Front discs | 21.6mm | 22.0mm | **FAIL** — below minimum thickness |
| RF tyre inner | 1.4mm | 1.5mm | **FAIL** — below legal minimum |
| LF CV boot | Split | — | **FAIL** — joint contaminated |

**Four failures, three of which are directly safety-critical.**

---

**Taking them in order of seriousness:**

**Brakes — pads *and* discs both under minimum.**

The pads at 2mm are below the 3mm service limit, and the discs at 21.6mm are below the minimum thickness stamped on the disc itself.

**The discs are the more serious finding.** A disc below minimum thickness has reduced heat capacity, and brakes work by converting kinetic energy into heat. A disc that cannot absorb and dissipate that heat is prone to **brake fade** under sustained or heavy braking — exactly the situation where the brakes matter most, such as a long descent or an emergency stop.

An under-thickness disc is also more likely to crack and to distort.

**This cannot be fixed by replacing pads alone.** New pads on under-minimum discs is a repair that fails immediately and is arguably worse, because it gives the appearance of a brake service.

**Tyre — 1.4mm on the inner edge.**

Below the 1.5mm legal minimum, so it fails on measurement alone.

But the more important finding is **why**: 1.4mm on the inner edge against 4mm centre and outer is a pronounced inner-edge wear pattern, which indicates a **camber or alignment fault**. That means:
- A new tyre fitted without correcting the alignment will wear out the same way — she would be paying twice
- There may be a worn suspension component causing the misalignment, which is itself an inspection item worth investigating

I would flag this specifically, because it changes what she should spend money on.

**CV boot — split with grease thrown.**

A split boot has been admitting dirt and water into the joint and throwing out grease. The joint is now contaminated and its life is limited.

**This is the least immediately dangerous item but the most financially urgent.** A boot replaced early is a relatively cheap job. A boot left until the joint fails means replacing the whole CV joint or driveshaft, at several times the cost. **Delaying this one specifically costs her more money.**

---

**Why I will not pass it "just this once":**

**I would be signing a false document.** An inspection certificate is a legal statement that the vehicle met the standard at the time of inspection. It did not. Issuing one is fraud, it carries personal liability, and it can result in loss of authorisation and prosecution.

**The consequence does not fall only on her.** If those brakes fade or that tyre fails, the people at risk include her children in the car, and other road users who had no involvement in this conversation and no opportunity to consent to the risk.

**"Next month" is an unenforceable promise made under financial pressure.** She may genuinely intend it. Whether she can do it depends on circumstances she does not control. Meanwhile the vehicle is on the road.

**And practically:** the brakes are the item most likely to be needed before next month, because emergencies do not schedule themselves.

---

**What I actually say to her:**

I would not be cold about it, and I would not lecture her.

*"I'm really sorry — I can't pass it. The brakes are the problem: the pads and the discs are both under the minimum, and the tyre is under the legal tread on the inner edge. I know that's the last thing you need to hear right now.*

*Can I show you what I've found and talk through what's actually urgent and what can wait? Not everything on this list has to happen this week."*

**Showing her matters.** A customer who sees a 2mm pad next to a new one, and the bald strip on the inner edge of the tyre, understands the finding. It also makes clear I am not inventing work.

---

**Then I help her triage it — this is the part that actually assists her:**

**Must be done before the car is driven:**
- **Front pads and discs.** Both, together. This is the non-negotiable item
- **Right front tyre.** Below legal tread

**Should be done as soon as possible:**
- **Wheel alignment**, at the same time as the tyre — otherwise she buys another tyre in a few months
- **CV boot.** Cheap now, expensive later. **This is the item where delay costs her the most money**

**Practical options I would raise with the service manager or workshop owner:**
- Are there **budget-tier pads and discs** that meet specification? Quality varies but there are compliant options at different price points
- Can the tyre be sourced as a **quality used tyre** with adequate tread, if that is acceptable and legal in our state and matches the other side?
- Does the workshop offer **payment arrangements**, or do we work with a finance option?
- Can we **prioritise into two visits** — brakes and tyre now, boot and alignment in a few weeks — with a clear written explanation of what remains outstanding?

**And the point I would make gently but clearly:** the vehicle is not safe to drive as it is. I would advise her not to drive it, record that I gave that advice, and note it on the report.

---

**What I document:**

- Every measurement with its value and the applicable limit
- The fail result for each item and the reason
- The inner-edge wear pattern and the alignment recommendation
- That I advised the customer the vehicle was unsafe to drive, and when
- The advisory items

**The measurements are what protect everyone here.** They are objective, they are recorded, and they are the reason this is not a matter of opinion or negotiation. If she seeks a second opinion, another inspector will measure the same values.

---

**The judgement:**

The pressure in this scenario is sympathy, not commerce, and that makes it harder to resist than a demanding customer would be. Her situation is genuinely difficult and she has not done anything wrong.

But **the brakes do not know she is having a hard month.** The most useful thing I can do for her is be straight about what is wrong, help her spend her limited money in the right order, and not put her and her kids in a car with brakes that cannot stop it.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student refuses clearly while treating the customer's circumstances with genuine respect — the tone is exactly right for a scenario designed to test whether sympathy erodes judgement. Each failure is assessed on its measured value against a stated limit, and the ranking by seriousness is correct. The disc analysis is the strongest technical element: identifying reduced heat capacity and consequent brake fade risk, and noting that fitting pads alone would be a repair that fails immediately, demonstrates understanding well beyond checking a number against a limit. Recognising the inner-edge tyre wear as an alignment symptom — and that replacing the tyre without correcting it means paying twice — is practical advice that genuinely serves the customer's financial position. Identifying the CV boot as the item where delay costs the most money reframes the triage in terms she can act on. The reasoning for refusal correctly identifies certificate fraud, personal liability, and critically that the risk extends to her children and to uninvolved road users who cannot consent. The triage into urgent versus deferrable, with concrete cost-reduction options, converts a refusal into assistance. Documentation of the unsafe-to-drive advice is correctly included. The closing line lands the principle without moralising. Strong Competent.
