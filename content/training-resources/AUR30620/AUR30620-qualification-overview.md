## What This Qualification Is

The Certificate III in Light Vehicle Mechanical Technology is the trade qualification for motor mechanics (light vehicles — cars, utilities, light commercial) in Australia. It is completed as an apprenticeship combining workshop-based learning with structured training at a TAFE or RTO.

Motor mechanics diagnose, service, and repair petrol, diesel, and increasingly hybrid and electric vehicles. The qualification covers the full range of vehicle systems — engine, transmission, braking, suspension, electrical, and air conditioning.

## The Automotive Industry

### Industry Scale
The Australian automotive service and repair industry comprises:
- **New car dealerships:** Franchise workshops servicing specific brands
- **Independent workshops:** General repairs and servicing across multiple makes
- **Specialist workshops:** Transmission, brake, clutch, exhaust, air conditioning, tyres
- **Fleet maintenance:** Corporate, government, and utility vehicle fleets
- **Mobile mechanics:** Field-based service and repair

### The Transition to Electric Vehicles
The automotive industry is undergoing rapid change. Electric vehicle (EV) sales are growing significantly, and technicians working on EVs require additional qualifications and competencies — particularly regarding high-voltage safety. The standard light vehicle qualification does not fully cover EV-specific work; additional units or qualifications are required.

### Industry Licensing
Motor mechanics in Australia do not require a trade licence in most states (unlike electricians and plumbers). However:
- **Motor vehicle repairers licence:** Required in NSW and some other states for businesses conducting vehicle repairs
- **Air conditioning:** Handling refrigerants requires an ARC (Australian Refrigeration Council) licence — the AUR30620 does not automatically confer this
- **Roadworthy/safety inspections:** In most states, certifying a vehicle as roadworthy requires specific authorisation beyond the trade qualification

## WHS in Automotive

Automotive workshops are high-risk environments:

| Hazard | Control |
|---|---|
| **Vehicle weight** | Never work under a vehicle supported only by a jack; always use safety stands |
| **Compressed gases (refrigerants, fuel)** | Proper handling procedures; ARC licence for refrigerants; no smoking near fuel systems |
| **Hydraulic systems** | Release pressure before disconnecting; assume lines are pressurised |
| **Hot components** | Allow vehicles to cool; use appropriate gloves; caution with cooling system caps under pressure |
| **Electrical — High Voltage (HV)** | EVs/hybrids contain HV systems (up to 400V or higher); must be de-energised before any HV work; requires specific HV safety training |
| **Battery acid** | Eye protection and gloves when handling lead-acid batteries; never short circuit |
| **Exhaust gases** | Adequate ventilation; carbon monoxide is odourless and fatal; engine running only with exhaust extraction |
| **Chemical hazards** | Brake fluid, degreasers, paints — appropriate PPE; SAFETY DATA SHEETS (SDS) must be available |

### Safety Data Sheets (SDS)
Every hazardous chemical in the workplace must have a Safety Data Sheet — a standardised document providing:
- Identification of the substance
- Hazards
- Handling and storage requirements
- PPE requirements
- First aid and emergency procedures
- Disposal requirements

SDS must be accessible to all workers who may be exposed to the substance.

## Automotive Technical Foundations

### The Diagnostic Process
Modern vehicle diagnosis uses a systematic approach:

1. **Gather information:** Customer complaint (exact symptom, when it occurs, conditions); service history; warning lights
2. **Verify the concern:** Reproduce the fault — do not assume the description is complete
3. **Research:** Manufacturer technical service bulletins (TSBs), wiring diagrams, specifications
4. **Test and diagnose:** Using scan tools, multimeter, oscilloscope as appropriate
5. **Determine the cause:** Root cause — not just the symptom
6. **Repair:** Replace/repair as diagnosed
7. **Verify the repair:** Confirm the original concern is resolved; check for related codes or conditions
8. **Document:** Record the complaint, cause, and correction

### OBD-II and Diagnostic Trouble Codes
Modern vehicles (post-1996 globally) use OBD-II (On-Board Diagnostics) systems that generate fault codes (Diagnostic Trouble Codes — DTCs) when a monitored system operates outside its parameters.

- **P codes (Powertrain):** Engine, transmission
- **B codes (Body):** Airbags, climate control, accessories
- **C codes (Chassis):** ABS, steering, suspension
- **U codes (Network):** Communication faults between modules

**Reading a DTC is the starting point of diagnosis, not the end.** A DTC indicates which system has flagged a fault — it does not necessarily identify the faulty component. Many inexperienced technicians replace the component mentioned in the code description without diagnosing the underlying cause, leading to repeat failures and customer dissatisfaction.
