## What This Unit Is About

A mechanic's tools are the interface between knowledge and the vehicle. Using the wrong tool, or the right tool incorrectly, produces rounded fasteners, damaged components, incorrect torque, and injuries.

AURAFA003 asks you to select the correct tool, use it safely and correctly, maintain it, and recognise when it is unserviceable.

## Hand Tools

### Spanners and Sockets

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| **Open-end spanner** | Access where a ring cannot fit; **grips only two flats — highest risk of rounding** |
| **Ring spanner** | Encircles the fastener; far better grip; preferred where access allows |
| **Combination spanner** | Open one end, ring the other |
| **Socket — 6 point** | Grips the **flats** of the fastener; **strongest grip, use on tight or corroded fasteners** |
| **Socket — 12 point** | Grips the **corners**; more engagement positions, but more likely to round a tight fastener |
| **Deep socket** | Reaches over protruding studs |
| **Impact socket** | Thicker wall, **chrome-molybdenum**, black finish; for impact wrenches |
| **Flare nut / line spanner** | Split ring for brake and fuel lines — grips more flats than an open-end without needing to slide over the line |
| **Torque wrench** | Applying a specified torque |

**Critical: never use a chrome socket on an impact wrench.** Chrome sockets are hardened for hand use and are brittle under impact. They shatter, sending fragments at high velocity. Impact sockets are made of chrome-molybdenum, which deforms rather than shattering.

**Six point versus twelve point:** a 12 point socket applies load to the corners of the fastener. On a tight or corroded fastener the corners deform and the socket slips, rounding the head. **Always reach for a 6 point on anything likely to be tight.**

**Correct technique:**
- **Pull a spanner toward you** rather than pushing — if it slips you fall away from the obstruction, not into it with your knuckles
- Ensure the tool is **fully seated** before applying force
- Use the correct size — a near-fit imperial socket on a metric fastener is how fasteners get rounded
- **Do not extend a spanner with a pipe** ("cheater bar") unless the tool is rated for it. It applies unknown torque and can break the tool

### Screwdrivers, Pliers, Punches

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Screwdrivers** | Match tip **type and size** — Phillips and Pozidriv are different; a loose fit cams out and destroys the head |
| **Pliers — combination** | General gripping; **not a substitute for a spanner** |
| **Pliers — long nose** | Access in confined spaces |
| **Circlip pliers** | Internal or external circlips; **wear eye protection — circlips fly** |
| **Multigrips / vice grips** | Last resort on damaged fasteners; they damage what they grip |
| **Punches and drifts** | Aligning holes, driving pins; use a drift, not a screwdriver |
| **Cold chisel** | Cutting and splitting seized fasteners |

**Mushroomed heads on punches and chisels must be dressed off.** A mushroomed head can shed metal fragments when struck, which is a serious eye injury risk.

### Hammers

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| **Ball pein** | General engineering; the standard workshop hammer |
| **Soft-faced (nylon, hide, copper)** | Striking finished surfaces without damage |
| **Dead blow** | Shot-filled; minimises rebound |
| **Sledge** | Heavy work |

**Never strike hardened steel with a hardened steel hammer** — both can chip. Use a soft-faced hammer or a brass drift.

**Check the head is secure before use.** A loose hammer head is a projectile.

## Torque

### Why It Matters

**Every threaded fastener on a modern vehicle has a specified torque.** Torque is not a matter of feel or preference.

**Under-torqued:** the joint loosens, the fastener fatigues and fails, gaskets leak, wheels come off.

**Over-torqued:** the fastener stretches beyond its elastic limit and yields, threads strip, components distort, brake discs warp, and alloy castings crack.

**Torque specifications come from the manufacturer's service data**, not from a general chart and not from experience.

### Torque Wrench Types

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Click type** | Most common; clicks at the set value |
| **Beam type** | Simple, robust, no calibration drift; harder to read in situ |
| **Digital** | Reads directly; often with angle measurement |

### Using a Torque Wrench Correctly

- **Set the value before use**, and confirm the units — **Nm and ft-lb are not interchangeable**
- Apply force **smoothly and slowly**, not in jerks
- **Stop at the click.** Continuing past it over-torques the fastener
- Pull at right angles to the wrench, with the hand on the handle grip — hand position affects the effective length and therefore the applied torque
- **Wind a click-type wrench back to its lowest setting after use.** Leaving it under spring tension causes calibration drift
- **Never use a torque wrench to loosen fasteners** — it damages the mechanism
- **Never use an extension bar** on the handle
- **Torque wrenches require periodic calibration.** An uncalibrated wrench reads plausibly and is wrong

### Torque-to-Yield Fasteners

Some fasteners — commonly cylinder head bolts — are designed to be tightened **into their yield range** to achieve consistent clamping load. They are tightened to an initial torque and then a specified **angle** (torque-angle method).

**Torque-to-yield fasteners are single use.** They have permanently stretched and must be replaced, not reused. Reusing them results in inadequate clamping force and head gasket failure.

**[VERIFY: Fastener reuse policy against the manufacturer's specific service data. Practice varies by engine.]**

### Thread Care

- **Clean threads before assembly.** Dirt and corrosion in a thread produce a false torque reading — the wrench clicks on friction rather than clamping load
- Chase damaged threads with a **thread chaser** rather than a tap where possible; a tap cuts new metal, a chaser cleans the existing thread
- **Do not lubricate threads unless specified** — oil on a dry-spec thread substantially increases the actual clamping load for the same torque reading, and can cause failure
- Apply thread locking compound only where specified

## Measuring Equipment

Accurate measurement determines whether a component is serviceable.

| Instrument | Measures | Typical resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Steel rule** | Basic linear | 0.5mm |
| **Vernier caliper** | External, internal, depth | 0.02mm |
| **Digital caliper** | As above, direct reading | 0.01mm |
| **Micrometer (external)** | Precise external dimensions — journals, discs | 0.01mm |
| **Bore gauge / telescopic gauge** | Internal bores | 0.01mm |
| **Dial indicator (DTI)** | Runout, endfloat, lift | 0.01mm |
| **Feeler gauges** | Clearances — valve, spark plug | Various |
| **Straight edge** | Flatness of heads and blocks, used with feeler gauges | — |
| **Plastigauge** | Bearing clearance | — |

**Using measuring equipment correctly:**
- **Zero the instrument before use** and check it against a known standard
- **Clean both the instrument and the component** — a fleck of dirt is larger than the tolerance being measured
- Take **multiple readings** at different points and orientations. A brake disc measured at one point tells you nothing about thickness variation; a journal measured on one axis tells you nothing about ovality
- Do not over-tighten a micrometer — use the **ratchet or friction thimble** for consistent pressure
- Store micrometers with the anvils **slightly apart** and in a case, away from temperature extremes

**Compare every measurement against the manufacturer's specification** — including the **service limit**, which is the point at which a worn component must be replaced.

## Workshop Equipment

### Vehicle Hoists
See AURAFA004. Key points: trained operators only, pre-use inspection, correct manufacturer lifting points, load rating checked (EVs are heavy), and **always lower onto the mechanical safety locks**.

### Jacks and Stands
**A jack lifts; a stand supports.** Never work under a vehicle on a jack alone. Rated stands in matched pairs at the manufacturer's support points, wheels chocked, park brake on, stability checked by pushing firmly before going underneath.

### Presses
Hydraulic presses store enormous energy and components can **eject violently**.
- Support the work correctly and squarely
- **Never stand directly in line with the work**
- Use a press guard where fitted
- Full face protection
- Increase pressure gradually; if something is not moving, reassess rather than adding force

### Compressed Air
- **Never direct compressed air at yourself or another person.** Air injected under the skin causes an embolism, which can be fatal
- Never use compressed air to clean clothing or skin
- Check hoses and couplings before use; a whipping hose causes serious injury
- Drain the receiver as scheduled — water accumulation corrodes the tank
- **Do not use compressed air to blow out brake assemblies** — see asbestos, below

### Impact Wrenches
- **Impact sockets only**
- Ensure the socket is fully engaged before triggering
- Not suitable for final torque — impact wrenches do not apply a controlled torque. **Final tightening is always by torque wrench**
- A common bad practice is running wheel nuts up with an impact and calling it done. Wheel nuts must be torqued

### Welding and Cutting
- Requires specific competency
- Screens to protect others from arc flash
- Correct PPE — helmet with the appropriate shade, gloves, leathers, respiratory protection
- **Fire watch** — hot work near fuel systems requires the fuel source removed or purged, screening, and an extinguisher immediately available
- **Disconnect the battery and, where required, the ECU** before welding on a vehicle; welding current can destroy electronic modules
- Ventilation — welding fume is hazardous

### Battery Chargers and Jump Starting
- Ventilate — lead-acid batteries emit **hydrogen**, which is explosive
- Connect **positive first, then the negative to a chassis earth point away from the battery** — so any spark occurs away from the hydrogen
- Charger off before connecting or disconnecting
- Never lean over a battery being charged or jump started
- Eye protection

## Diagnostic Equipment

| Equipment | Care |
|---|---|
| **Scan tool** | Keep software updated; handle connectors carefully; do not disconnect mid-programming |
| **Multimeter** | Correct range and function before connecting; **current measurement requires the leads in the correct sockets** — measuring voltage with the leads in the amps socket creates a short circuit |
| **Oscilloscope** | Protect leads and probes; correct voltage range |
| **Pressure gauges** | Check for damage; do not exceed rated pressure |

**A miscalibrated or damaged diagnostic tool produces plausible wrong readings**, which is worse than no reading at all — it sends the diagnosis down the wrong path with confidence.

## Hazardous Materials in Tool Use

### Asbestos
Friction materials in older vehicles — brake linings, clutch facings, some gaskets — may contain asbestos.

- **Never dry-brush or blow out a brake assembly with compressed air.** This aerosolises asbestos fibres into the workshop air, exposing everyone present
- Use **wet methods** or an **H-class vacuum** designed for asbestos
- Assume pre-2004 friction materials may contain asbestos unless confirmed otherwise

**[VERIFY: Asbestos was banned nationally in Australia from 31 December 2003, but imported components and older vehicles remain a risk. Confirm current Safe Work Australia guidance and your state requirements for handling asbestos-containing friction materials.]**

### Silica and Abrasives
Grinding and abrasive blasting generate hazardous dust. Extraction, screening, and respiratory protection required.

### Solvents and Parts Washers
Ventilation, gloves, eye protection. Many degreasers are skin sensitisers and some are neurotoxic with chronic exposure.

## Tool Maintenance

### Daily
- Clean tools after use — oil and grease attract dirt and make tools slip in the hand
- Return tools to the box or board; tools left in an engine bay get lost or fall into moving parts
- Wipe down and inspect

### Regular
- **Inspect for damage:** cracked sockets, rounded spanner jaws, mushroomed punches, split hammer handles, frayed power leads
- **Lubricate** ratchet mechanisms and moving parts
- **Calibrate** torque wrenches and measuring instruments at the required interval
- **Test and tag** portable electrical equipment
- Keep cutting tools sharp

### Removing Tools from Service
A damaged tool must be **tagged and removed**, not "used carefully":
- Cracked or damaged sockets and spanners
- Rounded or worn drive squares on ratchets
- Damaged electrical leads or plugs
- Torque wrenches that have been dropped — **a dropped torque wrench must be recalibrated before use**
- Measuring instruments that have been dropped
- Lifting equipment past its inspection date

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are fitting a wheel after a brake service. The vehicle specification calls for wheel nuts torqued to 110 Nm. Your torque wrench reads in ft-lb only and is currently set at 110. Another apprentice says "just do it up with the rattle gun, that's what everyone does, then give it a nip with the bar."*

*Identify the problems and describe the correct procedure.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

There are two separate problems here and either one on its own could put a wheel off a vehicle.

---

**Problem 1 — The units are wrong. This is the dangerous one.**

The specification is **110 Nm**. The wrench reads in **ft-lb**. Setting the wrench to "110" does not set 110 Nm — it sets **110 ft-lb**.

**Conversion:** 1 ft-lb ≈ 1.356 Nm

110 ft-lb × 1.356 = **149 Nm**

That is roughly **35% over specification**.

**What over-torquing wheel nuts does:**
- **Stretches the studs beyond their elastic limit.** A yielded stud has permanently reduced clamping capacity and is fatigue-prone. It may hold today and fail later, in service, at speed
- **Can strip the thread** in the stud or nut
- **Distorts the brake disc**, causing thickness variation and brake judder — a genuine and common consequence of over-torqued wheel nuts
- Makes the nuts extremely difficult to remove roadside, which matters if the customer has a puncture

**The correct value in ft-lb:** 110 Nm ÷ 1.356 = **81 ft-lb**

So the wrench should be set to approximately **81 ft-lb**, not 110.

**Better still, use a wrench that reads in Nm** so no conversion is required. Every conversion is an opportunity for an error, and this one is a 35% error that looks completely normal on the dial.

---

**Problem 2 — The rattle gun method is not torquing.**

**An impact wrench does not apply a controlled torque.** Its output varies with:
- Line pressure or battery state of charge
- Tool condition and internal wear
- How long the trigger is held
- Socket and extension used
- Thread condition

Two nuts run up with the same gun on the same wheel can end up substantially different.

**"Give it a nip with the bar" is worse, not better.** A breaker bar applies whatever torque the person's arm generates. It has no calibration and no feedback. It is a guess, and it is a guess that is almost always on the high side because people naturally over-tighten wheel nuts.

**"That's what everyone does" is not a specification.** Wheels coming off vehicles is a known and recurring failure mode in the industry, and improper torquing is a leading cause.

---

**The correct procedure:**

**1. Prepare the mating surfaces.**
- Clean the hub face and the wheel mating face. **Rust, dirt or debris between the wheel and hub cause the joint to settle after fitting**, and the nuts lose clamping load even though they were torqued correctly
- Check the studs and nuts for damage, stretched threads, or corrosion. Replace anything damaged

**2. Threads.**
- Clean threads
- **Do not lubricate the studs unless the manufacturer specifies it.** Oil on a dry-spec thread significantly increases the actual clamping load for the same indicated torque, and can yield the stud. This is a very common error made with good intentions

**3. Fit the wheel.**
- Locate the wheel on the hub and start all nuts **by hand** to confirm they are not cross-threaded
- Cross-threading is why nuts are started by hand, not by gun

**4. Run the nuts up — the gun's legitimate role.**
- An impact wrench may be used to **run the nuts up to seated**, which saves time
- It must **not** be used for final tightening

**5. Torque properly.**
- Set the wrench to the correct value — **81 ft-lb**, or 110 Nm on an Nm wrench
- Tighten in a **star / diagonal pattern**, not around the circle. A star pattern seats the wheel squarely; sequential tightening around the circle pulls it on unevenly and can distort the disc
- Apply force **smoothly** and **stop at the click**
- Complete a second pass around the star pattern to confirm — the first nuts torqued may relax slightly as later nuts pull the wheel fully home

**6. Lower and re-torque.**
- Lower the vehicle so the weight is on the wheel and re-check the torque, since the hub settles under load

**7. Advise the customer.**
- Recommend a **re-torque check after a short distance** — commonly around 50–100km — particularly on alloy wheels, which can settle. Many workshops issue a re-torque advice slip

**[VERIFY: Re-torque distance recommendations against the vehicle or wheel manufacturer's guidance.]**

**8. Wind the torque wrench back** to its lowest setting and return it to its case.

---

**What I would say to the other apprentice:**

*"That wrench is in ft-lb — 110 Nm is only about 81 ft-lb. If we set it to 110 we'd be nearly 150 Nm and we'd be stretching studs and warping the disc. And the rattle gun doesn't torque, it just tightens — we still have to do them properly."*

Said plainly, not as a lecture. He is repeating what he has been shown, which is a training problem rather than a character problem.

---

**The underlying point:**

A wheel detachment at speed is one of the few workshop errors that can kill someone who was never in the workshop. **It is also entirely preventable by a torque wrench set to the right number in the right units.**

The unit conversion here is the sort of error that produces a perfectly confident-looking job — the wrench clicked, the nuts are tight, everything looks right. That is precisely why it is worth checking every time.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student identifies the unit error immediately, performs the conversion correctly in both directions, and quantifies the overshoot at 35% — which makes the severity concrete rather than abstract. The consequences listed are accurate and include brake disc distortion from over-torqued wheel nuts, which is a real-world outcome many technicians do not connect to this cause. The critique of the impact wrench correctly identifies that its output varies with multiple uncontrolled factors, and the observation that a breaker bar "nip" is an uncalibrated guess biased high is precise. The procedure is complete and correctly sequenced, including several points frequently omitted: cleaning the hub mating face to prevent post-fit settlement, starting nuts by hand to detect cross-threading, the specific warning against unspecified thread lubrication, the star pattern with a confirming second pass, re-torquing with the vehicle's weight on the wheel, and customer re-torque advice. The recommendation to use an Nm-reading wrench to eliminate conversion entirely is the correct systemic fix rather than just the correct calculation. Addressing the other apprentice without condescension, and recognising it as a training gap, shows appropriate professional judgement. Strong Competent.
