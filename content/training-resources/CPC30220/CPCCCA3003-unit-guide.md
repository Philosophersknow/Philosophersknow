## What This Unit Is About

Framing is the structural skeleton of a timber-framed building. Every subsequent trade — plumbers, electricians, plasterers, tilers, cabinetmakers — depends on the frame being square, plumb, level, and dimensionally accurate. A frame that is out by 10mm creates problems that compound through every later stage of the build.

CPCCCA3003 is the central practical unit of the carpentry qualification. It asks you to set out and construct floor and wall frames that meet AS 1684 requirements and the tolerances specified in the plans.

## Terminology You Must Know

### Floor Framing

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Bearer** | Primary horizontal member spanning between piers or stumps; supports joists |
| **Joist** | Secondary horizontal member spanning between bearers; supports flooring |
| **Stump / pier** | Vertical support carrying bearers to the footing |
| **Ant cap** | Metal cap on top of stumps to deter termites and force them into the open |
| **Blocking / solid blocking** | Short members fitted between joists to prevent twisting |
| **Trimmer** | Member framing an opening (stairwell, hearth) |
| **Bracing** | Diagonal members or sheet material resisting racking (lateral) forces |
| **Sub-floor** | The whole assembly below the finished floor: stumps, bearers, joists |

### Wall Framing

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Bottom plate** | Horizontal member at the base of the wall frame |
| **Top plate** | Horizontal member at the top of the wall frame |
| **Stud** | Vertical member between plates |
| **Common stud** | A full-height stud in the regular spacing pattern |
| **Jamb stud** | Stud on either side of an opening; carries the lintel |
| **Jack stud / cripple** | Shortened stud above or below an opening |
| **Lintel / header** | Horizontal member over an opening carrying the load above |
| **Noggin / nogging** | Short horizontal member between studs; provides fixing and stiffens studs |
| **Sill / sill trimmer** | Horizontal member forming the bottom of a window opening |
| **Nib wall** | Short return wall |
| **Wall plate spacing (c/c)** | Centre-to-centre spacing of studs — typically 450mm or 600mm |

## Set-Out — The Foundation of Accurate Framing

Set-out is the process of establishing accurate positions and dimensions before construction begins. **Errors made at set-out are multiplied through the whole build.**

### Establishing Square — The 3-4-5 Method

The most reliable field method for establishing a right angle uses the Pythagorean theorem. Any triangle with sides in the ratio 3:4:5 contains a right angle.

**Method:**
1. From the corner, measure 3 units along one line and mark it (e.g., 3000mm)
2. From the same corner, measure 4 units along the other line and mark it (e.g., 4000mm)
3. Measure the diagonal between the two marks
4. If the corner is exactly 90°, the diagonal will be exactly 5 units (5000mm)
5. If it is not, adjust one line until the diagonal measures correctly

**Use larger multiples for greater accuracy** — 6-8-10 or 9-12-15 gives a more accurate result over a longer run than 3-4-5.

### Checking Square by Diagonals

For a rectangle, if both diagonals are equal in length, the shape is square (all corners 90°).

This is the standard field check for a floor frame or wall frame. Measure corner-to-corner both ways; if the two measurements differ, the frame is out of square and must be adjusted (racked) until they match.

**Tolerance:** Refer to the specification. A common residential tolerance is ±5mm over the length of a wall, but always check the specified requirement.

### Establishing Level

- **Spirit level:** Suitable for short runs; check the level itself for accuracy by reversing it end-for-end (readings should match)
- **Water level:** Two clear tubes connected by a hose filled with water; water finds its own level over any distance — accurate and unaffected by obstacles
- **Laser level:** Rotating or line laser; fast and accurate over long distances; the standard on most modern sites
- **Dumpy level / automatic level:** Optical instrument used with a staff for site levels and larger buildings

### Establishing Plumb

- **Spirit level (vertical vials):** Standard method for studs and short walls
- **Plumb bob:** Weight on a string — gravity establishes true vertical; unaffected by instrument calibration
- **Laser plumb:** Projects a vertical line or point

## Floor Framing — The Process

### Step 1: Verify the Set-Out
Check the slab, footings, or stump positions against the plan. Confirm:
- Overall dimensions match the plan
- Diagonals are equal (square)
- Levels are correct

**If the substructure is out, raise it before framing.** Building a square frame on an out-of-square base creates problems at the wall-to-frame interface.

### Step 2: Position and Level Bearers
- Bearers sit on stumps/piers at the spacing shown on the plan (governed by AS 1684 span tables)
- Ant caps must be in place on stumps
- Bearers must be level along their length and level relative to each other
- Packing may be required — use durable packers, not timber offcuts that will rot or compress

### Step 3: Set Out and Install Joists
- Mark joist positions on the bearers at the specified spacing (commonly 450mm c/c)
- Set out from one end consistently — measure from a single datum, not cumulatively from each joist (cumulative measuring compounds small errors)
- Install joists with the **crown up** — timber has a natural bow; the crown (high point) placed upward allows the load to flatten it
- Check joists are level and evenly spaced

### Step 4: Install Blocking and Bracing
- Solid blocking between joists prevents twisting and distributes loads
- Bracing (diagonal or sheet) resists racking

### Step 5: Check Before Sheeting
- Diagonals equal (square)
- All joists level and correctly spaced
- No twisted or crowned-down members
- Fixings complete and correct

## Wall Framing — The Process

### Step 1: Set Out the Bottom Plate

Mark the wall positions on the floor (chalk line or pencil), then mark out on the bottom plate:
- **Stud positions** at specified centres (450 or 600mm c/c)
- **Openings** — door and window positions with jamb stud locations
- **Intersecting walls** — where internal walls will connect

**Set out both plates together.** Lay the top plate alongside the bottom plate and mark them at the same time — this guarantees the studs are aligned top and bottom.

### Step 2: Mark the Stud Layout

Standard convention: the first stud is set so that the **centre of the stud falls at the spacing dimension** from the end. This ensures sheet materials (typically 1200mm wide) land on the centre of a stud.

Example at 600mm centres with 90mm studs:
- Mark the first stud so that its centre is at 600mm from the end of the plate
- Subsequent studs at 600mm centres
- A 1200mm sheet will then break exactly on the centre of the stud at 1200mm

**This is why stud spacing is 450 or 600 — both divide evenly into 1200mm sheet widths.**

### Step 3: Frame Openings

For a window or door opening:
1. **Jamb studs** are full-height studs on each side of the opening
2. **Lintel** spans the opening, supported by the jamb studs (or by trimmer studs fixed inside the jamb studs, depending on the detail)
3. **Jack studs (cripples)** fill the space above the lintel to the top plate
4. For windows: a **sill trimmer** forms the bottom of the opening, with jack studs below it to the bottom plate

**Opening size:** The framed opening must be larger than the finished door/window unit to allow for the frame, packing, and adjustment. Check the manufacturer's specified rough opening size — do not guess.

### Step 4: Assemble the Frame

Frames are typically assembled flat on the floor and then lifted into position:
1. Lay out the plates on edge, parallel, at the correct spacing
2. Position studs between them at the marked positions
3. Nail through the plates into the ends of the studs (2 nails per stud for 90mm framing — check the specification)
4. Install lintels, jack studs, and sill trimmers
5. Check the frame for square (diagonals) before standing

### Step 5: Stand, Plumb, and Brace

1. Lift the frame into position on the marked line
2. Fix the bottom plate to the floor
3. Plumb the frame using a level on the end studs
4. Brace temporarily to hold it plumb — temporary bracing must be adequate; frames blown over by wind are a serious safety and cost incident
5. Install permanent bracing as specified

### Step 6: Install Noggins

Noggins (blocking between studs) serve to:
- Stiffen studs against buckling
- Provide fixing for sheet material edges, fixtures, and fittings
- Provide backing for future fixings (towel rails, TV brackets, handrails)

Noggin height is typically specified in AS 1684 or on the plans. They may be staggered to allow straight nailing through the studs.

## AS 1684 — Residential Timber-Framed Construction

AS 1684 is the primary standard for timber framing in residential buildings. It provides:
- **Span tables:** Maximum spans for bearers, joists, lintels, and rafters based on member size, stress grade, spacing, and load
- **Connection requirements:** Nail and fixing patterns for structural connections
- **Bracing requirements:** How much bracing is required based on wind classification
- **Tie-down requirements:** Fixing the structure against uplift (critical in high-wind areas)

**You do not memorise AS 1684.** You learn to use the span tables and connection details to select and fix members correctly.

### Reading a Span Table
Span tables are structured by:
1. Member type (bearer, joist, lintel)
2. Stress grade (F5, F7, MGP10, MGP12, etc.)
3. Member size (90×45, 140×45, etc.)
4. Spacing (450, 600mm)
5. Load condition (roof only, roof + ceiling, floor load width)

Cross-reference these to find the maximum allowable span. **If a member spans further than the table allows, it must be upgraded** (larger section, higher grade, or reduced spacing).

## Wind Classification and Bracing

Australia is divided into wind classification regions (N1–N6 for non-cyclonic; C1–C4 for cyclonic). The wind classification for a site determines:
- The bracing capacity required
- Tie-down requirements
- Fixing specifications

A structural engineer or the plan specification will state the wind classification. Higher wind classification means more bracing and stronger tie-down.

**Bracing types:**
- **Diagonal timber bracing:** Let into the studs at approximately 45°
- **Metal strap bracing:** Tensioned steel straps in an X pattern
- **Sheet bracing:** Structural plywood or bracing-grade sheet fixed to the frame at a specified nailing pattern

Bracing must be evenly distributed and connected top and bottom to transfer loads into the structure.

## Common Framing Errors

**1. Cumulative measuring error**
Measuring each stud from the previous stud instead of from a single datum. A 1mm error repeated 20 times is a 20mm error at the end of the wall. **Always measure from a single fixed point.**

**2. Studs crowned inconsistently**
If some studs are crowned in and some crowned out, the wall face will be wavy. Sight along every stud before installing and orient all crowns the same way (typically all crowned toward the outside face for external walls).

**3. Frame not square before standing**
A frame assembled out of square and then stood is very difficult to correct. Check diagonals before lifting.

**4. Inadequate temporary bracing**
Frames stood without adequate temporary bracing can be blown over. This is a serious safety incident and destroys the frame.

**5. Rough openings sized incorrectly**
Framing an opening to the finished door size instead of the rough opening size means the frame must be cut out and rebuilt.

**6. Lintel undersized**
Using a lintel that does not meet the AS 1684 span requirement for the opening width and load. This is a structural failure risk and will fail frame inspection.

## Worked Example — Set-Out Calculation

**Prompt:**

*You are setting out a rectangular floor frame that must be 7200mm × 4800mm. You have positioned the first two corners along the 7200mm side. Describe how you would establish the third corner accurately, and how you would verify the completed rectangle is square. If the diagonals measure 8646mm and 8658mm, what does this tell you and what do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

**Establishing the third corner:**

I would use the 3-4-5 method, scaled up for accuracy over this distance. Working from the corner where the 7200mm side meets the 4800mm side:

- Measure 3600mm along the 7200mm line (this is my "3" side ×1200)
- Measure 4800mm out on the perpendicular line (this is my "4" side ×1200)
- The diagonal between these two points must measure 6000mm (the "5" side ×1200) for the corner to be exactly 90°
- I adjust the perpendicular line until the diagonal measures exactly 6000mm

Using a 3600-4800-6000 triangle rather than 3-4-5 at a smaller scale gives much greater accuracy — a small angular error over 600mm becomes large over 7200mm, whereas measuring over 4800mm reduces the angular error significantly.

Once the corner is established at 90°, I extend the line to the full 4800mm and mark the third corner. The fourth corner is then established by measuring 7200mm parallel to the first side.

**Verifying square:**

For a rectangle that is truly square, both diagonals must be equal. The theoretical diagonal for a 7200 × 4800 rectangle is:

√(7200² + 4800²) = √(51,840,000 + 23,040,000) = √74,880,000 = **8653mm** (rounded)

**Interpreting the measured diagonals:**

The measured diagonals are 8646mm and 8658mm — a difference of **12mm**. The frame is out of square.

The theoretical diagonal is 8653mm; my two measurements straddle it (8646 is 7mm short, 8658 is 5mm long), which confirms the overall dimensions are close to correct but the shape is racked — it is a parallelogram rather than a rectangle.

**What I do:**

I rack the frame — push the corner at the end of the longer diagonal (8658mm) inward, or pull the corner at the end of the shorter diagonal (8646mm) outward. Moving one corner changes both diagonals in opposite directions, so a correction of approximately half the difference brings them together.

I would adjust and re-measure both diagonals until they are equal (and match the theoretical 8653mm), then fix the frame in position before the racking can move again. On a floor frame this typically means fixing the bottom plate or bearers securely once square is achieved.

A 12mm error over a 7.2m frame is outside typical residential tolerance and must be corrected — if I sheet the floor at this point, every wall built on it will be out of square, and the error will carry through to the roof.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student scales up the 3-4-5 method correctly and explains *why* (accuracy over distance), calculates the theoretical diagonal correctly using Pythagoras, correctly diagnoses the frame as racked rather than incorrectly dimensioned, describes the correction method, and correctly identifies the consequence of not correcting it. This demonstrates both the calculation skill and the practical judgment the unit requires. Strong Competent.
