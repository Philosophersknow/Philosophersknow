## What This Unit Is About

Tools are how a carpenter converts knowledge into work. Selecting the wrong tool wastes time and produces poor results. Using a tool incorrectly produces injuries — carpentry has one of the higher rates of hand, eye and amputation injuries in the trades, and the great majority involve powered cutting tools.

CPCCCA2002 asks you to select the right tool for the task, use it safely and correctly, maintain it, and recognise when it is unserviceable.

## Hand Tools

### Measuring and Marking

| Tool | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tape measure** | Linear measurement | The hook is deliberately loose — it slides by its own thickness to read correctly for both internal and external measurements. **This is not a fault** |
| **Steel rule** | Precise short measurement | |
| **Combination square** | 90° and 45°, depth setting, marking | |
| **Try square** | Checking and marking 90° | Check its accuracy by marking a line, flipping the square, and re-marking — the lines must coincide |
| **Sliding bevel** | Transferring an arbitrary angle | Essential in roof work |
| **Framing / steel square** | Roof and stair set-out, checking large squares | |
| **Spirit level** | Level and plumb | Check by reversing end-for-end — readings must match |
| **Chalk line** | Long straight lines | Snap once; repeated snapping widens the line |
| **Marking gauge** | Lines parallel to an edge | |
| **String line** | Straightness over distance | |
| **Laser level** | Level, plumb, square over distance | Requires periodic calibration checking |

**Marking accuracy determines cutting accuracy.** A pencil line 2mm wide introduces 2mm of ambiguity. Use a sharp pencil or a knife line for precision work, and cut consistently to the same side of the line every time.

### Cutting

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| **Handsaw — rip** | Cutting **with** the grain; larger teeth, chisel-like |
| **Handsaw — crosscut** | Cutting **across** the grain; knife-like teeth |
| **Tenon / back saw** | Fine, accurate joinery cuts; stiffened spine |
| **Coping saw** | Curves and internal cuts |
| **Chisels** | Paring, housings, mortices, hinge gains |
| **Utility knife** | Marking, trimming, scoring |
| **Plane — jack** | General stock removal and straightening |
| **Plane — smoothing** | Final finishing surface |
| **Plane — block** | End grain, small work, one-handed |

**Chisel safety:** always cut **away from your body** and keep both hands behind the cutting edge. The hand holding the work must never be in front of the chisel. Chisels cause a significant share of carpentry hand injuries, almost always by slipping toward the holding hand.

**A blunt tool is more dangerous than a sharp one.** It requires more force, which means less control, and it is more likely to skid off the work.

### Fixing and Striking

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| **Claw hammer** | Driving and drawing nails |
| **Nail punch** | Setting nail heads below the surface |
| **Screwdrivers** | Match the tip to the fastener — Phillips, Pozidriv and square drive are not interchangeable |
| **Nail bar / wrecking bar** | Demolition, drawing large fixings |
| **Clamps** | Holding work; sash, F, G, spring, quick-action |

**Using the wrong screwdriver tip cams out, damages the fastener head, and slips.** Phillips and Pozidriv look similar and are not the same — Pozidriv has additional smaller ribs between the main flutes.

## Power Tools

### Circular Saw

The most-used and most dangerous power tool in carpentry.

**Safe use:**
- **Guard must operate freely.** Never wedge, tie back, or hold the lower guard open. This is the single most common circular saw modification and it causes the worst injuries
- **Set blade depth** so it projects only about 5–10mm below the material. Excessive depth increases kickback severity and exposes more blade
- **Support the work so the offcut falls free.** Material that pinches the blade causes kickback
- **Never cut freehand where the offcut is trapped between supports** — this closes on the blade
- **Stand to the side of the cut line**, not directly behind the saw
- Let the blade reach full speed before entering the cut, and let it stop before setting the saw down
- Both hands on the saw; the work clamped or supported

**Kickback** occurs when the blade binds and the saw is thrown violently back toward the operator. Causes: pinched blade, blade not aligned with the cut, cutting with a dull or damaged blade, or the offcut trapping the blade.

### Drop Saw / Mitre Saw

- **Clamp or hold the work firmly against the fence**
- Let the blade reach full speed before lowering
- **Let the blade stop before raising** — raising a spinning blade can throw the offcut
- Keep hands well clear of the blade path; use the hold-down clamp for short pieces
- **Never cross your arm over the blade** to hold work on the far side — reposition instead

### Drills and Drivers

- Select the correct bit type — twist for metal, brad point for timber, spade or auger for large holes, hole saw for large diameters
- Use the correct speed — large bits and hard materials require lower speed
- **Secure the work.** A drill bit binding in unsecured work spins the work, not the drill
- Beware of the reaction torque of large-diameter bits and hole saws; use a side handle
- Remove the chuck key before starting

### Nail Guns

Nail guns cause serious injuries, including fatalities, and are frequently treated casually.

- **Never disable the safety contact tip.** Bypassing the contact trip so the tool fires on trigger alone is the leading cause of nail gun injuries
- **Understand the firing mode.** *Sequential* firing requires contact then trigger, in order, for each nail — it is substantially safer. *Contact/bump* firing fires whenever the tip is bumped with the trigger held, and is responsible for most unintended discharges
- **Never point at any person**, loaded or not, connected or not
- **Disconnect the air or remove the battery** before clearing a jam, adjusting, or servicing
- Keep the non-firing hand well clear — nails deflect off knots and can exit the side of the timber
- Do not fire into unsupported material or near an edge where the nail may deflect out

### Other Power Tools

| Tool | Key safety point |
|---|---|
| **Jigsaw** | Support the work; blade extends below — check clearance |
| **Router** | Router bits spin at very high speed; secure work; take light passes; feed against the cutter rotation |
| **Planer** | Blades continue spinning after switch-off; never set down until stopped |
| **Angle grinder** | Correct disc for the material; **never use a cutting disc for grinding**; check disc for damage; guard in place |
| **Reciprocating saw** | Blade length must clear the work; check for services before cutting into structure |
| **Impact driver** | High torque; check fastener and material can take it |

## Powder-Actuated (Explosive Power) Tools

Explosive power tools drive fixings into concrete and steel using a controlled explosive charge. See also CPCCCM2007.

- **Operation requires specific training and, in most jurisdictions, a licence or certificate of competency**
- Charges are colour-coded by power — select the lowest charge that achieves the fixing
- **Never fire into or near an edge**, into brittle material, or where the fixing may pass through
- Check what is on the **other side** of the material before firing
- Eye and hearing protection mandatory
- Store charges securely and separately from the tool
- Misfires: keep the tool pointed at the work surface for the specified waiting period before opening

**[VERIFY: Licensing and competency requirements for explosive power tools vary by state. Confirm with your state WHS regulator.]**

## Electrical Safety with Power Tools

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **RCD protection** | All portable electrical equipment on a construction site must be protected by a residual current device |
| **Test and tag** | Portable equipment on construction sites must be inspected, tested and tagged at required intervals by a competent person |
| **Leads** | Off the ground where practicable; not run through water or across access ways; inspected for damage |
| **Damaged equipment** | Removed from service and tagged out — not "used carefully" |
| **Double insulated** | Marked with the double-square symbol; no earth required |
| **Wet conditions** | Electrical tools and water do not mix; use battery tools or stop work |

**[VERIFY: Test and tag intervals for construction sites against AS/NZS 3012 and your state regulator's requirements.]**

## Tool Maintenance

### Sharpening

- **Chisels and plane irons** are sharpened on a stone or grinder to the correct primary and secondary bevel angles, then honed
- **Overheating a tool on a grinder draws the temper** — the steel turns blue and permanently loses its ability to hold an edge. Grind lightly with frequent cooling
- Saw blades and drill bits are generally sharpened by a specialist or replaced

### Routine Care

- Clean tools after use — resin and sawdust build-up affects performance
- Light oil on steel to prevent rust
- Store in a dry place; do not leave tools on the ground or exposed to weather
- Check power leads and plugs before each use
- Keep guards clean and free-moving
- Replace worn or damaged blades and bits rather than persisting with them

### Inspection Before Use

Every tool, every time:
- Cords and plugs intact
- Guards present and operating
- Blades and bits sharp and undamaged
- Handles secure — a loose hammer head is a projectile
- Current test tag where required

## PPE for Tool Use

| PPE | When |
|---|---|
| **Eye protection** | Any cutting, drilling, grinding, nailing, or use of compressed air — **the majority of carpentry eye injuries occur during tasks the worker judged too brief to warrant glasses** |
| **Hearing protection** | Power tools; cumulative and permanent damage |
| **Respiratory (P2 minimum)** | Timber dust — hardwood dust is a recognised carcinogen; engineered products and MDF produce fine dust with added binders |
| **Gloves** | Handling material — but **not when operating rotating tools**, where a glove can be caught and drag the hand in |
| **Safety footwear** | At all times |
| **Hard hat, hi-vis** | Per site requirements |

**Timber dust:** wood dust is classified as hazardous. Use on-tool dust extraction where available, work in ventilated areas, and wear a P2 respirator when cutting or sanding — particularly MDF and treated timbers.

**Treated timber (CCA, ACQ):** do not burn offcuts, avoid inhaling the dust, and wash hands before eating. Dispose of offcuts correctly — treated timber is not general waste.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are on site and need to rip a 3.6m length of 240 × 45 hardwood down to 180mm width. Another apprentice offers you a circular saw. You notice the lower guard is stiff and does not spring back fully — it hangs about 20mm open. He says "yeah it's been like that for weeks, just flick it with your thumb, everyone uses it."*

*What do you do, and how would you approach this cut safely?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I would not use that saw, and I would not accept "everyone uses it" as a reason to.

---

**Why the guard matters:**

The lower guard is the primary safety device on a circular saw. Its job is to close over the blade the instant the saw leaves the cut. A guard hanging 20mm open leaves a spinning blade exposed every time the saw is lifted or set down.

The specific hazard: when a cut finishes and the saw is lifted, the blade is still spinning at full speed for several seconds. With a functioning guard, it closes immediately. With this saw, there is an exposed blade at hand and thigh height while the operator is moving. Circular saw injuries commonly occur in exactly this moment — not during the cut.

**"Flick it with your thumb" makes it worse, not better.** It puts a hand near a spinning blade as a routine part of operating the tool, and it depends on the operator remembering to do it every single time, including when tired, rushed, or distracted.

**The duration of the fault is not a defence.** "It's been like that for weeks" means the hazard has existed unaddressed for weeks and that multiple people have been exposed to it. That makes it more serious, not less.

---

**What I do:**

**1. Do not use the saw.** I would say so plainly: *"I'm not using that with the guard hanging open — I'll grab another one."*

**2. Tag it out and remove it from service.** A defective tool left in the rack will be picked up by the next person, who may not notice. I would tag it, isolate it (remove the battery or coil the lead and tag the plug), and put it somewhere it will not be casually grabbed.

**3. Report it to my supervisor.** Not to get the other apprentice in trouble, but because the tool needs repair or replacement and because a tool with a known defect circulating for weeks indicates the pre-use inspection process is not working.

**4. Get a serviceable saw** and check it myself before use — guard operates freely, blade sharp and undamaged, cord and plug intact, current test tag.

---

**How I would approach the cut safely:**

This is a **rip cut** — with the grain — in 3.6m of 240 × 45 hardwood. Several specific considerations:

**Blade selection:**
- A rip or general-purpose blade with fewer, larger teeth. A fine crosscut blade will burn and bog in a long rip in hardwood
- Blade sharp and undamaged — a dull blade in hardwood dramatically increases binding and kickback risk

**Blade depth:**
- Set so the blade projects about **5–10mm below the material**. For 45mm stock, roughly 50–55mm of depth. Excessive depth exposes more blade and makes kickback more violent

**Work support — the critical issue for a long rip:**
- The board must be supported so the **offcut falls free and does not pinch the blade**
- Support on **both sides of the cut line**, with the supports positioned so the kerf can open rather than close
- **Do not support only at the two ends** — a 3.6m board supported only at the ends will sag in the middle, closing the kerf onto the blade as the cut progresses. This is a classic kickback setup
- Use trestles with bearers running perpendicular to the cut, or foam board, so both pieces stay supported throughout
- Hardwood is heavy — a 3.6m length of 240 × 45 hardwood is a significant weight and will sag noticeably

**Guiding the cut:**
- A 3.6m rip needs a guide. Freehand over that length will wander, and a wandering blade binds
- Clamp a straight edge as a fence, or use a track/guide rail
- Confirm the guide is clamped at both ends and cannot shift

**Technique:**
- Stand **to the side of the blade line**, not directly behind the saw
- Let the blade reach full speed before entering the cut
- Feed at a steady rate — forcing it bogs the blade; too slow burns
- Watch that the kerf stays open behind the blade. **If it starts to close, stop** — the material is moving and needs re-supporting
- Do not reach across the saw
- Let the blade stop before setting the saw down

**PPE:**
- Eye protection — non-negotiable for a long rip throwing chips
- Hearing protection — a long cut in hardwood is sustained noise
- **P2 respirator** — hardwood dust is a recognised carcinogen and a 3.6m rip in 45mm hardwood produces a lot of it
- **No gloves** while operating the saw
- Dust extraction connected if available

**Consider whether a circular saw is even the right tool:**
- If a table saw is available on site, a 3.6m rip is safer and more accurate on a table saw with proper infeed and outfeed support
- The circular saw with a clamped guide is a legitimate approach, but it is worth asking rather than defaulting

---

**The broader point:**

The pressure in this scenario is social, not commercial. Refusing a tool that "everyone uses" means implying that everyone has been doing something unsafe, and as an apprentice that is uncomfortable.

But the guard is the difference between a near miss and a serious laceration, and the fact that nobody has been hurt yet is not evidence that the tool is safe — it is evidence that they have been lucky. **I would rather be the person who tagged the saw than the person who was holding it when the luck ran out.**

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student refuses the defective tool without hesitation and correctly identifies the specific mechanism of harm — the exposed blade after the cut, not during it — which demonstrates genuine understanding rather than rule recall. The rebuttal of "flick it with your thumb" is precise: it introduces a hand near the blade as routine practice and depends on unfailing memory. Recognising that a long-standing defect is *more* serious rather than less is a mature judgement. The tag-out and isolation step shows the student is protecting the next user, not just themselves. The rip cut approach is technically excellent: correct blade selection, correct depth with reasoning, and — critically — the identification that a 3.6m hardwood board supported only at the ends will sag and pinch the kerf, which is the exact kickback mechanism this cut presents. The instruction to stop if the kerf begins closing is the kind of in-process judgement that separates safe operators from lucky ones. Identifying hardwood dust as carcinogenic and questioning whether a table saw would be better both show thinking beyond the immediate instruction. The closing reflection on social pressure names the real obstacle honestly. Strong Competent.
