## What This Unit Is About

Construction is one of the highest-risk industries in Australia. Falls from height, being struck by falling objects, plant incidents, electrical contact, excavation collapse, and manual handling injuries collectively make construction one of the most dangerous working environments in the country. WHS knowledge is not background knowledge in construction — it is the foundation on which everything else sits.

CPCCWHS2001 goes beyond the White Card (CPCCWHS1001). Where the White Card provides general induction awareness, this unit requires you to apply WHS requirements actively in your daily work on a construction site — identifying hazards, implementing controls, participating in the site safety system, and knowing your rights and responsibilities as a construction worker.

## The Legislative Framework for Construction WHS

### Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Cth) — Model Act
The model WHS Act (adopted in most states and territories) establishes the primary obligations. As a construction worker:

**Your duties as a worker:**
- Take reasonable care for your own health and safety
- Take reasonable care that your actions do not adversely affect others
- Comply with reasonable WHS instructions from your employer
- Cooperate with WHS policies and procedures

**Your rights as a worker:**
- To work in an environment free from risks to health and safety (so far as is reasonably practicable)
- To be consulted on WHS matters that affect you
- To refuse unsafe work — and not be penalised for doing so
- To be represented by a Health and Safety Representative (HSR)

### Construction-Specific Regulations
Beyond the model WHS Act, construction is also regulated by specific codes of practice and regulations covering high-risk work:

- **Safe Work Method Statements (SWMS):** Required for high-risk construction work (see below)
- **Work at height:** Specific requirements for scaffolding, fall protection, working on roofs
- **Excavation and trenching:** Specific requirements for shoring and battering
- **Plant and equipment:** Licensing requirements for operating cranes, EWPs, forklifts
- **Hazardous substances:** Silica dust, asbestos (if disturbed during demolition or renovation), lead

## High-Risk Construction Work

High-risk construction work (HRCW) requires a **Safe Work Method Statement (SWMS)** before work commences. HRCW includes:

- Work at height (risk of fall of more than 2 metres)
- Work on or near energised electrical installations or services
- Work involving the risk of a person being buried or trapped (excavation, trenching, tunnelling)
- Work with or near pressurised gas distribution mains or piping
- Work on a road or adjacent to moving traffic where there is a risk to safety
- Work in areas with contaminated or flammable atmospheres
- Work involving demolition
- Work with explosives
- Work involving tilt-up or precast concrete
- Work near water or other liquid where there is a risk of drowning
- Work involving structural alterations that require temporary support to prevent collapse

**As a carpenter, the most common HRCW you will encounter is work at height (roofing, upper-floor framing) and work adjacent to traffic.**

## Safe Work Method Statements (SWMS)

A SWMS is a document that:
1. Identifies the high-risk work being performed
2. Identifies the hazards associated with that work
3. Describes the controls that will be implemented to manage those hazards
4. Must be prepared before the work starts
5. Must be followed during the work
6. Must be available at the workplace where the work is being performed

**Your role with SWMS:**
- You must be told about the SWMS before starting high-risk work
- You must sign or acknowledge the SWMS to confirm you have read and understood it
- You must follow the controls described in the SWMS
- If you identify a new hazard not in the SWMS, you must stop and raise it before continuing

A SWMS is not a piece of paper you sign without reading. If something goes wrong and you did not follow the SWMS, you may bear personal responsibility for the outcome.

## Hazard Identification in Construction

Construction sites are dynamic environments — hazards change as work progresses. Common construction hazards by type:

| Hazard Type | Construction Examples |
|---|---|
| **Falls from height** | Unprotected edges, unguarded holes in floors, unstable ladders, scaffold without edge protection |
| **Falling objects** | Tools or materials dropped from elevated work; unsecured materials in wind |
| **Struck by plant/vehicles** | Reversing vehicles; cranes swinging loads; mobile plant on site |
| **Electrical** | Contact with overhead or underground powerlines; damaged power tools; improper temporary electrical |
| **Manual handling** | Carrying heavy materials; repetitive movements; awkward postures |
| **Hazardous materials** | Silica dust (from cutting, grinding concrete, brick, stone); asbestos (in renovation/demolition); lead (older painted surfaces) |
| **Noise** | Power tools; compressors; concrete cutters — cumulative hearing damage |
| **Structural instability** | Incomplete structures; temporary works failures; excavation collapse |
| **Heat/UV** | Sun exposure during outdoor work; heat stress in hot weather |
| **Housekeeping** | Trips and slips from timber offcuts, cables, wet surfaces |

## The Hierarchy of Controls in Construction

| Level | Control | Construction Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Elimination** | Design the work so the hazard does not exist — prefabricating wall frames off-site to reduce time working at height |
| 2 | **Substitution** | Use a less hazardous method or material — using engineered products that do not require cutting on site (reduces silica dust) |
| 3 | **Isolation** | Separate people from the hazard — exclusion zones under crane lifts; barriers around excavations |
| 4 | **Engineering** | Physical controls — guard rails on edges; safety mesh under roof battons; edge protection on scaffolding |
| 5 | **Administrative** | Procedures and training — permit to work systems; training before operating plant; tool box talks |
| 6 | **PPE** | Last line of defence — hard hat, safety boots, hi-vis, eye protection, hearing protection, gloves, dust mask, fall arrest harness |

**In construction, PPE is always required — but it is the last line of defence, not the primary control. An unguarded roof edge is not made safe by wearing a harness — the primary control is guardrails or other fall protection systems.**

## Working at Height — Specific Requirements

Falls from height are the leading cause of fatalities in the Australian construction industry. The requirements are stringent:

**Fall prevention hierarchy:**
1. **Eliminate the risk** — do the work at ground level if possible (e.g., assemble roof trusses on the ground, lift into place)
2. **Passive fall prevention** — guardrails, perimeter protection, safety mesh — do not require the worker to take any action to be protected
3. **Work positioning systems** — scaffold, elevated work platforms — control the work position to prevent falls
4. **Fall arrest** — harnesses and lanyard systems — do not prevent a fall but arrest it; used when passive prevention is not practicable

**Fall protection is required when:**
- Working on a roof at any height (where there is a risk of falling from the edge)
- Working on any surface where a fall of 2 metres or more is possible
- Working near or over an unprotected opening or void

**Ladders:** Ladders are temporary access equipment, not work platforms. You should not perform carpentry work from a ladder if you can use a more stable platform. When ladders must be used, they must be:
- Set at the correct angle (1 unit out for every 4 units up — 75°)
- Secured at the top or footed at the bottom
- Extended at least 1 metre above the landing point (when used for access)
- Inspected before use

## Toolbox Talks

A toolbox talk (also called a toolbox meeting or pre-start meeting) is a brief safety discussion held at the worksite — typically at the start of the day or the start of a new phase of work. They:

- Remind workers of the hazards relevant to the work being done that day
- Reinforce the controls in place
- Allow workers to raise safety concerns
- Are typically brief (5–15 minutes)
- Are a legal compliance tool — records should be kept of attendance and topics covered

**Your responsibility at a toolbox talk:** Pay attention. Raise any concerns or hazards you have identified. Ask questions if you are unclear about a procedure or control. Signing the toolbox talk record means you attended and understood — not that you agreed with everything.

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) in Construction

On construction sites, the following PPE is typically mandatory as a minimum:

| PPE Item | When Required |
|---|---|
| **Hard hat (safety helmet)** | All times on site (where there is risk of head injury from falling objects or impact) |
| **Safety footwear (steel cap boots)** | All times on site |
| **Hi-visibility vest or clothing** | All times on site; essential where plant or vehicles are operating |
| **Eye protection** | When cutting, grinding, nailing, or any operation that creates flying particles or dust |
| **Hearing protection** | When using power tools, compressors, or in areas with noise above 85dB |
| **Dust mask / respirator** | When cutting or grinding materials that create hazardous dust (silica, asbestos — P2 minimum for silica) |
| **Gloves** | When handling materials with sharp edges, splinters, or chemicals |
| **Sun protection** | For outdoor work — sunscreen, hat (under hard hat), long-sleeve clothing |
| **Fall arrest harness** | When working at height where passive fall prevention is not in place |

**Your responsibility:** Inspect PPE before use. Report damaged or missing PPE. Do not use damaged PPE. Store PPE correctly. Replace when worn or damaged.

## Incident Reporting and Emergency Procedures

### What Is a Reportable Incident?

- Any injury requiring medical treatment beyond first aid
- Any **notifiable incident** (serious injury, death, dangerous incident) — must be reported to the WHS regulator immediately

**As a worker, your immediate obligation:**
1. Make the area safe (without putting yourself at further risk)
2. Call emergency services if needed (000)
3. Notify your supervisor immediately
4. Do not disturb the scene (preserve it for investigation)
5. Complete an incident report as required by your organisation

### Dangerous Incidents (Notifiable to the Regulator)

In construction, dangerous incidents include:
- Collapse, overturning, or failure of any plant
- Collapse or failure of an excavation
- Uncontrolled escape of a substance, energy source, or agent
- Implosion, explosion, or fire

These are **notifiable to the state WHS regulator** (SafeWork NSW, WorkSafe VIC, etc.) immediately and the site must not be disturbed until the regulator gives permission (except to make the area safe or assist the injured).

## Your Right to Cease Unsafe Work

Under the model WHS Act, you have the right to cease or refuse to carry out work if you have a reasonable concern that it would expose you or another person to a serious risk. You must:
- Notify your supervisor of the concern as soon as practicable
- Remain available to carry out alternative safe work

**You cannot be penalised for refusing unsafe work.** An employer who penalises a worker for refusing unsafe work is committing an offence.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are a second-year carpentry apprentice. You are working on a two-storey residential framing job. Your supervisor asks you to nail off the top plates on the upper floor without any edge protection in place. The edge of the floor is open to a drop of approximately 3.5 metres to concrete below. There are no guardrails or safety mesh. Your supervisor says "we'll put the railing up once the framing is done — just be careful." What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

Working within 2 metres of an open edge with a drop of 3.5 metres to concrete is high-risk construction work. A fall from this height onto concrete would likely be fatal or cause catastrophic injury. I have a legal right — and a duty of care to myself — not to perform this work without adequate fall protection in place.

**What I would say to my supervisor:**

"I'm not comfortable working that close to that edge without edge protection in place — the drop is about 3.5 metres and that's serious. Is there any way we can get the guardrails or safety mesh up first, or can I work from the inside of the floor away from the edge?"

I would raise this respectfully but clearly. My supervisor may not have thought carefully about the risk — raising it gives them the opportunity to solve it. Solutions that would allow the work to proceed safely include:

1. **Install temporary edge protection** (guardrails or a safety mesh barrier) before the framing work — this is the correct approach
2. **Use an elevated work platform** on the outside of the building so the work can be done from outside with the EWP providing fall protection
3. **Redesign the work sequence** — frame sections of wall away from the edge and manoeuvre them into position

**If my supervisor directs me to proceed without protection:**

I have the right to refuse this work under the model WHS Act. I would tell my supervisor that I am not willing to work in that position without fall protection, and I would not be penalised for this refusal. If the pressure continued, I would contact the site supervisor/principal contractor, and if necessary, the HSR or the WHS regulator.

I would document the direction I was given and my response.

**What I would NOT do:** Work at the unprotected edge. "Be careful" is not a control measure — it is an instruction to manage the risk personally, which is a PPE-level (or worse, no-level) control for a hazard that requires engineering controls. The potential consequence of a fall here is fatal.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies the legal threshold (2m+ fall = high-risk construction work), proposes practical solutions before invoking the right of refusal (giving the supervisor a way to solve the problem), knows what the right to cease unsafe work means and how to exercise it, and correctly identifies that "be careful" is not a risk control. This response demonstrates both WHS knowledge and mature professional judgment. Strong Competent.
