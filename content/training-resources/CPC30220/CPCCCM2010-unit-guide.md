## What This Unit Is About

Carpenters are electrocuted regularly, and almost never by doing electrical work.

They are electrocuted by a scaffold tube contacting an overhead line, a nail gun into a concealed cable, a ladder swung up into a service, an excavator bucket into an underground main, or a damaged lead in wet conditions.

CPCCCM2010 asks you to recognise electrical hazards in your work environment, maintain safe approach distances, and know what to do when contact occurs.

**You are not being trained to work on electrical installations.** Only licensed electrical workers do that. You are being trained not to be killed by them.

---

## Why Electricity Kills

**It takes very little current.** The lethal quantity is far smaller than most people assume.

| Current through the body | Effect |
|---|---|
| ~1 mA | Perception threshold — a tingle |
| ~5 mA | Painful |
| ~10–20 mA | **"Let-go" threshold exceeded** — muscular contraction prevents release of the conductor |
| ~30 mA | Respiratory paralysis; the level at which an RCD is designed to trip |
| ~50–100 mA | **Ventricular fibrillation — the usual mechanism of electrocution death** |
| Higher | Severe burns, cardiac arrest, tissue destruction |

**[VERIFY: current thresholds and physiological effects against current Safe Work Australia and electrical safety regulator guidance. Values are indicative and vary with duration, path and individual factors.]**

**Three points that matter practically:**

**1. The let-go threshold is why you cannot rely on pulling away.** Above roughly 10–20 mA, the muscles contract and grip. A person holding an energised tool cannot release it. This is why bystanders find someone apparently frozen in contact.

**2. It is current, not voltage, that kills** — but voltage drives current. At mains voltage, contact through wet skin can easily produce a lethal current.

**3. Path matters.** Hand-to-hand or hand-to-foot puts the current across the chest and the heart. Contact with both hands is more dangerous than one.

### Factors That Increase Risk

- **Water and moisture** — dramatically reduces skin resistance. Wet conditions, sweat, wet gloves, standing on damp ground
- **Earthed conductive surfaces** — concrete slabs, steel frames, scaffolding, wet timber. Standing on an earthed surface completes the circuit
- **Damaged insulation** on leads, tools, or fixed wiring
- **No RCD protection**
- **Metal tools and ladders**
- **Confined or cramped positions** where you cannot avoid contact

## Overhead Powerlines

**Overhead line contact is the leading cause of electrical fatality in construction.**

### Why It Happens

- Scaffold tubes, ladders, and lengths of timber being carried vertically
- Elevating work platforms and scaffolds erected under lines
- Excavators, cranes, tippers and concrete pumps with booms raised
- Delivery trucks tipping under lines
- Roof work adjacent to a service line
- **Lines being invisible against a bright sky** — they genuinely do not register visually

### No Go Zones and Approach Distances

Electricity does not require contact. **At higher voltages it will arc across an air gap**, so approach distances are set well back from the conductor.

**Approach distances depend on the voltage and on who is working.** They are set by state electrical safety regulations and the network operator, and they differ between:
- An ordinary person or worker
- A person working under specified conditions with a safety observer
- Authorised or accredited persons

**[VERIFY: no go zone and minimum approach distances are prescribed by state electrical safety legislation, the network operator's requirements, and the relevant code of practice. They vary by voltage and by jurisdiction. Obtain the applicable distances from the network operator and the site's documentation before any work near overhead lines — do not rely on a general figure.]**

**What you can rely on without looking anything up:**
- **You cannot tell the voltage by looking at a line.** Assume the highest until confirmed
- **Assume every line is live**, including one that appears disused
- **Insulated-looking covering is not insulation.** Weatherproofing is not electrical insulation, and it degrades
- **If you are unsure of the distance, stop and ask.** The number is obtainable in minutes

### Controls

**In order:**

1. **Eliminate** — do the work away from the line, or have the line **de-energised or relocated** by the network operator. This is the only complete control and it is available more often than people assume
2. **Isolation** — have the network operator **cover or insulate** the line, or install physical barriers
3. **Engineering** — tiger tails (line markers) to increase visibility, height-limiting devices on plant, goal posts and warning markers at approaches
4. **Administrative** — a **safety observer** whose only job is watching the clearance and calling a stop; permits; exclusion zones; briefing every person on site including delivery drivers
5. **PPE** — of essentially no value against overhead line contact

**Tiger tails are visibility markers, not insulation.** This is a genuinely dangerous and widespread misunderstanding. A tiger tail makes a line easier to see. It does not make it safe to touch and it provides no protection whatsoever.

### The Safety Observer

Where the approach distance cannot be maintained by clearance alone, a **safety observer** is required.

- Their **only duty** is watching the clearance
- They must have a clear view of the conductor and the plant or object
- They must have **authority to stop the work** and use it
- They must be positioned safely and not be part of the work

## Underground Cables

### The Hazard

An underground cable struck by an excavator bucket, a jackhammer, a post hole auger, or a pick produces an **arc flash** — an explosive release of energy generating temperatures far above those needed to cause severe burns, plus molten metal, blast pressure and intense ultraviolet light.

**The operator may be burned without ever touching the cable.**

### Controls

**Before any ground disturbance:**

1. **Before You Dig enquiry** — obtain the plans
2. **Physically locate** the services with an electromagnetic locator, and where required a qualified locator
3. **Mark** on the ground — **red for electrical**
4. **Non-destructive excavation** within the exclusion distance — vacuum excavation or careful hand digging
5. **Support and protect** exposed cables; do not use them as a step or a lever

**Plans alone are never authority to dig.** They show approximate positions and are frequently inaccurate. See RIIWHS201E in the civil pack for the full four-step process.

### Concealed Cables in Existing Buildings

Directly relevant to carpentry in renovation work.

**Before penetrating any wall, floor or ceiling in an existing structure:**
- **Use a cable and metal detector.** They are inexpensive and they work
- **Consider what is likely to be there.** Cables run vertically and horizontally from switches, sockets and light fittings. **The zone directly above and below a socket or switch is the highest-risk area in any wall**
- **Consider the fixing depth** — a 90mm nail into a 45mm stud reaches well beyond the timber
- **Where the risk cannot be excluded**, have the circuit isolated by a licensed electrician before penetrating

**Nail guns and cables are a recurring and severe combination.** A nail through a live cable can energise the tool, the timber, and the operator.

## Portable Electrical Equipment on Site

### RCD Protection

A **Residual Current Device** detects an imbalance between active and neutral current — indicating current leaking to earth, potentially through a person — and disconnects rapidly.

**All portable electrical equipment on a construction site must be protected by an RCD.**

- Test the RCD before use, using its test button
- An RCD that will not trip on test is faulty — **do not use the circuit**
- **An RCD reduces the severity of a shock. It does not prevent one.** You still receive current until it trips

### Test and Tag

Portable electrical equipment and leads on construction sites must be **inspected, tested and tagged** at required intervals by a competent person.

**[VERIFY: test and tag intervals for construction and demolition sites are specified in AS/NZS 3012 and by state regulators. Confirm the applicable interval — construction site intervals are shorter than general workplace intervals.]**

**Before every use, regardless of the tag:**
- Check the lead for cuts, abrasion, crushing, exposed conductors
- Check the plug and socket for damage, cracks, bent or missing pins
- Check the tool housing for cracks
- Check the tag is present and current

**A current tag on a lead damaged this morning means nothing.** The tag records a past inspection; your eyes are the current one.

### Leads and Extension Cords

- **Keep leads out of water** and off wet ground
- **Do not run leads through doorways** where they will be crushed, or across access ways where they are a trip hazard and will be damaged
- **Elevate leads** where practicable — over a shoulder height support rather than across the floor
- **Do not join leads** with makeshift connections
- **Fully uncoil a reel before use.** A coiled reel under load overheats and can melt the insulation
- **Never use a domestic-grade lead** on a construction site
- **Do not remove the earth pin** from a plug. This is done occasionally to fit an old socket and it removes the primary protection

### Damaged Equipment

**Tag it out and remove it from service.** Not "use it carefully", not "it's only got a small nick".

A damaged lead is the most common cause of electric shock on construction sites and it is entirely preventable at the point of inspection.

## Working Near Electrical Installations

### Switchboards and Meter Boxes

- **Do not open** a switchboard or meter box. Only licensed electrical workers work inside them
- Maintain clear access — **an obstructed switchboard is both a safety hazard and a compliance breach**
- Do not store material against or in front of them
- Do not work in a way that could damage the board or its cabling

### Temporary Builder's Supply

The temporary supply serving a construction site is an electrical installation.

- Do not modify it, add to it, or interfere with it
- Report damage immediately
- Do not overload it

### Wet Weather

**Stop electrical work in wet conditions unless the equipment and installation are rated and protected for it.**

Water on leads, in extension sockets, on tool housings, and on the ground transforms the risk profile. Battery tools are the practical answer in wet conditions.

## If Contact Occurs

**This is the most important section of this unit, and the correct response is counterintuitive.**

### Do NOT Touch the Person

**A person in contact with a live conductor is energised.** Touching them puts you in the circuit and produces a second casualty.

**The let-go threshold means they cannot release themselves.** They are not choosing to hold on.

### The Sequence

**1. Do not approach.** Particularly with overhead line contact — the ground around a contact point may be energised through **step potential**, where voltage gradients across the ground can be lethal to a person walking toward the contact.

**2. Isolate the supply if you can do so safely and immediately** — the switchboard, the RCD, the isolator, the plug.

**For overhead lines, you cannot isolate.** Do not attempt it.

**3. Call 000.** State that it is an electrical incident and whether it involves overhead lines, so the network operator can be tasked.

**4. Keep everyone away.** Establish a wide exclusion zone. For overhead line contact, well back — the safe distance is larger than intuition suggests.

**5. If the supply is confirmed isolated**, and only then, provide first aid. Commence CPR if required and if you are trained.

**6. If a vehicle or plant has contacted an overhead line:**
- **The occupant must stay inside.** The vehicle is generally the safest place — the tyres and the structure provide a degree of protection
- **Do not touch the vehicle**
- **Wait for the network operator to confirm isolation**
- **If the vehicle is on fire and evacuation is unavoidable**, the occupant must **jump clear without touching the vehicle and the ground simultaneously**, land with feet together, and shuffle or hop away keeping both feet together and in contact — to avoid bridging a voltage gradient across the ground with two separated feet

**[VERIFY: emergency response procedures for overhead line contact against your network operator's guidance and the applicable code of practice.]**

### Electric Shock Requires Medical Assessment

**Anyone who receives an electric shock must be medically assessed, even if they appear unaffected.**

Electric shock can cause **delayed cardiac arrhythmia** — a disturbance of heart rhythm that may develop hours after the event. Internal burns along the current path may also be present with minimal external injury.

**"I'm fine, it just gave me a jolt" is not a clinical assessment.** Insist.

### Reporting

**Electric shock is a notifiable dangerous incident** under WHS legislation and must be reported to the regulator immediately. The scene must be preserved. There will also be an electrical safety regulator notification requirement in most jurisdictions.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are on a two-storey residential renovation. The crew needs to erect a mobile scaffold along the rear wall to install new fascia and cladding.*

*Running along the rear boundary, approximately 3 metres out from the wall and roughly 6 metres above ground, is an overhead service line. You cannot tell whether it is the property's service connection or a distribution line.*

*Your leading hand says: "It's only the house service, it's insulated, and we'll be under it anyway. Just be careful when you're standing the frames up."*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**I stop and we get the line identified and dealt with before any scaffold frame is lifted.** Three separate statements in that instruction are wrong, and each one on its own would be enough to stop the job.

---

**"It's only the house service"**

**Nobody on site can determine the voltage of a line by looking at it.** I cannot, and neither can my leading hand.

A rear boundary line at 6 metres could be a low-voltage service connection, a low-voltage distribution main, or a higher-voltage line. The visual differences are not reliable to an untrained eye, and the approach distances differ significantly between them.

**The default position is to assume the highest voltage until the network operator confirms otherwise.** That confirmation is a phone call.

**And even a low-voltage service connection will kill you.** Mains voltage through a scaffold tube into a person standing on damp ground is lethal. "Only the house service" is not a reassurance — domestic mains kills people every year.

---

**"It's insulated"**

**This is the most dangerous statement in the instruction.**

The covering on an overhead conductor is **weatherproofing, not electrical insulation.** It is there to protect the conductor from moisture and abrasion. It is not rated to prevent contact, it degrades with age and UV exposure, and it cannot be relied on at all.

**If tiger tails have been fitted** — the orange or yellow markers sometimes seen on lines near work areas — those are **visibility markers**. They make the line easier to see. They provide **no insulation and no protection whatsoever.** Believing otherwise is a documented cause of fatalities.

**There is no such thing as a safely touchable overhead line.**

---

**"We'll be under it anyway"**

Electricity does not require contact. **At sufficient voltage it arcs across an air gap**, which is why the prescribed distance is a **no go zone** measured from the conductor rather than a rule about touching.

And "under it" is not where the risk is. The risk is in the **erection**:

- Scaffold frames are **lifted and swung vertically**. A frame being stood up passes through a much larger envelope than its final position
- **Standard scaffold frames are steel.** Excellent conductors
- A 3 metre horizontal offset and 6 metre height gives very little margin when a person is lifting a frame overhead, on uneven ground, possibly in wind
- **Mobile scaffold gets moved.** A tower erected in a safe position and then wheeled along the wall may travel directly under or beside the line
- Working platforms put people **higher**, reducing the clearance further as the scaffold goes up

**"Just be careful" is not a control measure.** It transfers a fatal risk to individual concentration during a two-handed lifting operation.

---

**What I actually do**

**1. Stop before any frame is lifted.** Not partway through. The hazard exists from the first lift.

**2. Contact the network operator and get the line identified.**

They will tell us the voltage, the required no go zone and minimum approach distances, and what options are available. This is a routine enquiry for them and it is usually answered quickly.

**3. Ask for the line to be de-energised or covered.**

**This is the primary control and it is available far more often than crews assume.** Depending on the line and the network operator:
- **De-energising** for the duration of the work — most likely with a service connection to the property itself
- **Temporary disconnection** of the service, which is often straightforward
- **Insulated covering (tiger tail sleeving or line covers)** installed by the network operator — note this is different from a visibility marker and is installed by them, not by us
- **Temporary relocation**

There is usually a cost and a lead time, and both are trivial against the alternative.

**4. If the line stays live, obtain the actual approach distance and plan to it.**

- Get the prescribed distance for that voltage **in writing**
- Assess whether the scaffold can be erected and moved while maintaining it, **including during the lift**
- If it cannot, the work does not proceed in that configuration

**5. If the distance cannot be maintained by clearance alone, a safety observer is required.**

Someone whose **only** job is watching the clearance, with a clear view of both the conductor and the frames, and with authority to stop the lift. Not someone also helping lift.

**6. Consider an alternative method entirely.**

This is worth raising because it may be simpler than any of the above:
- **An elevating work platform** positioned to keep clear of the line
- **Working from inside** the roof space or from a different aspect
- **Sequencing** — doing the section under the line at a time when the service can be temporarily disconnected

**7. Brief everyone, including delivery drivers.**

Anyone bringing a truck, a tipper or a concrete pump into that yard needs to know about the line. **Plant with a raised boom or tray is the other main contact mechanism** and drivers arriving on site have no knowledge of it.

**8. Physical markers** — goal posts at the approach, and marking on the ground indicating the no go zone, so the boundary is visible rather than remembered.

---

**What I say**

*"I'm not lifting frames under that until we know what it is. We can't tell the voltage by looking, that covering is weatherproofing not insulation, and the risk is in standing the frames up — not where they end up. Can we get [network operator] out to identify it? If it's the house service they can probably disconnect it for a day, and that solves the whole thing."*

**Offering the de-energising option is what makes this a solvable problem rather than an argument.** It is usually cheap, it is often quick, and it removes the hazard entirely rather than managing it.

---

**If directed to proceed**

I decline, and I exercise the **right to cease unsafe work** — I have a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk, and that right is legally protected.

I would escalate to the site supervisor or builder, document the direction and my response, and **tell the rest of the crew why**, because the person lifting the other end of the frame is equally exposed and is entitled to know.

---

**The thing I would want understood**

Every element of my leading hand's reasoning is a genuine, widely held belief on residential sites: that a house service is low risk, that the covering is insulation, and that being underneath is safe.

**All three are wrong, and the combination is exactly the profile of an overhead line electrocution.** These deaths do not happen to people taking obvious risks. They happen to people who believed the line was safe.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student takes the instruction apart claim by claim and demolishes each on technically correct grounds. The identification of the covering as weatherproofing rather than insulation, and the explicit distinction between a tiger tail as a visibility marker and network-installed insulated covering, addresses the single most lethal misconception in this area. The analysis of *where* the risk actually sits — in the vertical envelope swept during erection, not the final position, compounded by steel frames, uneven ground, wind, mobile scaffold being relocated, and platforms raising people closer — demonstrates genuine understanding rather than rule recitation. Correctly identifying that the voltage cannot be determined visually and that the default is to assume the highest is the right professional posture. The controls are worked in hierarchy order with de-energising correctly identified as primary and realistically available, and the observation that a service connection to the property itself is often straightforward to disconnect makes the recommendation actionable. Briefing delivery drivers is an unprompted and genuinely important addition, since plant with a raised tray is a major contact mechanism and arriving drivers have no site knowledge. The closing observation that all three of the leading hand's beliefs are commonly held and all three are wrong identifies precisely why this hazard kills competent tradespeople. Strong Competent.
