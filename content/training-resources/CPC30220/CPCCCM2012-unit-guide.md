## What This Unit Is About

Falls from height are the leading cause of death in the Australian construction industry. They are also among the most preventable — the controls are well established, well understood, and frequently not applied because they take time.

CPCCCM2012 asks you to demonstrate that you can identify fall risks, select and apply the correct controls in the correct order, use fall protection equipment properly, and refuse work where adequate controls are absent.

For carpenters this is not a peripheral unit. Roof framing, upper-floor framing, and external cladding all involve sustained work at height.

---

## When Fall Protection Is Required

The model WHS Regulations require a PCBU to manage risks of a fall **from any height** where a person could be injured, and impose specific duties where a fall of **more than 2 metres** is possible.

**Work at height requiring fall protection includes:**
- Any work where a person could fall more than 2 metres
- Work on a roof — **at any height**, because roof edges, fragile surfaces, and slope create fall risk independent of the drop
- Work near an unprotected edge, penetration, void, or opening
- Work on or near a fragile surface (skylights, old fibre cement sheeting, corroded roofing)
- Work on a structure being erected or demolished
- Work on plant or a structure in an elevated position

**Do not rely on the 2 metre figure as a threshold below which nothing is required.** Falls from under 2 metres cause serious head and spinal injuries regularly. The obligation is to manage the risk of any fall that could cause injury.

**[VERIFY: Fall height triggers and specific duties vary between jurisdictions. Confirm the current requirement in your state's WHS Regulations.]**

## The Fall Prevention Hierarchy

This is the core content of the unit and the structure every assessment answer should follow. Controls are applied in this order, and a lower-level control is only acceptable where higher levels are not reasonably practicable.

### Level 1 — Eliminate the need to work at height

The only control that removes the risk entirely.

**Carpentry examples:**
- Prefabricate wall frames and roof trusses on the ground, then crane them into position
- Assemble components at ground level before lifting
- Use tools with extension poles to work from the ground
- Design out the need — specify pre-finished materials that do not require working at height to install

**This is the control most often skipped and most often available.** Before accepting that work must occur at height, ask whether any part of it can be done on the ground.

### Level 2 — Passive fall prevention

Physical controls that protect a worker **without requiring them to do anything**. This is the critical distinction — passive protection works even if the worker forgets, is distracted, or is unconscious.

**Examples:**
- **Guardrails / edge protection** — top rail, mid rail, and toeboard around the perimeter, around voids, and at open edges
- **Perimeter scaffolding** with edge protection
- **Safety mesh** installed under roof sheeting
- **Covers over penetrations and voids** — securely fixed and clearly marked, capable of bearing load
- **Fully decked working platforms** with edge protection

**Passive controls are the preferred practical solution for most carpentry work.** They do not depend on training, discipline, or correct fitting.

### Level 3 — Work positioning systems

Systems that control the worker's position so a fall cannot occur.

**Examples:**
- **Elevating Work Platforms (EWPs)** — scissor lifts, boom lifts
- **Mast climbers**
- **Travel restraint systems** — a harness and lanyard adjusted so the worker **physically cannot reach** the fall edge

**Travel restraint prevents the fall. Fall arrest catches you after it.** Travel restraint is the superior of the two and should be chosen where the work allows it.

### Level 4 — Fall arrest systems

Systems that do not prevent a fall but stop it before impact.

**Examples:**
- **Harness and shock-absorbing lanyard** attached to a rated anchor
- **Inertia reel / self-retracting lifeline**
- **Safety nets**
- **Catch platforms**

**Fall arrest is the last resort, not a default.** Using it requires:
- A rated anchor point capable of taking the arrest force
- Adequate **clearance distance** below (see below)
- A **rescue plan** in place before work begins
- Training in fitting, inspecting, and using the equipment

### Level 5 — Administrative controls

Procedures, training, signage, exclusion zones, supervision. These support the higher controls but **do not protect anyone on their own.**

"Be careful near the edge" is not a control measure.

## Fall Arrest — What You Must Understand

### Clearance Distance

The single most misunderstood element of fall arrest. **If there is not enough clear space below you, the system will not stop you before you hit the ground — you will simply hit it while wearing a harness.**

Required clearance is the sum of:

| Component | Typical contribution |
|---|---|
| **Lanyard length** | 2m for a standard lanyard |
| **Shock absorber deployment** | Up to 1.75m when it tears open |
| **Height of the worker below the attachment** | Approximately 1.5m (D-ring to feet) |
| **Safety margin** | Minimum 1m clearance to the surface below |

**This means a standard 2m shock-absorbing lanyard can require in the order of 6.5 metres of clear fall distance below the anchor point.**

**[VERIFY: Exact clearance calculations depend on the specific equipment. Always use the manufacturer's stated free-fall and deployment figures for the equipment in use.]**

**Practical consequence:** on a two-storey residential frame, there is frequently **not enough clearance** for a standard lanyard system. Options are a shorter lanyard, an inertia reel (which arrests much faster and over a shorter distance), an anchor point placed higher, or — better — using travel restraint or passive protection instead.

### Anchor Points

- Must be **rated for fall arrest** — commonly a minimum of 15kN for a single person, though this varies with the standard and system
- Must be installed by a competent person and inspected
- **A structural member is not automatically an anchor point.** Tying off to a truss, a purlin, a piece of scaffold, or a handrail without engineering assessment is a serious error
- Anchor as **high as practicable** — a higher anchor reduces free-fall distance and swing

**[VERIFY: Anchor point load ratings against AS/NZS 1891 and the relevant equipment standard.]**

### Swing Fall (Pendulum Effect)

If you fall while positioned to the side of your anchor point, you swing in an arc. This can:
- Cause you to strike a structure, wall, or the ground during the swing
- Substantially increase the effective fall distance

**Control:** work as close to directly below your anchor as practicable, or use a horizontal lifeline that allows the attachment point to travel with you.

### Suspension Trauma — Why a Rescue Plan Is Mandatory

A person suspended motionless in a harness after a fall can develop **suspension trauma** (orthostatic intolerance). Leg straps restrict venous return; blood pools in the legs; the brain becomes under-perfused. **This can cause loss of consciousness and death in as little as 10–20 minutes.**

**Therefore:**
- **A rescue plan must exist before fall arrest work begins** — not be improvised after a fall
- The plan must identify who performs the rescue, with what equipment, and how, and it must be achievable within minutes
- "Call 000" is not by itself an adequate rescue plan — emergency service response time will frequently exceed the safe suspension window
- **Suspension trauma relief straps** allow a suspended person to stand in the straps and relieve leg pressure, buying time
- Never work in fall arrest alone

**[VERIFY: Suspension trauma onset times cited in guidance material vary. Refer to current Safe Work Australia and state regulator guidance.]**

## Ladders

Ladders cause a very high proportion of falls, largely because they are treated as work platforms when they are **access equipment**.

### The Rules

- **Ladders are for access and short-duration light work only.** Sustained carpentry work should be done from a platform, not a ladder
- **Do not use a ladder** where you need both hands for the task, where you must reach out from the ladder, where you must carry materials up, or where you would work from it for extended periods

### Correct Set-Up

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Angle** | 4:1 ratio — 1 unit out for every 4 units of height (approximately 75°) |
| **Extension above landing** | At least 1 metre above the landing point when used for access |
| **Securing** | Tied off at the top, or footed at the base, or otherwise secured against movement |
| **Footing** | Firm, level, non-slip surface |
| **Condition** | Inspected before use — no cracked or missing rungs, no damaged stiles, no makeshift repairs |
| **Rating** | Industrial rated (minimum 120kg) — **domestic ladders are not permitted on construction sites** |
| **Three points of contact** | Two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand, maintained at all times |
| **Positioning** | Do not overreach — keep your belt buckle within the stiles. Move the ladder instead |
| **Metal ladders** | Must not be used near live electrical work — use non-conductive fibreglass |

### Stepladders
- Fully open and locked
- **Never stand on the top two rungs or the top plate**
- Not to be used as a straight ladder leaned against a surface

## Scaffolding

### Types

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Mobile scaffold (tower)** | Wheeled tower; common in residential carpentry |
| **Modular / system scaffold** | Component-based, erected by scaffolders |
| **Frame scaffold** | Prefabricated frames and braces |
| **Trestle scaffold** | Planks on trestles; low-height only |

### High Risk Work Licence

**Erecting, altering, or dismantling scaffold from which a person or object could fall more than 4 metres requires a High Risk Work Licence (Scaffolding).**

Mobile scaffold below that threshold can generally be erected by a competent person without a licence — but "competent" means trained, not merely willing.

**[VERIFY: High Risk Work Licence thresholds and classes against your state regulator's current requirements.]**

### Using Scaffold Safely

- **Check the scaffold tag** before use. A green tag means it has been inspected and is safe to use; a red or incomplete tag means **do not use it**
- **Never modify scaffold yourself.** Removing a guardrail, plank, or brace "just for a minute" to get access is a common cause of falls. Only a competent scaffolder alters scaffold
- Ensure full decking, edge protection (top rail, mid rail, toeboard), and safe access (internal ladder or stair, not climbing the frame)
- **Mobile scaffold:** wheels locked before use; **never move it while someone is on it**; check for overhead powerlines and floor obstructions before moving; observe the height-to-base ratio limit and use outriggers where required

## Elevating Work Platforms (EWPs)

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Scissor lift** | Vertical travel only; larger platform |
| **Boom lift (knuckle/telescopic)** | Reach over obstacles; greater height |
| **Vertical lift / personnel lift** | Compact, single person |

**Licensing:** operating a boom-type EWP with a boom length of **11 metres or more** requires a High Risk Work Licence (WP). Below that, and for scissor lifts, operator training and familiarisation with the specific machine is required.

**[VERIFY: EWP licensing threshold — confirm current requirement with your state regulator.]**

**Safe use:**
- Pre-start inspection — controls, emergency lowering, tyres, outriggers, guardrails
- Assess ground conditions — EWPs overturn on soft ground, slopes, and unseen voids
- Check for **overhead powerlines** and observe minimum approach distances
- **Wear a harness with a short restraint lanyard in a boom lift**, attached to the designated anchor. This is to prevent being catapulted from the basket if the boom is struck or jolts — a documented cause of fatality
- Do not climb on or over the guardrails to gain extra height
- Do not exceed the rated capacity, including tools and materials
- Never use an EWP as a crane or to transfer between the basket and a structure unless the machine and procedure specifically permit it

## Roof Work — Carpentry Specific

Roof work is where carpenters face the highest fall exposure.

**Key hazards:**
- **Unprotected perimeter edges**
- **Penetrations** — skylights, vents, stairwells, service openings
- **Fragile surfaces** — skylights and old fibre cement sheeting will not bear a person's weight and are frequently indistinguishable from sound sheeting when dusty or painted
- **Slope** — pitch increases slip risk substantially; a roof becomes hazardous well before it becomes visibly steep
- **Weather** — wet, frosty, or windy conditions dramatically increase risk. **Wind is particularly dangerous when handling sheet material**, which acts as a sail

**Controls, in order:**
1. Do as much as possible at ground level — assemble trusses on the ground and crane them up
2. **Perimeter edge protection** (guardrails or scaffold) around the roof edge — the standard passive control
3. **Safety mesh** under the sheeting, which catches a person who falls through
4. **Cover and mark all penetrations** securely
5. Travel restraint or fall arrest only where the above are not reasonably practicable
6. Stop work in unsuitable weather

## Falling Objects

Working at height creates risk for people **below**.

- **Toeboards** on platforms and scaffold prevent tools and material being kicked off the edge
- **Tool lanyards** secure hand tools to the worker or the platform
- **Exclusion zones** below the work area, with barriers and signage
- **Debris nets or catch platforms** where required
- **Do not stack material at the edge** of a platform
- **Never throw material down** — lower it or use a chute

## Equipment Inspection and Maintenance

Fall protection equipment is life safety equipment.

**Before every use, inspect:**
- **Harness** — webbing for cuts, fraying, abrasion, chemical damage, UV degradation; stitching intact; D-rings undistorted and free of cracks; buckles functioning
- **Lanyard** — webbing or rope condition; **shock absorber pack not deployed or torn**; connectors functioning
- **Connectors and karabiners** — gates close and lock fully; no distortion or cracks
- **Inertia reel** — webbing retracts smoothly; locks when pulled sharply; housing undamaged

**Mandatory removal from service:**
- **Any equipment that has arrested a fall must be immediately removed from service** and not used again unless a competent person certifies it. Shock absorbers are single-use by design
- Any equipment with damage, missing labels, or an expired inspection

**Periodic inspection** by a competent person is required at intervals set by the standard and manufacturer, with records maintained.

**Storage:** clean, dry, out of direct sunlight, away from chemicals. UV degradation weakens webbing without obvious visual change.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are installing fascia and barge boards on a new two-storey house. The eaves are approximately 5.5 metres above a concrete driveway. There is no scaffold on site. Your supervisor offers you a harness and a 2 metre shock-absorbing lanyard, and says you can anchor to the roof trusses and work off an extension ladder.*

*Assess this proposal and state what you would do.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I would not do the work as proposed. There are three separate problems, and at least two of them mean the system as described would not save me.

---

**Problem 1 — There is not enough clearance for that lanyard. The system would not work.**

This is the decisive issue and it is worth working through, because it is not obvious.

A 2 metre shock-absorbing lanyard requires clearance made up of:

| Component | Distance |
|---|---|
| Lanyard length | 2.0 m |
| Shock absorber deployment | up to 1.75 m |
| My height below the D-ring | approx. 1.5 m |
| Safety margin | minimum 1.0 m |
| **Total required** | **approx. 6.25 m** |

I would be working at the eaves, roughly 5.5 metres above the driveway, and anchored to the trusses — so the anchor is only slightly above me, not far above.

**The available clearance is less than the system requires.** If I fell, the shock absorber would deploy and I would still strike the concrete driveway before the system arrested me — while wearing a harness that gave me the false confidence to be there in the first place.

**A fall arrest system that cannot arrest the fall within the available distance is not protection. It is decoration.**

If fall arrest were genuinely the only option, I would need an **inertia reel** rather than a shock-absorbing lanyard — an inertia reel locks within a much shorter distance and substantially reduces the clearance requirement. But that is not the first question here.

---

**Problem 2 — The anchor point is not verified.**

"Anchor to the trusses" is not an anchor point specification. A fall arrest anchor must be **rated** — commonly a minimum of 15kN for one person — and installed or assessed by a competent person.

A roof truss is a structural member engineered to carry distributed roof loads downward. It is **not** engineered for the sudden concentrated shock load of arresting a falling person, and it is generally fixed with connections designed for gravity loads, not uplift or lateral shock.

Tying a lanyard around a truss and calling it an anchor is one of the most common serious errors in residential construction. I would need a rated anchor, installed and certified, or a proprietary temporary roof anchor system.

---

**Problem 3 — A ladder is the wrong equipment for this work.**

Fascia and barge board installation is **sustained work requiring both hands** — holding and positioning a long board, levelling it, and fixing it. That is not what a ladder is for.

Ladders are access equipment suitable for short-duration light tasks. This work involves:
- Both hands occupied
- Handling long, awkward material
- Reaching laterally along the run
- Extended time at height

Every one of those is a reason not to use a ladder. Working from a ladder here would mean overreaching, which is precisely how ladder falls happen.

---

**Problem 4 — No rescue plan.**

Fall arrest work requires a rescue plan **in place before the work starts**. If I fell and was left suspended, suspension trauma can cause loss of consciousness within roughly 10–20 minutes. With nobody on site trained or equipped to retrieve me, and emergency services response time likely exceeding that window, the plan would effectively be to hope.

Working alone in fall arrest is not acceptable in any case.

---

**What I would propose instead — working up the hierarchy:**

**1. Eliminate where possible.** Fascia and barge can be partly prepared at ground level — cut to length, joined, primed, and pre-drilled. This does not remove the need to be at height to fix it, but it reduces the time spent there and the amount of handling required aloft.

**2. Passive protection — the right answer here.** **Perimeter scaffold with edge protection** is the appropriate control for eaves work on a two-storey house. It provides a stable full working platform, protects passively so it works regardless of what I do, allows both hands free, gives somewhere to place material, and removes the fall risk rather than managing its consequences. Scaffold on a two-storey residential job is normal, expected, and priced into most builds.

**3. Work positioning — the practical alternative.** If scaffold cannot be erected, an **elevating work platform** would be appropriate. It positions me at the work with guardrails around me, and the driveway provides firm level ground for it. In a boom-type EWP I would wear a harness with a short restraint lanyard to the designated anchor.

**4. Fall arrest — only if neither of the above is reasonably practicable**, and then only with a rated certified anchor, an inertia reel rather than a shock-absorbing lanyard given the clearance, a documented rescue plan, and never alone.

---

**What I would say to my supervisor:**

*"I can't work off a ladder for the fascia — I need both hands and I'll be reaching along the run. And that lanyard won't work at 5.5 metres over concrete: with the shock absorber deployment and my height, it needs over six metres of clearance and we haven't got it. We'd also need a certified anchor, not just a truss. Can we get scaffold up, or a scissor lift on the driveway? The driveway's flat and hard so a lift would sit fine."*

I am proposing solutions, not just refusing. A supervisor under schedule pressure needs a workable alternative, and both of mine are readily available.

**If directed to proceed regardless**, I would exercise my **right to cease unsafe work** under the WHS Act. I have a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk — a 5.5 metre fall onto concrete with a fall arrest system that mathematically cannot arrest me in the available distance. That right is legally protected and I cannot be lawfully disadvantaged for exercising it. I would notify the supervisor, escalate to the site HSR or principal contractor, and document the direction and my response.

---

**The point I would want understood:**

Being offered a harness can feel like being offered safety. In this case the harness would have made me *more* likely to be killed, because it would have persuaded me to work in a position I would otherwise have refused — while providing no actual protection. **The equipment does not make the work safe; the system does, and only if the numbers work.**

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding — one of the strongest possible responses to this scenario. The clearance calculation is the critical insight and the student works it through explicitly rather than asserting it, arriving at the correct conclusion that the offered system is mathematically incapable of arresting the fall. The identification of an unrated truss as an inadequate anchor, with a correct explanation of *why* (engineered for distributed gravity loads, not concentrated shock loads), demonstrates genuine structural understanding rather than rule recall. The ladder critique correctly identifies the specific features of fascia work — both hands, lateral reach, sustained duration — that make it unsuitable. The rescue plan and suspension trauma point is correctly raised. Most importantly, the student works *up* the hierarchy to propose two readily available practical alternatives rather than simply refusing, and pitches the conversation to a supervisor under schedule pressure in a way likely to succeed. The closing observation — that being given a harness can increase risk by producing false confidence without real protection — is the exact insight this unit exists to instil. Strong Competent.
