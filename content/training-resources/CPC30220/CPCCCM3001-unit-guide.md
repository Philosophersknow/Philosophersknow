## What This Unit Is About

An elevating work platform is a fall prevention control. It is also a machine that can overturn, crush, or eject its occupant.

For carpenters, EWPs are the standard answer to eaves work, external cladding, fascia, upper-level framing and roof access — and they are the right answer, because they provide a stable guarded platform rather than managing the consequences of a fall. But they introduce their own hazard set, and the incidents are consistent and predictable: overturning on soft or sloping ground, contact with overhead lines, crushing between the platform and a structure, and ejection.

CPCCCM3001 asks you to select the right machine, inspect it, set it up on suitable ground, operate it safely, and know its limits.

---

## Types of EWP

| Type | Movement | Typical carpentry use |
|---|---|---|
| **Scissor lift** | Vertical only | Interior work, level hard surfaces, ceiling and bulkhead work |
| **Vertical / personnel lift** | Vertical, single person, compact | Interior fit-out, confined spaces |
| **Boom lift — telescopic** | Vertical and horizontal reach in a straight line | Reaching over obstacles, high external work |
| **Boom lift — articulating (knuckle)** | Reach up and over obstacles | Working around structures, awkward access |
| **Trailer-mounted boom** | Towed, then set on outriggers | Residential external work |
| **Truck-mounted boom** | Vehicle mounted | Street work, high reach |
| **Vertical mast lift** | Vertical, small footprint | Interior, light duty |

**Choosing the machine:**

| Question | Determines |
|---|---|
| What **height** must the platform reach? | Working height, allowing for reach from the platform |
| Does it need to reach **over or around** something? | Articulating boom rather than telescopic or scissor |
| What is the **ground** like? | Rough terrain, four-wheel drive, or hard-surface only |
| What **load** — people, tools, material? | Rated capacity, including everything on the platform |
| **Indoors?** | Electric rather than diesel; floor loading limits |
| What is **overhead**? | Powerlines, structures, ceiling height |

**A scissor lift on soft or sloping ground is one of the most common EWP overturn scenarios.** Most scissor lifts are designed for firm level surfaces and have very limited tolerance for slope.

## Licensing and Competency

**High Risk Work Licence:** operating a **boom-type EWP with a boom length of 11 metres or more** requires a High Risk Work Licence (class WP).

**Below 11 metres, and for scissor lifts:** no HRWL is required, but the operator must be **trained and competent**, and **familiarised with the specific machine**. Competency is not transferable between machine types without familiarisation.

**Additionally required in practice:**
- **EWP operator training** — commonly a Yellow Card or equivalent industry scheme
- **Machine-specific familiarisation** — controls differ significantly between makes
- **Working at heights** competency
- **Emergency rescue and lowering** training

**[VERIFY: High Risk Work Licence classes, the 11 metre boom threshold, and any additional state-specific requirements — confirm with your state WHS regulator. Industry card schemes are separate from statutory licensing.]**

**A licence is not a familiarisation.** A licensed operator on an unfamiliar machine must still be familiarised with its controls, its emergency lowering, and its limitations before use.

## Pre-Operational Inspection

Conducted before each shift and recorded.

### Machine — Powered Off

- **Structure** — cracks, deformation, damaged welds, bent members
- **Platform and guardrails** — top rail, mid rail, toeboard present and secure; gate closes and latches
- **Anchor points** for harness — present, undamaged, and identified
- **Tyres or tracks** — condition, pressure, damage
- **Outriggers and stabilisers** — condition, pins, pads present
- **Hydraulics** — hoses for chafing, cracking, weeping; cylinders for leaks and scoring
- **Fluid levels** — hydraulic, engine, fuel, coolant, battery
- **Chains, cables, wear pads** on scissor mechanisms
- **Decals and rating plate** — legible. **The rated capacity must be readable**
- **Manual on board**
- **Log book and current inspection records**
- **No damage from a previous incident** that has not been reported

### Machine — Powered On

- **Ground controls** operate correctly
- **Platform controls** operate correctly
- **Emergency stop** at both ground and platform
- **Emergency lowering** — locate it and confirm it functions
- **Horn**
- **Warning lights and alarms** — travel alarm, tilt alarm, overload alarm
- **Interlocks** — pothole protection, tilt cut-out, outrigger interlocks
- **Levelling** where fitted

**Any defect means the machine is tagged out and not used.** Particularly guardrails, interlocks, emergency lowering and the tilt alarm — these are the systems that prevent the fatal outcomes.

## Site Assessment and Set-Up

**Most EWP fatalities are set-up failures, not operating failures.**

### Ground Conditions

**The single most important assessment.**

- **Firm, level, and capable of bearing the load.** An EWP concentrates several tonnes onto a small footprint
- **Check for voids and soft ground** — recently backfilled trenches, service trenches, pits, drains, sullage, garden beds
- **Backfilled ground is the classic failure.** A trench backfilled last week, looking like solid ground, will not carry an outrigger. **EWPs have overturned into their own site's service trench**
- **Check slope** against the machine's rating. Most scissor lifts tolerate very little
- **Wet or saturated ground** loses bearing capacity
- **Use spreader pads** under outriggers to distribute the load. On soft or questionable ground, use larger pads
- **Check for underground services and structures** that could collapse — basements, tanks, culverts, cellars, pits

### Overhead

- **Powerlines** — identify them, obtain the approach distance, and treat as live. See CPCCCM2010. **Overhead line contact is a leading cause of EWP fatality**
- **Structures, beams, awnings, signage, trees**
- **Other plant** operating in the area
- **Height limits** in car parks and under structures

### Around

- **Pedestrians and traffic** — exclusion zone, barriers, spotter or traffic control as required
- **Crush points** — the space between the platform and a structure. **This is where operators are killed by their own machine**
- **Other trades** working above or below
- **Access route** for the machine to reach the position

### Weather

- **Wind is the limiting factor.** Every EWP has a **maximum wind speed rating**, and the platform, occupants and any material act as a sail. **Sheet material in a platform in wind is extremely hazardous**
- **Do not operate above the rated wind speed.** Use an anemometer rather than judgement
- Rain — electrical risk, slip risk, reduced visibility
- Lightning — cease work

**[VERIFY: maximum wind speed ratings are machine-specific and stated in the operator's manual. Common ratings are in the order of 12.5 m/s but must be confirmed for the specific machine.]**

## Operating Safely

### Harness

**In a boom-type EWP, wear a full body harness with a short restraint lanyard attached to the designated anchor point.**

**Why:** the hazard is **catapulting**. If the boom is struck, or the machine jolts, or a wheel drops into a hole, the boom whips and can eject the occupant over the guardrail. This has killed people.

**The lanyard is a restraint, not a fall arrest lanyard** — it must be short enough to keep you inside the platform. A long shock-absorbing lanyard would let you exit the basket and then arrest, which defeats the purpose.

**In a scissor lift**, harness requirements vary — some jurisdictions and many site rules require it, others do not for scissor lifts on level ground. **Follow the site rule and the manufacturer's requirement.**

### Load

- **Never exceed the rated capacity.** It includes people, tools, and material
- **Distribute the load** — do not concentrate it at one end of the platform
- **Do not carry long material that projects beyond the platform** unless the machine and the task permit it. It changes the load geometry and creates a snag and contact hazard

### Positioning and Movement

- **Look before you move**, in the direction of travel, and check overhead
- **Travel with the platform lowered** unless the machine is specifically rated for elevated travel
- **Travel slowly.** Sudden movement destabilises the machine and jolts the occupants
- **Watch for the pothole protection** deploying, and never override an interlock
- **Do not travel elevated over rough ground** even where the machine permits elevated travel

### The Prohibitions

These are absolute and they are all things people do.

- **Do not climb on or over the guardrails** to gain extra height. If the platform will not reach, the machine is wrong for the job
- **Do not stand on the toeboard, the mid rail, or a ladder, box or plank inside the platform**
- **Do not use the platform as a crane** to lift or suspend loads
- **Do not use the boom to push, pull, or drag anything**
- **Do not exit the platform at height** and re-enter, unless the machine and procedure specifically allow it and appropriate fall protection is in place
- **Do not tie the platform to a structure.** If the structure or the machine moves, the platform is destroyed or the machine overturns
- **Do not modify the machine or its guardrails**
- **Do not exceed the platform's rated occupancy**

### Crush Hazard

**Operators have been killed by being crushed between the platform guardrail and an overhead structure**, in some cases by inadvertently operating the controls while leaning over.

- **Be aware of what is above you** at all times while raising
- **Raise slowly** in confined areas
- **Do not lean out over the guardrail** while operating the controls
- Keep a clear line of sight upward

## Emergency Procedures

### Emergency Lowering

**Every EWP has a ground-level emergency lowering system.** Locate and test it as part of the pre-start.

**Someone at ground level must know how to operate it.** A person incapacitated at height in a platform requires ground-level lowering — and if nobody present knows where the control is or how to use it, that is a rescue that does not happen.

**This means an EWP should not be operated with nobody else on site.**

### Rescue Plan

Required before elevated work commences. It must address:
- How an incapacitated occupant is brought to ground
- Who operates the emergency lowering
- What happens if the machine cannot be lowered — for example after a boom failure or an entanglement
- Emergency service access

**"Call 000" is not a rescue plan** for a person suspended in a harness or trapped at height. Suspension trauma can cause loss of consciousness within roughly 10–20 minutes.

### Machine Failure at Height

- Do not panic or attempt to climb out
- Use the platform emergency stop
- Signal for ground-level emergency lowering
- Remain in the platform

### Overhead Line Contact

- **Stay in the platform.** Do not touch the machine or attempt to exit
- **Nobody approaches the machine** — step potential may energise the surrounding ground
- Isolation must be confirmed by the network operator
- If the machine is on fire and evacuation is unavoidable, jump clear without touching the machine and the ground simultaneously, land with feet together, and shuffle away with feet together

See CPCCCM2010.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are installing fascia and gutter on a new two-storey house. The eaves are 5.8m above ground.*

*A trailer-mounted boom lift is available on site. The area along the rear of the house where you need to work has:*

*- A stormwater trench that was backfilled four days ago, running parallel to the wall about 2m out*
*- A slight fall away from the house, roughly 4 degrees*
*- An overhead service line to the house, running from a pole in the rear yard to a point on the side wall*
*- A forecast of 30–40 km/h winds building through the afternoon*

*Describe your assessment and what you would do.*

---

### Exemplary Response

**Three of these four conditions are individually capable of stopping the job, and two of them are the classic EWP fatality mechanisms.** I would work through them before the machine is moved into position.

---

### 1. The backfilled trench — the one that will overturn the machine

**This is the most serious hazard here and the one most likely to be overlooked**, because backfilled ground looks like ground.

A stormwater trench backfilled four days ago has not consolidated. **It will not reliably carry an outrigger load.** A trailer-mounted boom concentrates its weight onto a small number of outrigger footprints, and an outrigger punching through into a soft trench causes the machine to overturn — with the platform elevated and occupied.

**EWPs have overturned into their own site's service trenches.** It is a documented and recurring incident type.

It runs **parallel to the wall, 2m out** — which is precisely where the machine needs to be to reach the eaves.

**What I do:**
- **Identify and mark the trench line** on the ground so it is visible, not remembered
- **Set up so that no outrigger bears on the trench or its influence zone** — and the influence zone extends beyond the trench width, because the load spreads at an angle into the ground beside it
- **If the machine cannot be positioned clear of it**, then either the trench needs to be properly compacted and verified, or steel road plates or crane mats need to be used to bridge and distribute the load, or a different machine or method is required
- **Use spreader pads under all outriggers regardless**, sized for the ground

**I would not accept "it's been four days, it'll be right."** Compaction is verified by testing, not by elapsed time.

---

### 2. The 4 degree slope

I need to check this against **the machine's rated slope tolerance**, from the manual — not against a general figure.

**Trailer-mounted booms are set on outriggers and levelled**, so a 4 degree fall is often manageable — but only if:
- The outriggers have sufficient extension range to level the machine on that fall
- The ground under the downhill outriggers is sound. **A slope means the downhill outriggers carry more load and are extended further, which increases both the bearing pressure and the consequence of a punch-through**
- The tilt alarm and interlocks function

**The slope and the trench interact badly.** Water runs downhill and the ground on the low side may be softer, and the trench may be on the low side.

**If the machine cannot be levelled within its rating, it does not go there.** Operating an unlevelled EWP is how they overturn.

---

### 3. The overhead service line — the other fatality mechanism

There is a service line running from a pole in the rear yard to the side wall, and I will be operating a **boom** lift in that yard, at 5.8m platform height, with the boom moving through a much larger envelope.

**I do not assume anything about that line.**

- **I cannot determine its voltage by looking.** Assume the highest until the network operator confirms
- **The covering is weatherproofing, not insulation**
- **A boom lift can arc without contact** at sufficient voltage, which is why an approach distance applies rather than a no-touch rule
- The boom sweeps a large volume while positioning, well beyond where the platform ends up

**What I do:**
- **Contact the network operator** to identify the line and obtain the prescribed no go zone and minimum approach distance
- **Request de-energising or temporary disconnection.** For a service connection to the house being worked on, this is frequently straightforward and is the primary control. It removes the hazard rather than managing it
- If it stays live, **assess whether the boom can reach the work while maintaining the approach distance throughout its full range of movement** — not just at the working position
- If clearance cannot be assured, a **safety observer** whose only job is watching the clearance, with authority to stop
- **Mark the no go zone** physically so it is visible from the platform
- **Brief every person on site**, including anyone delivering material with a raised tray

See CPCCCM2010.

---

### 4. Wind — 30–40 km/h building through the afternoon

**Wind is the limiting weather factor for EWP operation**, and this forecast is likely to exceed the machine's rating.

**Two compounding factors specific to this task:**

**The platform and occupants act as a sail** at 5.8m, and wind speed at height is higher than at ground level.

**I am handling fascia and gutter — long sheet material.** A length of gutter or fascia in an elevated platform in 40 km/h wind is genuinely dangerous. It will catch wind, it can be torn from your grip, it can pull you off balance against the guardrail, and it applies a lateral force to the machine.

**What I do:**
- **Get the machine's maximum wind speed rating from the manual.** Common ratings are in the order of 12.5 m/s, which is roughly 45 km/h, but this must be confirmed for the specific machine — and a rating is a limit, not a target
- **Measure the actual wind speed with an anemometer**, not by judgement. Judgement systematically underestimates wind at height
- **Plan the work for the morning** while the wind is lower, and be prepared to stop when it builds
- **Stop when the rating is approached** — not when it is exceeded. There is no partial credit for being close

**Handling long sheet material at height in building wind is the specific combination I would stop for first**, even if the machine's rating had not been reached.

---

### My decision and plan

**The work can be done, but not in the configuration as presented and probably not this afternoon.**

**Sequence:**

1. **Call the network operator today** to identify the line and request disconnection for the work period. This has a lead time so it goes first
2. **Locate and mark the trench**; determine whether the machine can be set up clear of it. If not, arrange road plates or an alternative
3. **Confirm the machine's slope and wind ratings** from the manual
4. **Pre-operational inspection** including outriggers, spreader pads, tilt alarm, interlocks and emergency lowering
5. **Plan the work for the morning** to stay ahead of the wind
6. **Confirm someone on site is briefed on emergency lowering** and knows where the control is — I would not operate this machine with nobody else present
7. **Harness with a short restraint lanyard** to the designated anchor, because this is a boom lift and the hazard is catapulting

**What I would say to the supervisor:**

*"I can do the fascia off the boom, but not today as it stands. That stormwater trench is only four days old and it'll take an outrigger straight through, so I need to set up clear of it or get plates. There's a service line to the house that needs identifying — if they can disconnect it for a day that solves it. And with 30 to 40 forecast this afternoon I don't want to be handling gutter at six metres. Can we do it first thing tomorrow morning and get the network operator organised today?"*

---

### The judgement

Individually, none of these four conditions looks dramatic. Four-day-old backfill looks like ground. A 4 degree fall looks flat. A house service line looks harmless. A 40 km/h afternoon is an ordinary day.

**Together they are an overturn, an electrocution, and a fall waiting for the same afternoon.** The EWP is the correct fall prevention control for this task — it just has to be set up on ground that will hold it, clear of a line that will kill you, in wind it is rated for.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies the backfilled trench as the primary hazard and explains the mechanism precisely — outrigger punch-through causing overturn with the platform elevated — while noting that the load influence zone extends beyond the trench width and that elapsed time is not evidence of compaction. The observation that the trench runs exactly where the machine needs to be is the practical crux and is identified. The slope analysis is sophisticated: recognising that a fall loads and extends the downhill outriggers further, increasing both bearing pressure and consequence, and that the low side is likely to be the softer side, shows the student is thinking about how the hazards interact rather than treating them as a list. The powerline section correctly applies the approach distance principle to the boom's full range of movement rather than only its working position. The wind analysis is the strongest element: identifying that handling long fascia and gutter in an elevated platform is the specific hazard, that wind speed is higher at height, that judgement underestimates it, and that the rating is a limit rather than a target. Requiring someone on site briefed on emergency lowering, and the short restraint lanyard for catapult protection, are both correct and specific. The plan sequences the long-lead item first. Strong Competent.
