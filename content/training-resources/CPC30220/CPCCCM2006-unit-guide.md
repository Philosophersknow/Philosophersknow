## What This Unit Is About

Levelling is how a building gets its heights right. Floor levels, footing depths, wall plate heights, drainage falls, and site cut-and-fill all depend on being able to transfer a height accurately from one point to another — often over distances where a spirit level is useless.

CPCCCM2006 asks you to set up and use levelling equipment, take and record readings, calculate reduced levels, and set out heights on site.

---

## Levelling Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Datum** | A fixed reference point from which all heights are measured |
| **AHD (Australian Height Datum)** | The national datum — approximately mean sea level |
| **Benchmark (BM)** | A permanent point of known height |
| **Temporary Benchmark (TBM)** | A site-established point of known or assumed height |
| **Reduced Level (RL)** | The height of a point above the datum |
| **Backsight (BS)** | The first reading taken, onto a point of known RL |
| **Foresight (FS)** | The last reading taken from an instrument position, before moving |
| **Intermediate Sight (IS)** | Any reading between the backsight and foresight from the same setup |
| **Height of Collimation (HC)** | The RL of the instrument's line of sight |
| **Change Point / Turning Point** | A point read as a foresight, then as a backsight from the new instrument position |
| **Rise and fall** | The difference in height between consecutive points |
| **Invert level** | The RL of the inside bottom of a pipe or drain |

## Levelling Equipment

### Spirit Level
Accurate over its own length only. Suitable for short runs — a wall plate, a door frame, a single joist.

**Check for accuracy:** place on a surface, note the bubble, then rotate the level end-for-end in the same position. The bubble should read identically. If it does not, the level is out and must be adjusted or replaced.

**Extending a spirit level** with a straight edge extends its usefulness — but any bow in the straight edge introduces error.

### Water Level
Two clear tubes connected by a flexible hose filled with water. **Water finds its own level regardless of distance or obstruction.**

- Accurate over long distances and around corners, where line of sight is blocked
- Requires no calibration — physics does the work
- **Must be completely free of air bubbles**, which produce false readings
- Slow compared with a laser, but reliable and cheap

### Laser Level

| Type | Function |
|---|---|
| **Rotating laser** | Sweeps a horizontal (or vertical) plane; used with a detector on a staff for outdoor work |
| **Line laser** | Projects visible lines; interior work |
| **Dot/point laser** | Projects reference points; plumb and square transfer |

- **Self-levelling** within a compensation range — outside that range they either flash a warning or fail to level. **Check the machine has actually self-levelled before relying on it**
- Outdoors, a rotating laser with a detector on a staff is the practical standard
- **Requires periodic calibration checking** — see the two-peg test below
- Eye safety: do not look into the beam; use the appropriate class of equipment

### Automatic (Dumpy) Level

An optical instrument with an internal compensator that maintains a horizontal line of sight once roughly levelled.

**Components:** telescope, compensator, tripod, levelling foot screws, circular bubble, staff.

**Setup:**
1. Set the tripod firmly, legs well spread, head approximately level
2. Tread the legs into the ground
3. Centre the circular bubble using the three foot screws
4. Focus the eyepiece on the crosshairs, then focus the telescope on the staff
5. Check for **parallax** — move your eye slightly; if the crosshairs appear to move against the staff, refocus. Parallax introduces reading error

### Staff
A graduated measuring rod, typically 4m or 5m, read through the instrument.

**Reading a staff:** graduations are typically in metres, with the E-pattern representing 10mm divisions. Readings are recorded to three decimal places (millimetres).

**The staff must be vertical.** A leaning staff always reads **high**. Use a staff bubble, or rock the staff gently toward and away from the instrument and take the **lowest** reading — the lowest reading is the vertical one.

## The Height of Collimation Method

The standard method for calculating reduced levels.

### The Principle

**Height of Collimation (HC) = RL of known point + Backsight reading**

**RL of any other point = HC − staff reading at that point**

The logic: the backsight tells you how far the instrument's line of sight is above the known point. Add that to the known RL and you know the RL of the line of sight itself. Every other reading is then subtracted from that line.

**Higher ground gives a smaller staff reading. Lower ground gives a larger staff reading.** This is the check that catches most sign errors.

### Worked Calculation

A TBM has an RL of 25.000m. The instrument is set up and readings taken:

| Point | Reading | Type |
|---|---|---|
| TBM | 1.450 | BS |
| A | 1.820 | IS |
| B | 0.960 | IS |
| C | 2.310 | FS |

**Step 1 — Height of Collimation:**
HC = 25.000 + 1.450 = **26.450m**

**Step 2 — Reduced Levels:**

| Point | Reading | Calculation | RL |
|---|---|---|---|
| TBM | 1.450 | (known) | 25.000 |
| A | 1.820 | 26.450 − 1.820 | **24.630** |
| B | 0.960 | 26.450 − 0.960 | **25.490** |
| C | 2.310 | 26.450 − 2.310 | **24.140** |

**Sense check:** B has the smallest reading and the highest RL. C has the largest reading and the lowest RL. That is correct.

### Booking and the Arithmetic Check

Readings are recorded in a level book with columns for BS, IS, FS, HC and RL.

**The arithmetic check for the height of collimation method:**

**Σ Backsights − Σ Foresights = Last RL − First RL**

This checks the arithmetic, **not the field work.** A misread staff or a moved instrument will pass this check. It confirms you added and subtracted correctly, nothing more.

## Change Points

When the required points cannot all be seen from one instrument position — because of distance, obstruction, or slope — the instrument must be moved.

**Procedure:**
1. Take a **foresight** onto a stable, well-defined point (the change point) before moving
2. Calculate the RL of the change point
3. **Move the instrument** and set up again
4. Take a **backsight** onto the same change point, without moving the staff
5. Calculate a **new height of collimation** from the change point's RL
6. Continue

**The change point must not move between the foresight and the backsight.** Use a firm, well-defined point — a change plate, a peg, a nail in a stump. Never a soft or loose surface, and never a point that someone might disturb.

## Checking Instrument Accuracy — The Two-Peg Test

Levelling instruments and rotating lasers go out of adjustment, and a mis-adjusted instrument produces readings that look perfectly plausible and are wrong. The two-peg test detects it.

**Method:**

1. Drive two pegs, A and B, a set distance apart — commonly around 30–60m
2. **Set up exactly midway** between them. Read the staff on A and on B
   - The true height difference = reading A − reading B
   - **At the midpoint, any collimation error affects both readings equally and cancels out.** This measurement is therefore correct regardless of instrument error
3. **Move the instrument close to one peg** (or beyond it, per the method used) and read both A and B again
   - Calculate the height difference again
4. **Compare.** If the two height differences agree within tolerance, the instrument is in adjustment. If they differ, the instrument has a collimation error and must be adjusted or serviced

**[VERIFY: Two-peg test procedure variants and acceptable tolerance against the instrument manufacturer's instructions and your organisation's calibration requirements.]**

**Do this periodically, and always after an instrument has been dropped or knocked.**

## Setting Out Heights

Levelling is not only about measuring existing heights — it is about **establishing** required heights on site.

### Calculating a Required Staff Reading

To set a point at a specified RL:

**Required staff reading = HC − Required RL**

**Example:** HC is 26.450m. You need to establish a point at RL 25.800m.

Required reading = 26.450 − 25.800 = **0.650m**

Move the staff up or down until it reads exactly 0.650, and mark at the base of the staff. That mark is at RL 25.800.

### Common Applications

| Task | Application |
|---|---|
| **Floor level** | Establishing finished floor level from a datum |
| **Footing depth** | Setting excavation depth to design RL |
| **Wall plate height** | Transferring a consistent height around a building |
| **Drainage falls** | Setting invert levels to achieve required gradient |
| **Site cut and fill** | Determining existing versus design surface levels |
| **Profiles and hurdles** | Timber set-ups carrying string lines at working height |

### Gradients and Falls

Drainage and paving require a specified fall, usually expressed as a ratio or a percentage.

**Fall = Length × Gradient**

**Example:** a drain runs 18m at a gradient of 1:80.
Fall = 18 ÷ 80 = **0.225m (225mm)**

If the upstream invert is at RL 24.500, the downstream invert is at 24.500 − 0.225 = **RL 24.275**

**Percentage gradients:** 1:100 = 1%, 1:50 = 2%, 1:80 = 1.25%.

**Insufficient fall causes ponding and blockage; excessive fall in a drain can cause solids to be left behind as the liquid runs away.** Both matter, and the specified gradient is a design decision, not a preference.

## Sources of Error

| Error | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Staff not vertical** | Leaning staff — always reads high | Staff bubble; rock the staff and take the lowest reading |
| **Parallax** | Eyepiece and telescope not correctly focused | Focus crosshairs first, then the staff; check by moving the eye |
| **Instrument settlement** | Tripod sinking into soft ground between readings | Tread legs in firmly; avoid soft ground; work efficiently |
| **Collimation error** | Instrument out of adjustment | Two-peg test; keep BS and FS distances approximately equal, which cancels the error |
| **Change point moved** | Disturbed between FS and BS | Use a firm, defined point; do not move the staff between readings |
| **Misreading the staff** | Reading the wrong graduation or transposing digits | Read twice; sense-check against expected ground shape |
| **Booking error** | Recording in the wrong column | Book immediately; apply the arithmetic check |
| **Long sights** | Reading accuracy degrades with distance and heat shimmer | Keep sight distances reasonable; avoid reading low over hot ground |

**Keeping backsight and foresight distances approximately equal is the most effective single practice**, because it cancels collimation error, refraction and curvature effects.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are setting out for a new garage slab. The site TBM is a nail in a concrete kerb with an assumed RL of 50.000m. The design finished floor level (FFL) of the slab is RL 50.320m.*

*You set up the level and take a backsight of 1.275 on the TBM. You then need to check four corner peg positions and set them all to FFL.*

*Your readings at the four corners are: A 1.610, B 1.585, C 1.720, D 1.655.*

*Calculate the existing RLs, determine the staff reading required to set FFL, and state how much each corner needs to be raised or cut. Then explain how you would check your work.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

**Step 1 — Height of Collimation**

HC = RL of TBM + Backsight
HC = 50.000 + 1.275 = **51.275m**

This is the RL of the instrument's line of sight. Every subsequent reading is subtracted from it.

---

**Step 2 — Existing RLs at each corner**

RL = HC − staff reading

| Corner | Reading | Calculation | Existing RL |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1.610 | 51.275 − 1.610 | **49.665** |
| B | 1.585 | 51.275 − 1.585 | **49.690** |
| C | 1.720 | 51.275 − 1.720 | **49.555** |
| D | 1.655 | 51.275 − 1.655 | **49.620** |

**Sense check:** B has the smallest reading and the highest RL; C has the largest reading and the lowest RL. That is the correct relationship — higher ground gives a smaller reading. If I had these reversed I would have made a sign error.

---

**Step 3 — Required staff reading to set FFL**

Required reading = HC − Required RL
Required reading = 51.275 − 50.320 = **0.955m**

So anywhere I place the staff and read exactly **0.955**, the base of the staff is at RL 50.320 — the design finished floor level.

---

**Step 4 — Fill required at each corner**

Difference = FFL − Existing RL

| Corner | Existing RL | FFL | Difference | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 49.665 | 50.320 | +0.655 | **Fill 655mm** |
| B | 49.690 | 50.320 | +0.630 | **Fill 630mm** |
| C | 49.555 | 50.320 | +0.765 | **Fill 765mm** |
| D | 49.620 | 50.320 | +0.700 | **Fill 700mm** |

**All four corners require fill.** The design FFL sits between 630mm and 765mm above existing ground across the footprint.

**The site falls from B (highest) toward C (lowest), a difference of 135mm across the slab.**

---

**Step 5 — What I would flag before proceeding**

The calculations are straightforward, but the result raises a question I would not proceed past silently.

**Six to seven hundred millimetres of fill under a slab is substantial.** That is not a trimming exercise; it is engineered fill. Before anyone starts carting material in, I would want confirmation of:

- **Is this fill specified and engineered?** Fill under a slab must be placed in controlled layers and compacted to a specified density, with compaction testing. Uncontrolled fill settles, and a slab on settling fill cracks
- **Is the design FFL correct?** A discrepancy this size is worth confirming against the plans. It is possible the TBM assumed RL is wrong, or that I have been given the wrong FFL, or that the design genuinely does sit this high — for flood immunity, to match an existing floor level, or to achieve drainage falls to the street
- **Does the site have drainage implications?** Raising a slab this much changes where water goes, both on this site and potentially onto neighbours
- **Are footings designed for fill?** Footings may need to found below the fill into natural ground, or be designed for the fill condition

I would raise this with the supervisor: *"The corners are coming up 630 to 765 of fill to reach that FFL — is that right, and is the fill spec'd? That's a fair bit and I want to make sure I've got the right level before we start bringing material in."*

**Being wrong about a floor level is discovered when the slab is poured**, which is the worst possible time and the most expensive to correct.

---

**Step 6 — How I would check my work**

**Check 1 — Re-read the TBM (the most important check).**

Before packing up, I would take a second reading on the TBM. **It should read 1.275 again.** If it does not, either the instrument has settled, been knocked, or is out of adjustment — and every reading in between is suspect.

This is the single most valuable check in practical levelling and takes ten seconds.

**Check 2 — Sense-check against the ground.**

Look at the site. Does the calculated shape match what I can see? If my figures say C is the low corner, C should visibly be the low corner. Numbers that contradict the visible ground are wrong numbers.

**Check 3 — Verify with a second method.**

Set the staff at a calculated position and check with an independent method where practical — a second instrument, a water level over a short distance, or a check from a second setup with a change point back to the TBM.

**Check 4 — Instrument condition.**

If there is any doubt, or if the instrument has been knocked or transported roughly, run a **two-peg test**. A collimation error produces readings that are internally consistent and uniformly wrong — which is exactly the failure mode that survives arithmetic checking.

**Check 5 — Sight distances.**

I would confirm my backsight and foresight distances were reasonably similar. Keeping them approximately equal cancels collimation error, refraction and curvature. If the TBM was 10m away and a corner was 60m away, that asymmetry introduces error that a closer, more balanced setup would avoid.

**What the arithmetic check does not do:**

The standard check — Σ BS − Σ FS = Last RL − First RL — only confirms I added and subtracted correctly. **A misread staff, a leaning staff, a settled tripod, or a mis-adjusted instrument all pass it perfectly.** It is a bookkeeping check, not a field check. The re-read of the TBM is what actually verifies the work.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The required-staff-reading calculation is correctly derived and clearly explained. The strongest element is Step 5: the student does not treat this as a pure arithmetic exercise but recognises that 630–765mm of fill is a substantial engineering matter and stops to confirm the design intent, the fill specification, drainage implications and footing design before material is carted in. That is exactly the professional judgement the unit is intended to develop, and the observation that a wrong floor level is discovered at pour is correct and commercially significant.
