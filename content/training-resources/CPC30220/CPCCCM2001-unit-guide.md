## What This Unit Is About

You cannot build what you cannot read. Plans and specifications are the technical language of construction — they communicate to every tradesperson exactly what must be built, to what dimensions, using what materials, and to what standard. A carpenter who misreads a plan or ignores a specification produces work that fails inspections, requires expensive rectification, and may be structurally unsafe.

CPCCCM2001 asks you to demonstrate that you can read and correctly interpret the drawings and documents that tell you what to build.

## Types of Construction Documents

### Working Drawings
Working drawings (construction drawings) are the graphical representation of what is to be built. They include:

| Drawing Type | What It Shows |
|---|---|
| **Site plan** | The property boundaries, position of the building on the site, north point, driveways, easements |
| **Floor plan** | Layout of rooms, walls, doors, windows, stairs — viewed from above, as if the roof were removed |
| **Elevations** | External faces of the building viewed from each direction (front, rear, left side, right side) — show height, window positions, roof shape, external finishes |
| **Section** | A vertical cut through the building at a specified location — shows construction detail that is not visible in plan or elevation |
| **Detail drawings** | Large-scale drawings of specific elements — connection details, waterproofing, structural junctions |
| **Structural drawings** | Engineer-prepared drawings showing structural elements — beams, columns, footings, connections, member sizes |
| **Services drawings** | Electrical, plumbing, hydraulic, HVAC — usually prepared by specialist consultants |

### Specifications
Specifications describe in words what the drawings show in graphics. They provide:
- Materials to be used (species, grade, treatment of timber; type and brand of fittings)
- Standards to be met (Australian Standards, BCA requirements)
- Methods of construction (adhesive types, fastener patterns, waterproofing systems)
- Quality requirements and tolerances
- Manufacturer installation requirements

**Where there is a conflict between drawings and specifications, specifications typically take precedence — but always raise conflicts with the supervisor or builder, never just choose one.**

## Scales and Dimensions

Construction drawings are reduced from full size to fit on a sheet of paper. The scale tells you the relationship between the drawing and the real object.

### Common Drawing Scales

| Scale | Use |
|---|---|
| 1:500 or 1:200 | Site plans — large areas at small scale |
| 1:100 | Floor plans, elevations — whole buildings |
| 1:50 | Sections, construction details |
| 1:20 or 1:10 | Connection details, joinery details |
| 1:1 | Full-size details (rare — used for complex moulding profiles etc.) |

**Reading a scale:** If a drawing is at 1:100, every 1mm on the drawing represents 100mm (100mm = 10cm = 0.1m) in reality. A wall shown as 35mm on the drawing is 3,500mm (3.5m) in reality.

**Using a scale rule:** A scale rule has multiple scales marked. Select the correct face for the drawing you are reading and read dimensions directly.

**Always check the scale stated on the drawing** — do not assume. Different drawings in the same set may be at different scales.

### Dimensions on Drawings
Most construction drawings in Australia provide actual dimensions in millimetres (mm) — do not rely only on the scale rule. If a dimension is shown, use it.

**Standard carpentry dimensions are expressed in millimetres (mm).** In construction:
- Wall heights: typically 2400mm or 2700mm (floor to ceiling)
- Stud spacing: typically 450mm or 600mm (measured centre to centre, written c/c)
- Door heights: typically 2040mm or 2340mm
- Timber sizes: width × depth, e.g., 90×45 (mm)

## Symbols and Conventions

Construction drawings use standardised symbols. As a carpenter you need to recognise:

### Wall Symbols (Floor Plan)
- **Solid lines with fill:** Indicates a wall; the type of fill indicates the material (brick, concrete, timber frame, etc.)
- **Dimension lines with arrows:** Measure to the face, centre, or structural element as specified

### Door and Window Symbols
- **Doors:** Shown as a line (door panel) with an arc indicating the swing direction
- **Windows:** Shown as parallel lines in the wall representing the frame and glass
- **Sliding doors/windows:** Shown as parallel lines with an arrow indicating the sliding direction

### Levels and Heights
- **Floor Level (FL):** Finished floor level at a specified point
- **Reduced Level (RL):** Height above Australian Height Datum (AHD) — used for site levels and earthworks
- **Datum:** A reference point from which levels are measured
- **Ridge height, wall height, head height, sill height:** Specific heights shown on elevations and sections

### Material Hatch Patterns
Different materials are shown by different hatch patterns in section:
- Concrete: dots or stipple pattern
- Brickwork: diagonal lines with horizontal zones
- Timber (section): diagonal hatching

## Reading a Floor Plan — Step by Step

When you receive a floor plan, work through it systematically:

1. **Identify the north point** — orientation affects your understanding of the site
2. **Check the scale** — stated in the title block or adjacent to the drawing
3. **Locate the overall dimensions** — total building footprint
4. **Identify structural walls and non-structural walls** — structural walls carry loads; non-structural (stud partition) walls can be modified more easily
5. **Locate all openings** — doors (identify size and swing), windows (size and type), voids
6. **Read the internal dimensions** — room sizes, door/window positions
7. **Note any levels changes** — steps, floor height changes
8. **Cross-reference to other drawings** — section marks, detail references, structural drawing references

## Specifications — What to Look For

As a carpenter, the specifications relevant to your work include:

- **Timber species and grade:** e.g., F5, F7, F17 stress grades; MGP10, MGP12 (machine-graded pine)
- **Treatment:** H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6 hazard class (higher number = more hazardous environment = more treatment)
- **Fasteners:** Type, size, spacing, and material (galvanised, stainless for external/marine environments)
- **Fixing patterns:** Nail patterns for structural connections; screw types for finishing
- **Tolerances:** Maximum permitted deviation from plumb, level, or square
- **Moisture content:** Acceptable moisture content for structural and finishing timber
- **Finishes:** Painting, staining, sealing requirements

## Australian Standards Relevant to Carpentry

| Standard | Coverage |
|---|---|
| AS 1684 — Residential Timber-Framed Construction | The primary standard for timber framing in residential buildings — span tables, member sizes, connection requirements |
| AS 1720 — Timber Structures | Structural timber design |
| AS 4440 — Installation of Nailplated Timber Roof Trusses | Handling, installing, and bracing manufactured roof trusses |
| AS 1170 — Structural Design Actions | Wind, snow, and seismic loads — determines design requirements for structural members |
| NCC/BCA Volume 2 | Performance requirements for residential buildings including structural, fire safety, energy efficiency |

**You do not need to memorise these standards.** You need to know they exist, what they cover, and where to find the relevant information when needed.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*A floor plan shows a bedroom with a dimension of 32mm × 45mm at a scale of 1:100. The wall between the bedroom and the hallway has a door opening shown. The door symbol shows a panel 9mm wide with a 90° arc. The specification states "all internal timber framing: 90×45mm MGP10 pine, H2 treated." What are the actual room dimensions, what size is the door opening, and what does the specification require you to use for internal wall framing?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

**Room dimensions:**
At a scale of 1:100, multiply the drawing measurement by 100 to get the actual dimension.
- 32mm on drawing × 100 = 3,200mm (3.2m) actual
- 45mm on drawing × 100 = 4,500mm (4.5m) actual

The bedroom is 3,200mm × 4,500mm (3.2m × 4.5m).

**Door opening size:**
The door panel is shown as 9mm wide on the drawing.
- 9mm × 100 = 900mm actual

The door opening is 900mm wide. The 90° arc confirms it is a standard hinged door that swings to a full right angle. I would check the specification or door schedule for the height — not shown in the plan information provided, but typically 2040mm for a standard internal door.

**Framing specification:**
The specification requires:
- **90×45mm** cross-section timber (width 90mm, depth 45mm — standard stud size for most residential walls)
- **MGP10** — machine-graded pine with a bending strength grade of 10. MGP10 is a common grade for internal wall framing.
- **H2 treated** — Hazard Class 2. H2 treatment is appropriate for timber used above ground in a protected, enclosed environment where there is a risk of attack by borers. For internal framing, H2 is the standard minimum treatment requirement in most of Australia.

**Examiner Annotation:** The student correctly applies the scale to both dimensions, correctly interprets the door symbol, and correctly explains each element of the specification. The note about checking the door schedule demonstrates initiative. Strong Competent.
