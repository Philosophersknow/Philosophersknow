## What This Unit Is About

CPCCCA3002 covers setting out and framing a roof. This unit covers **erecting** it — getting trusses or rafters into position safely and correctly — and **cladding** it, so the building is weathertight.

Two things dominate this unit: **temporary bracing during erection**, and **fall protection**. Partially erected roof structures collapse, and roof work is the highest-fall-risk activity a carpenter performs. Read this alongside CPCCCM2012.

## Roof Trusses

### What a Truss Is

A prefabricated, engineered frame of timber members connected by nailplates, designed to span between supports and carry roof and ceiling loads. Trusses replace site-cut rafters, ceiling joists, struts and underpurlins with a single engineered component.

### Truss Types

| Type | Function |
|---|---|
| **Standard / common truss** | The repeating truss across the main body |
| **Girder truss** | Heavier truss carrying the load of other trusses framing into it — at a hip end or where a roof section intersects |
| **Truncated truss** | Flat-topped truss forming the base of a hip end |
| **Jack truss** | Shortened truss framing into a girder |
| **Hip truss** | Forms the hip end |
| **Cantilever truss** | Extends beyond its support |

**The girder truss is the critical one.** It carries the accumulated load of everything framing into it, is often doubled or tripled, and has specific fixing and support requirements. **Its supports must be in place and adequate before it is loaded.**

### Handling and Storage

Trusses are **strong in their design plane and weak laterally.** A truss standing on edge is engineered; a truss lifted flat by one end can fold.

- **Store flat on level bearers**, evenly supported, or vertically braced upright
- **Do not store on uneven ground** — trusses distort
- **Protect from weather** — prolonged wetting causes swelling around nailplates and can loosen them
- **Lift at the designated points**, using a spreader bar or multiple slings for long trusses
- **Never lift by the apex alone** or by one end
- **Never drag a truss** — this can pull nailplates
- **Inspect on delivery and before erection** — check for damaged members, lifted or partly withdrawn nailplates, splits, and transit damage. **A damaged truss must not be installed**; report it and obtain a replacement or an engineer's repair detail

### The Prohibition on Modification

**Do not cut, notch, drill, or alter any truss member.**

A truss is engineered as a system. Every member is sized for its specific tension or compression role, and the nailplates are sized for the forces at each joint. Cutting a web member — even a small notch to pass a pipe or cable — can invalidate the entire design.

**If a service must pass through, that must be designed for.** Options are routing the service through the designed openings, using a different truss configuration, or obtaining an engineer's detail for a modification.

**"It's only a small notch" is how truss failures start.** This is a common site pressure, usually from another trade, and the answer is no.

## Erecting Trusses

### Before Erection

- Confirm **wall frames are complete, square, plumb, straight and braced**, and wall plates are level. Trusses will not correct an out-of-level wall plate — the roof will follow it
- Confirm **truss layout drawing** — truss positions, girder locations, bracing and tie-down. This is the authority
- Confirm **spacing** — commonly 600mm or 900mm centres per the design
- Confirm **girder truss supports** are in place and adequate
- Plan the **lifting method** — crane, telehandler, or manual for small spans
- Plan **fall protection before starting**, not after the first truss is up

### The Erection Sequence

1. **Mark truss positions** on the wall plates from a single datum at each end, so error does not accumulate
2. **Install girder trusses first**, fully fixed and braced, since other trusses frame into them
3. **Erect the first standard truss** and brace it **securely to a fixed point** — a wall frame, a temporary prop into the floor, or a ground anchor. This first truss is the anchor for everything that follows and its bracing must be substantial
4. **Erect subsequent trusses**, fixing temporary bracing to each as it goes up
5. **Maintain spacing** using temporary spacing ties as you go
6. **Install permanent bracing** per the layout drawing
7. **Install tie-down** connections
8. **Remove temporary bracing** only after permanent bracing is complete

### Temporary Bracing — The Critical Control

**Trusses are unstable until permanently braced.** A row of erected, unbraced trusses will fold sideways like dominoes — this is a documented cause of serious injury and death on residential sites.

**Temporary bracing must:**
- Be installed **as erection proceeds**, on each truss as it goes up — never "we'll brace them all at the end"
- Include **longitudinal ties** at ridge and at intervals down the slope, connecting each truss to the ones already braced
- Include **diagonal bracing** in the plane of the roof to prevent the whole assembly racking
- Connect back to a **fixed, stable point**
- Be adequate for **wind loading during construction** — a partly erected roof presents a large sail area and unbraced trusses have been lost in modest wind
- Remain in place until permanent bracing is complete

**Stop work in high wind.** Handling and erecting trusses in wind is genuinely dangerous, and the judgement about "how much wind is too much" should be made before the crane is booked, not while a truss is swinging.

## Ceiling Joists

In a stick-built roof, ceiling joists serve two functions:

1. **Carry the ceiling** — plasterboard, insulation, and any stored load
2. **Tie the wall plates together**, resisting the outward spread of the rafters

**The tie function is structural and frequently underestimated.** Rafters bearing on wall plates push outward. Without the ceiling joists acting as a tie, the walls spread and the ridge drops. Ceiling joists must therefore be **fixed to both the wall plate and the rafter** where the design requires, with the specified connection.

Where joists cannot run in the direction of the span, **hanging beams** support them, and **strutting beams** carry struts where there is no loadbearing wall below.

## Roof Cladding

### Cladding Types

| Type | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Sheet metal — corrugated / trapezoidal** | Most common in Australia; light, fast, suits low pitches, long spans |
| **Concrete tiles** | Heavy; requires stronger structure; suits steeper pitches |
| **Terracotta tiles** | Traditional; heavier; steeper pitch |
| **Slate** | Heavy, expensive, very long life |
| **Membrane** | Flat and very low pitch roofs |

**Cladding choice affects the structure.** Tile roofs weigh substantially more than sheet metal and require heavier framing, closer batten spacing, and different tie-down. **You cannot substitute tile for sheet on a frame designed for sheet.**

### Minimum Pitch

Every roof covering has a **minimum pitch** below which it will not shed water reliably and the manufacturer's warranty does not apply.

- Corrugated profiles generally require a higher minimum pitch than trapezoidal profiles
- Tiles require substantially more pitch than sheet metal
- Below the minimum, water is driven back up the laps by wind and capillary action

**[VERIFY: Minimum pitch requirements against the specific product manufacturer's specification and AS 1562 / the NCC. These vary by profile and are not interchangeable between products.]**

**Laying a roof below its minimum pitch is a leak that has not happened yet.**

### Battens

Battens carry the roof covering and transfer its load to the rafters or trusses.

- **Batten size and spacing** are determined by the covering type, the rafter/truss spacing, and the wind classification — read from AS 1684 or the manufacturer's specification
- Tile battens are more closely spaced than metal roofing battens, and carry more load
- **Fixing and tie-down** per the wind classification
- Battens must be straight and in plane — a bowed batten telegraphs through the finished roof

### Sarking and Insulation

**Sarking** (roof underlay) sits between the battens and the structure and provides:
- A secondary water barrier against wind-driven rain and condensation
- A vapour or thermal function depending on the product
- Fire performance in bushfire-prone areas

**Install with correct laps, running downslope**, so any water is directed out and over the fascia — not into the roof space. Laps run the same way as the water.

**Condensation management** is increasingly regulated. Sealed, well-insulated roof spaces without adequate ventilation accumulate moisture, causing mould and timber decay. Follow the specified ventilation and vapour control requirements.

**[VERIFY: Condensation management and roof space ventilation requirements against the current NCC. These provisions have been substantially strengthened in recent NCC editions.]**

### Flashings

Flashings direct water away from junctions and penetrations — the points where roofs actually leak.

| Flashing | Location |
|---|---|
| **Ridge capping** | Along the ridge |
| **Hip capping** | Along hips |
| **Valley gutter** | Internal roof junctions — carries substantial water volume |
| **Barge / gable flashing** | Gable ends |
| **Apron flashing** | Where a roof abuts a vertical wall |
| **Step flashing** | Where a sloping roof meets a wall |
| **Penetration flashings** | Around vents, flues, pipes, skylights |

**The universal principle: every layer laps over the layer below, and water is always directed outward and downward.** A flashing lapped the wrong way is worse than no flashing — it actively channels water into the building.

**Valleys carry the combined flow of two roof planes** and are a common leak point. They must be adequately sized, correctly supported, and not restricted by the covering.

### Fixing Sheet Roofing

- Fix **through the crest** on most profiles, not the pan — fixing through the pan puts a hole in the water channel
- Use the **specified fastener** with an intact sealing washer
- **Do not overtighten** — this crushes the profile and distorts the washer, creating a leak path
- Observe the specified fixing pattern and frequency, which increases at edges and in higher wind classifications
- **End laps and side laps** per the manufacturer's requirement, with laps facing away from the prevailing weather

**Never walk on the pans of unsupported sheet roofing.** Walk over the battens, on the crests, or use crawl boards.

## Safety — Roof Erection and Cladding

This is the highest-risk work in the qualification.

| Hazard | Control |
|---|---|
| **Falls from the perimeter** | **Perimeter edge protection or scaffold** — passive protection, the primary control |
| **Falls through the roof** | **Safety mesh** under the covering; cover and mark all penetrations |
| **Fragile surfaces** | Skylights and old sheeting will not bear weight and are hard to identify when dusty. Treat as fragile unless confirmed otherwise |
| **Truss collapse during erection** | Adequate temporary bracing installed as erection proceeds |
| **Wind** | Stop work. Sheet material acts as a sail; trusses present a large area |
| **Crane / lifting** | Exclusion zone; no one under a suspended load; licensed operator and dogger |
| **Falling objects** | Toeboards, tool lanyards, exclusion zone below |
| **Manual handling** | Sheets are large and awkward; tiles are heavy and repetitive |
| **Slip** | Wet, frosty, or dusty roofs. Pitch increases risk well before it looks steep |
| **Sun and heat** | Roofs are hot and exposed; metal roofs reflect UV |

**Fall protection is required for roof work at any height** — not only above 2 metres. Roof edges, slope, and fragile surfaces create fall risk independent of the drop height.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are part of a two-person crew erecting trusses on a single-storey house, 12m long, trusses at 600mm centres. Twenty trusses have been craned onto the wall plates in a bundle. Your leading hand has stood up the first six trusses, fixed them to the wall plates, and connected them to each other with a single temporary tie at the ridge. He says the crane is booked for another job and asks you to keep standing trusses while he goes to get the bracing timber, which is at the other end of the site.*

*A moderate breeze has picked up during the morning.*

*Identify the problems and describe what you would do.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I would stop erecting trusses. What is standing right now is already inadequately braced, and adding more to it makes a bad situation worse rather than getting the job closer to done.

---

**Problem 1 — The existing bracing is inadequate (the immediate danger)**

Six trusses connected by **a single tie at the ridge** are not braced. They are hinged.

A single longitudinal tie holds the trusses at a consistent spacing at the ridge, but it does nothing to stop the whole assembly rotating about the wall plates and folding sideways. There is no diagonal bracing in the plane of the roof, and nothing tying the assembly back to a fixed point.

**This is the classic progressive collapse setup.** A row of unbraced trusses fails the way dominoes fall — the first one goes, and each one takes the next with it. It happens fast enough that a person working among them cannot get clear, and it has killed people on residential sites.

**What adequate temporary bracing requires:**
- **Longitudinal ties at multiple levels** — ridge *and* at intervals down the slope, not ridge alone
- **Diagonal bracing in the plane of the roof**, to stop the assembly racking
- **Connection back to a fixed point** — the wall frame, a prop to the floor, or a ground anchor
- The **first truss braced substantially**, because it anchors everything that follows

None of that is present.

---

**Problem 2 — Wind, with a large sail area**

A moderate breeze on six standing trusses is a real load. Trusses present a large surface area, they are stiff in their own plane and weak laterally, and there is nothing here resisting a lateral push.

Wind is not a constant — a gust can substantially exceed the steady breeze. **Trusses have been lost in conditions that felt entirely manageable at ground level**, and it is windier at ridge height than it is on the ground.

Adding more trusses increases the sail area every time, while the bracing stays exactly as inadequate as it is now.

---

**Problem 3 — Working alone**

I would be erecting trusses by myself while the leading hand walks to the far end of the site. Standing and positioning a truss is a two-person task at minimum, and if something goes wrong there is nobody there.

If the assembly started to go, I would be alone, at height, among falling trusses, with no one to raise the alarm.

---

**Problem 4 — The reason given is a schedule reason, not a safety reason**

"The crane is booked for another job" explains the pressure. It does not change the physics. The bracing does not become adequate because the schedule is tight.

This is worth naming clearly to myself, because schedule pressure is exactly the mechanism by which people end up doing something they know is unsafe.

---

**Problem 5 — Fall protection**

The scenario does not mention it, and I would want to confirm it before anything else proceeds. Truss erection is work at height on an incomplete structure. **Roof work requires fall protection at any height.** If there is no perimeter edge protection or scaffold, that is a separate stop-work issue in its own right.

---

**What I do:**

**1. Stop. Do not stand any more trusses.**

I would not continue and then raise it afterwards. Once more trusses are up, the hazard is larger and harder to remedy.

**2. Say so immediately, before he walks off:**

*"Hang on — before you go, I'm not putting any more up. Six trusses on one ridge tie isn't braced, and with this breeze getting up I'm not comfortable adding to it. Can we get the bracing on what's standing first? I'll come and help you carry it — it'll be quicker with two of us anyway."*

That does three things: it stops the unsafe work, it explains why in terms of the actual hazard rather than a rule, and it offers a way to solve the problem faster than he was going to solve it alone. A leading hand under time pressure responds much better to that than to a flat refusal.

**3. Make what is standing safe first.**

Before anything else goes up:
- Install **additional longitudinal ties** down the slope, not just at ridge
- Install **diagonal bracing** in the roof plane
- **Tie the assembly back to a fixed point** — the wall frame or props to the floor
- Check the first truss is substantially braced, since everything hangs off it

**4. Then resume properly.**

Once the standing trusses are secure, continue erecting — **bracing each truss as it goes up**, not batching the bracing for later. That is the whole principle: the bracing proceeds with the erection.

**5. Reassess the wind.**

If the breeze keeps building, we stop. Handling a truss in gusty conditions is a genuine hazard to the person holding it as much as to the structure.

**6. If I were told to keep going regardless:**

I would exercise my **right to cease unsafe work**. I have a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk — progressive collapse of an unbraced roof structure while I am working underneath and among it. That right is protected and I cannot be lawfully penalised for it. I would notify the leading hand, escalate to the site supervisor, and document the direction and my response.

---

**The thing worth holding onto:**

The failure mode here is quiet. Nothing about six standing trusses on a light tie *looks* dangerous. They are up, they are fixed to the plates, they look like a roof starting to happen. The danger is entirely in what is not there.

**Truss collapses do not give warning.** By the time the first one moves, the decision about whether to be standing there has already been made.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies that a single ridge tie is not bracing and explains the specific mechanism — the assembly hinges about the wall plates and fails progressively — rather than simply asserting that more bracing is needed. The specification of what adequate temporary bracing actually comprises is accurate and complete. Recognition that adding trusses increases sail area while bracing remains static is a sound piece of reasoning that goes beyond the prompt. The observation that it is windier at ridge height than at ground level is practical and correct. Identifying the lone-working issue and the absence of any mention of fall protection shows the student is assessing the whole situation rather than only the question asked. The script offered to the leading hand is well judged: it stops the work, explains the hazard in physical rather than procedural terms, and offers a faster path to resolution, which is far more likely to succeed with someone under schedule pressure than a bare refusal. The closing observation that the danger is invisible and gives no warning captures precisely why this hazard kills people who are neither careless nor untrained. Strong Competent.
