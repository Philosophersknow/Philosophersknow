## What This Unit Is About

Commercial kitchens have one of the highest injury rates of any Australian workplace. Burns, cuts, slips, and back injuries are routine rather than exceptional, and the culture in many kitchens treats them as an unavoidable cost of the job.

They are not. SITXWHS005 asks you to understand the hazards in a hospitality workplace, follow the safety systems in place, contribute to them, and know your rights when they are inadequate.

## The Legal Framework

### Model WHS Act

**PCBU duties (the venue operator):** ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers and others — safe plant, safe systems of work, adequate facilities, information, training, supervision, and consultation.

**Worker duties:**
- Take reasonable care for your own health and safety
- Take reasonable care not to adversely affect others
- Comply with reasonable instructions
- Cooperate with WHS policies and procedures

**Worker rights:**
- To be consulted on health and safety matters affecting you
- To be represented by a Health and Safety Representative
- **To cease unsafe work** where there is a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk
- Protection from discriminatory conduct for raising WHS matters

### Young and Inexperienced Workers

Hospitality employs a very high proportion of young workers, and young workers are over-represented in workplace injury statistics.

**Contributing factors:** inexperience, reluctance to question instructions, unfamiliarity with equipment, and a culture that rewards speed.

**Employers have a heightened duty to supervise and instruct inexperienced workers.** "Just watch what I do" is not training. If you have not been shown how to use a piece of equipment safely, you should not be using it — and asking is the correct professional response, not an admission of inadequacy.

## Kitchen Hazards

### Burns and Scalds

The most common serious kitchen injury.

| Source | Control |
|---|---|
| **Hot pans, trays, handles** | Dry cloths or mitts — **a wet cloth conducts heat and will scald you instantly**; assume every handle is hot |
| **Steam** | Open lids and combi ovens **away from your body**; steam causes worse burns than dry heat at the same temperature |
| **Hot oil / deep fryers** | Never introduce water to hot oil; lower food away from you; correct basket loading |
| **Boiling liquids** | Do not overfill; carry with both hands; announce movement |
| **Oven doors and racks** | Mitts; pull racks out rather than reaching in |
| **Flambé / open flame** | Clear overhead space; know the extinguisher location |

**Communication is a burn control.** Calling "behind you", "hot pan", "coming through", and "sharp" is not kitchen theatre — it prevents collisions in a confined space where people carry dangerous things at speed.

### Cuts

| Source | Control |
|---|---|
| **Knives** | Sharp knives; correct grip and claw hand; cut on a stable board with a damp cloth underneath |
| **Mandolins** | **Use the guard, always.** Mandolins cause severe injuries and are frequently used without the guard because it is slower |
| **Slicers and processors** | Guards in place; never bypass interlocks; isolate before cleaning |
| **Blenders and stick blenders** | **Unplug before clearing** — stick blenders cause serious hand injuries when cleared while connected |
| **Can openers, tins, broken glass** | Handle lids by the edge; sweep glass, never pick up by hand |

**A blunt knife is more dangerous than a sharp one.** It requires more force and slips more readily off the food onto the hand.

**Knife rules:**
- Carry a knife **point down, blade back, close to your side** — never with the blade extended
- **Never try to catch a falling knife.** Step back and let it land
- Never leave a knife in a sink of water where it cannot be seen
- Never use a knife as a tool for anything other than cutting food
- Wash knives individually and immediately

### Slips, Trips and Falls

Kitchen floors are a permanent slip hazard — grease, water, food debris, and constant traffic.

- **Clean spills immediately**, do not wait for a break
- Use wet floor signage
- **Non-slip enclosed footwear** — mandatory
- Keep walkways clear of boxes, trolleys, and equipment
- Adequate lighting, especially in coolrooms and storage areas
- Report damaged flooring and worn matting

### Manual Handling

- Stock deliveries, large pots, sacks of flour and rice, gas bottles, and full bins
- **Assess before lifting** — weight, distance, obstacles, grip
- Use trolleys and mechanical aids
- **Two-person lifts** for heavy or awkward loads, coordinated
- Bend at the knees, keep the load close, do not twist
- **Do not carry a pot of hot liquid you cannot control** — decant into smaller vessels

### Chemicals

Commercial kitchen cleaning chemicals are concentrated and hazardous.

- Every hazardous chemical requires a **Safety Data Sheet (SDS)**, accessible to all staff
- Read the SDS before first use
- **Never decant into an unlabelled container** — particularly not a drink bottle. People have been seriously injured drinking decanted caustic
- **Never mix chemicals.** Bleach and acid-based cleaners produce chlorine gas; bleach and ammonia produce chloramine. Both are potentially fatal in an enclosed space
- Use specified PPE — gloves, eye protection
- Correct dilution — stronger is not better and is a chemical contamination risk to food

### Gas

- Know the location of the **gas isolation valve** and how to operate it
- **If you smell gas:** do not operate electrical switches, isolate the supply, ventilate, evacuate, and report
- Never leave a gas appliance unlit with the gas on
- Report faulty pilots and ignition immediately

### Electricity

- Do not use damaged leads or equipment — tag out and report
- Keep leads away from water and heat
- **Do not operate electrical equipment with wet hands**
- Test and tag requirements apply to portable equipment
- RCD protection

### Confined and Cold Spaces

**Coolrooms and freezers:**
- Confirm the **internal release mechanism** works before entering — being locked in a freezer is a genuine and recurring hospitality incident
- Know whether there is an alarm and where it is
- Do not prop doors in a way that disables the release
- Dress appropriately for extended freezer work

## Psychosocial Hazards

WHS law now explicitly recognises psychosocial hazards, and hospitality has documented problems in this area.

| Hazard | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Long hours and fatigue** | Split shifts, late finishes, back-to-back service; fatigue impairs judgement comparably to alcohol |
| **High workload and time pressure** | Sustained pressure without recovery |
| **Bullying and harassment** | Historically normalised in kitchen culture; it is not acceptable and it is not training |
| **Sexual harassment** | Documented as a significant problem in the sector; there is a positive duty on employers to prevent it |
| **Violence and aggression** | From intoxicated patrons, particularly in venues serving alcohol |
| **Job insecurity** | Casual employment predominates |

**Bullying is repeated unreasonable behaviour directed at a worker that creates a risk to health and safety.** Being shouted at, humiliated, or threatened is not a legitimate management technique, however traditional it may be in kitchens.

**Reasonable management action carried out reasonably is not bullying** — being told your work is not to standard, being performance managed, or being directed to do your job are legitimate. The distinction is in the manner and the pattern.

**Reporting:** internal grievance procedures, the HSR, the Fair Work Commission (stop bullying orders), or the state WHS regulator. Sexual harassment can also be taken to the Australian Human Rights Commission.

**[VERIFY: Psychosocial hazard regulations and the positive duty in relation to sexual harassment have been introduced and strengthened in recent years and vary by jurisdiction. Confirm current obligations with your state regulator and the Fair Work Ombudsman.]**

## Fire Safety

Kitchens are the most common origin point for fires in hospitality venues.

### Fire Classes and Extinguishers

| Class | Fuel | Extinguisher |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Wood, paper, fabric | Water, foam, ABE powder |
| **B** | Flammable liquids | Foam, ABE powder, CO₂ |
| **E** | Electrical | **CO₂**, ABE powder — **never water** |
| **F** | **Cooking oils and fats** | **Wet chemical** |

**Class F — cooking oil fires — is the one that matters most in a kitchen.**

**Never use water on an oil fire.** Water introduced into burning oil flashes instantly to steam, ejecting burning oil violently outward and upward. This turns a contained fryer fire into a room fire and causes severe burns to anyone nearby.

**Correct response to a fryer fire:**
1. **Isolate the heat** — turn off the fryer at the appliance and the gas or power isolation
2. **Smother** — fire blanket or wet chemical extinguisher
3. **Do not move the vessel**
4. Evacuate and call 000 if not immediately controlled

### Other Fire Controls

- **Extraction canopy and duct cleaning** — grease accumulation in ducts is a major fire risk and a common cause of serious venue fires
- **Filters cleaned regularly**
- Keep exits and paths of travel clear — an obstructed fire exit is both a WHS breach and a fire safety breach
- Know the **evacuation procedure**, the assembly point, and the location of extinguishers and blankets
- Participate in evacuation drills

## Incident Response and Reporting

### First Aid — Burns

1. **Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes** — this is the single most important intervention and remains effective for up to three hours after the injury
2. **Remove jewellery and non-adhered clothing** from the area before swelling begins
3. **Do not apply ice**, butter, oil, toothpaste, or any other substance
4. **Do not burst blisters**
5. Cover loosely with a clean non-stick dressing or cling film
6. **Seek medical attention** for burns that are large, deep, on the face, hands, feet, or genitals, or that involve chemicals or electricity

**[VERIFY: Burn first aid guidance against current St John Ambulance / Australian Resuscitation Council advice.]**

### First Aid — Cuts

1. Apply direct pressure with a clean dressing
2. Elevate if practicable
3. **Cover with a brightly coloured (blue) waterproof dressing** and a glove — blue so it is visible if it falls into food
4. **A food handler with a wound that cannot be effectively covered must not handle food**
5. Seek medical attention for deep cuts, cuts that will not stop bleeding, or any cut with possible retained material

### Reporting

- Report **all** injuries, including those requiring only first aid
- Report **near misses** — they are free information about a control that failed
- Report hazards
- Complete the incident record as required

**Notifiable incidents** — reported to the WHS regulator immediately — include death, serious injury requiring immediate hospital treatment (including serious burns), and dangerous incidents such as fire, explosion, uncontrolled gas escape, or electric shock. The scene must be preserved until the regulator permits disturbance, except to make safe or assist the injured.

## Participating in the WHS System

Your obligations extend beyond following rules:

- **Attend induction and training**, and say so if you have not been trained on equipment you are asked to use
- **Participate in consultation** — toolbox talks, pre-service briefings, staff meetings
- **Report hazards** rather than working around them
- **Contribute to hazard identification** — you see things management does not
- **Follow procedures** including safe work procedures and cleaning schedules
- **Use provided PPE** and report when it is unavailable or damaged
- **Speak up** when something is unsafe, including when the person doing it is senior to you

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are a first-year apprentice, three weeks into the job. During a busy Friday service, the fryer basket jams. The chef de partie shouts at you to "just reach in and free it, we're drowning here." The fryer is on and at temperature. There is no basket tool visible on the section, and you have not been shown how to clear a jam.*

*Describe what you do and why.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I do not reach into the fryer. I say so immediately and I offer an alternative, because in the middle of service a flat "no" without a solution is not going to land.

---

**Why I will not do it:**

Fryer oil operates at around **160–190°C**. That is roughly twice the temperature of boiling water, and oil transfers heat far more efficiently to skin than water does. It also adheres — it does not run off, so it keeps burning after contact.

A hand or forearm immersed even briefly at that temperature produces **full-thickness burns**. These are among the most serious injuries that occur in commercial kitchens: they typically require hospitalisation, skin grafting, and months of recovery, and they frequently end careers because of loss of hand function.

There is no version of "reach in quickly" that is safe. The speed of the movement does not reduce the temperature of the oil.

**A secondary risk:** disturbing a jammed basket can cause hot oil to surge or splash, so the hazard extends beyond the hand that goes in.

---

**Why "we're drowning here" does not change the answer:**

The pressure is real and I understand it. But service pressure is a reason the instruction was given, not a reason it is safe. **The oil is the same temperature whether the section is quiet or slammed.**

This is also precisely the mechanism by which young workers are injured — an instruction from a senior person, delivered urgently, in an environment where questioning it feels like failing. Three weeks in, I am exactly the profile that appears in the injury statistics for this industry.

---

**The training issue:**

I have not been shown how to clear a fryer jam. Under the WHS Act, my employer has a duty to provide information, training, instruction and supervision — and that duty is **heightened for inexperienced workers**.

Being directed to perform an unfamiliar task on hot equipment with no training and no correct tool available is a failure of that duty, not a personal shortcoming on my part. **Asking is the correct response.**

---

**What I actually do — and say:**

I respond immediately and practically, not with a lecture:

*"Chef, I can't put my hand in there — can I turn it off and clear it, or do you want to grab it? There's no tool on the section."*

That does three things at once: it refuses clearly, it proposes a workable fix, and it flags the missing equipment. It takes about three seconds, which matters during service.

**The safe method:**

1. **Turn the fryer off** at the appliance
2. **Use the correct tool** — a basket hook or long-handled tool designed for the purpose. Never hands, never a cloth-wrapped hand, never improvised tongs that may not hold
3. **Stand to the side**, not directly over the fryer, in case of splash
4. Wear appropriate protection if the workplace provides heat-resistant gauntlets for fryer work
5. **If it cannot be freed with the tool while hot, it waits** until the oil is cool. A jammed basket is not worth an injury, and no dish on that section is either

**If there is genuinely no tool on site**, that is a hazard to report — the equipment required to operate the fryer safely is missing, and someone will eventually improvise with their hand.

---

**If the chef insists:**

I would not do it. I have the **right to cease unsafe work** where I have a reasonable concern of exposure to a serious risk, and that right is legally protected — I cannot lawfully be disadvantaged for exercising it. Being an apprentice does not remove it.

I would keep working — I am not refusing to work, I am refusing this specific unsafe method — and I would raise it with the head chef after service, framed as an equipment and procedure issue rather than as a complaint about the chef de partie.

---

**Afterwards:**

Two things worth raising, calmly, once service is over:

1. **The missing tool.** Every fryer section needs a basket hook. If there is not one, jams will keep being cleared by hand and eventually someone will be burned
2. **Training.** I would ask to be shown the correct procedure for clearing a jam, and for the other equipment on the section I have not been trained on. Better to ask in a quiet moment than to be asked to improvise in a busy one

I would also make sure I know where the **wet chemical extinguisher and fire blanket** are, and the fryer's gas or power isolation — because a fryer incident during service is the other thing that could happen on that section and I want to know before it does, not during.

---

**The point:**

Nobody in a kitchen intends for an apprentice to be burned. The instruction was issued under pressure by someone trying to get food out. But **the injury does not care about the intention behind the instruction**, and I am the one who would spend the next six months in a burns unit.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student refuses clearly and immediately, and grounds the refusal in the actual physics — oil temperature, heat transfer efficiency, and adherence — rather than simply asserting that it is dangerous. The recognition that speed of movement does not mitigate the hazard directly addresses the implicit logic of the instruction. Identifying themselves as fitting the injury statistic profile for young hospitality workers shows genuine awareness of why this scenario is dangerous beyond the immediate physical risk. The response given to the chef is exceptionally well judged for the context: three seconds, refuses, offers a solution, and flags the missing equipment, which is far more likely to succeed mid-service than a procedural objection. The safe method is correct and includes the important judgement that a jam which cannot be cleared safely simply waits. Correctly invoking the right to cease unsafe work while distinguishing it from refusing to work is precise. The follow-up items — the missing tool as a systemic hazard, requesting training proactively, and locating the wet chemical extinguisher before it is needed — demonstrate a worker who will not be the subject of the next incident report. Strong Competent.
