## What This Unit Is About

Food safety and food cost are both decided at the loading dock and in the coolroom, before a pan is ever picked up.

Stock accepted at the wrong temperature is unsafe no matter how well it is subsequently cooked. Stock stored badly spoils and becomes waste. Stock not rotated becomes a hazard. Stock not checked against the invoice means paying for what did not arrive.

SITXINV006 asks you to receive deliveries correctly, store products under the right conditions, rotate stock, and maintain the records that make this verifiable.

## Receiving Deliveries

Receiving is a **critical control point** in any food safety program. It is the last opportunity to reject unsafe product before it enters the premises.

### Before the Delivery

- Know what has been ordered — quantities, specifications, agreed prices
- Have the **order or purchase order** available to check against
- Have a **calibrated probe thermometer** ready, cleaned and sanitised
- Ensure receiving area is clean and clear
- Plan to put stock away **immediately** — product left on the dock is product in the danger zone

### Checking the Delivery

**1. Check the vehicle.**
- Is it clean?
- Is refrigerated product genuinely arriving refrigerated, in a functioning unit?
- Is raw product separated from ready-to-eat product in the vehicle?

**2. Check temperatures — the most important check.**

| Product | Required receiving temperature |
|---|---|
| **Chilled / refrigerated** | **5°C or below** |
| **Frozen** | **−15°C or below (frozen hard)** |
| **Hot food (if delivered hot)** | **60°C or above** |

**How to probe correctly:**
- Use a **clean, sanitised, calibrated** probe
- Probe **between two packages** for pre-packaged chilled goods, or use an infrared thermometer for a surface reading, to avoid piercing packaging unnecessarily
- Where product must be pierced, use a designated pack
- Take **multiple readings** across the load, not one
- **Record the temperature** on the receiving record

**Frozen goods:** check for evidence of thawing and refreezing — large ice crystals, ice in the bottom of the packaging, product frozen into a solid block when it should be free-flowing, or misshapen packaging. **A product that has thawed and been refrozen is unsafe and must be rejected**, even if it is currently frozen hard.

**3. Check quality and condition.**
- Packaging intact — no tears, dents in cans, swollen or leaking packs
- **Swollen or dented cans must be rejected** — swelling can indicate *Clostridium botulinum* activity, which is potentially fatal
- No signs of pest damage or contamination
- Produce: appropriate condition, no mould, no excessive bruising or wilting
- Meat and seafood: correct colour and smell, no sliminess, no off odour
- Eggs: clean, uncracked

**4. Check dates.**
- **Use By** — a **food safety** date. Product must not be used or sold after this date. Reject product arriving at or near its use-by date
- **Best Before** — a **quality** date. Product may still be safe after it, but quality declines. Whether it is accepted is a commercial decision
- Ensure remaining shelf life is adequate for how quickly you will use it

**5. Check quantity and specification against the invoice and order.**
- Count and weigh where relevant
- Confirm the specification ordered is what arrived — cut, grade, size, brand
- Check pricing against the agreed price

### Rejecting Stock

**You have the right and the obligation to reject non-conforming product.**

Grounds for rejection:
- Temperature outside the required range
- Evidence of thawing and refreezing
- Damaged, swollen, or compromised packaging
- Past or too close to use-by date
- Poor quality or condition
- Pest evidence
- Wrong product or specification
- Contamination risk in the vehicle

**Procedure:**
1. **Do not accept it into storage.** Once it is in the coolroom, it becomes yours
2. Record the rejection — what, how much, why, temperature reading if relevant
3. Have the driver acknowledge the rejection on the delivery documentation
4. Notify the supplier
5. Notify the chef or manager
6. Arrange replacement if needed for service

**Do not accept product "just this once" because service depends on it.** If the product is unsafe, it is unsafe regardless of the menu. Escalate to the chef and change the menu if necessary.

### Documentation

Receiving records are required under most food safety programs and must show:
- Date and time of delivery
- Supplier
- Products and quantities
- **Temperature readings**
- Condition on arrival
- Any rejections and the reason
- Signature of the person receiving

**[VERIFY: Receiving record requirements against your workplace's documented food safety program. Requirements are set by the program, not by general guidance.]**

## Storage

### Storage Temperatures

| Storage type | Temperature |
|---|---|
| **Dry store** | Cool, dry, well ventilated — typically 10–21°C, out of direct sunlight |
| **Coolroom / refrigerator** | **5°C or below** |
| **Freezer** | **−18°C or below** |

**Monitor and record storage temperatures** at the frequency required by the food safety program — commonly at least daily. A coolroom drifting above 5°C affects everything in it.

### The Refrigerator Storage Hierarchy

Store food from top to bottom in order of **required cooking temperature** — so that anything dripping falls onto product requiring a higher cook temperature, not onto ready-to-eat food.

| Position | Product |
|---|---|
| **Top** | Ready-to-eat food, cooked food, dairy, desserts |
| | Raw whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb |
| | Raw mince and raw seafood |
| **Bottom** | **Raw poultry** (highest required cooking temperature) |

**Additional rules:**
- **Cover everything.** Uncovered food is exposed to contamination and dries out
- **Store off the floor** — minimum clearance to allow cleaning and prevent pest harbourage
- **Do not overload** — air must circulate. An overpacked coolroom does not maintain temperature evenly
- Keep raw and ready-to-eat **physically separated**, ideally in different units where volume allows
- **Do not store food in opened cans** — decant into food-grade containers

### Dry Storage

- Cool, dry, well ventilated, away from heat sources and direct sunlight
- **Off the floor on shelving** — pest control and cleaning
- Away from walls to allow inspection behind
- Containers sealed and food-grade
- **Chemicals stored completely separately from food** — never above food, never in the same area
- Heavy items on lower shelves
- Well lit, so stock can be inspected and dates read

### Freezer Storage

- **−18°C or below**
- Wrapped or sealed to prevent **freezer burn** — dehydration causing dry, discoloured patches
- Labelled with contents and date frozen
- **Do not refreeze thawed product**
- Do not overload; allow air circulation
- Defrost the unit per schedule — ice build-up reduces efficiency

### Thawing

**Thawing is a critical control point** and is done incorrectly more often than almost anything else in a kitchen.

**Acceptable methods:**
1. **In the refrigerator at 5°C or below** — the preferred method. Slow but safe. Plan for it: a large frozen item may take 24–48 hours
2. **Under cold running potable water**
3. **In a microwave**, if the food is cooked immediately afterwards
4. **As part of the cooking process**, for suitable items

**Unacceptable:**
- **Thawing at room temperature on a bench.** The outside of the product enters the danger zone and remains there for hours while the centre is still frozen. This is a leading cause of food-borne illness
- Thawing in warm or hot water
- Thawing in standing water

**When thawing in the refrigerator, place the item on the bottom shelf in a container** to catch drip — thawing raw meat drips, and drip from raw poultry onto anything below is a serious cross-contamination event.

## Stock Rotation

### FIFO — First In, First Out

Older stock is used before newer stock. When new stock arrives, it goes **behind** or **under** existing stock, not in front of it.

**Implementation:**
- Label everything with the date received, or the date made and the use-by date
- Physically position older stock for access first
- Check dates during every stocktake and every prep session
- Remove and record anything out of date

**FIFO fails when new stock is put away quickly at the front because it is faster.** This is how product at the back of a shelf reaches six months past date without anyone noticing.

### FEFO — First Expired, First Out

A refinement: use by **expiry date** rather than receipt date. More accurate where products have different shelf lives or where a later delivery has an earlier expiry.

### Labelling

Every prepared or decanted item requires:
- **Product name**
- **Date made or opened**
- **Use-by date**
- Prepared by, where the program requires it

**An unlabelled container in a coolroom is a container of unknown age and unknown contents.** It cannot be safely used and must be discarded — which is pure waste created by a five-second omission.

## Stock Control

### Stocktake

A physical count of stock on hand, conducted at set intervals.

**Purposes:**
- Determine the value of stock held
- Calculate **cost of goods sold**
- Identify variance between expected and actual stock
- Identify theft, waste, over-portioning, or recording errors
- Inform ordering

**Method:**
- Count systematically, section by section, so nothing is missed or double counted
- Count in consistent units
- Record accurately, including partial containers
- Two people counting and recording reduces error

### Cost of Goods Sold

**COGS = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock**

**Example:** Opening stock $8,000. Purchases during the period $22,000. Closing stock $7,000.

COGS = 8,000 + 22,000 − 7,000 = **$23,000**

**Food cost percentage = (COGS ÷ Food sales) × 100**

If food sales for the period were $76,000:

Food cost % = (23,000 ÷ 76,000) × 100 = **30.3%**

**Food cost percentage is the headline measure of kitchen cost control.** A percentage drifting upward indicates over-portioning, waste, spoilage, theft, price increases not passed on, or a shift in sales mix toward lower-margin dishes.

**[VERIFY: Target food cost percentages vary substantially by venue type and business model. Use the venue's own target, not a general benchmark.]**

### Par Levels

The standard quantity of an item to hold. Ordering brings stock back up to par.

**Set from:** usage rate, delivery frequency, lead time, shelf life, and storage capacity.

**Too high** ties up cash and risks spoilage. **Too low** risks running out mid-service.

## Pest Control

Pests contaminate food, damage stock, and indicate poor practice. A pest sighting during a health inspection is a serious finding.

**Signs to report immediately:**
- Droppings
- Gnaw marks on packaging or structure
- Nests or nesting material
- Live or dead insects
- Smear marks along walls (rodents)
- Damaged or opened packaging
- Unusual smells

**Prevention is a storage discipline:**
- Store off the floor and away from walls
- Seal all containers
- Remove waste promptly; keep bins closed
- Clean spills immediately, including behind and under shelving
- Keep external doors closed or screened
- Inspect deliveries for pests before they enter

**Report to the chef or manager immediately. Do not use domestic insecticides in a food area** — pest control in a food business must be carried out by a licensed pest controller using approved methods, and records maintained.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are receiving the Tuesday morning delivery. The driver is in a hurry and has three more drops. The delivery includes:*

*1. Fresh chicken breast, 10kg — probes at 8°C*
*2. Frozen prawns, 5kg — frozen hard, but there is a 15mm layer of ice in the bottom of the box and the prawns are frozen together in a solid block*
*3. Cream, 6 × 1L — probes at 4°C, use-by date is in two days*
*4. Tinned tomatoes, 1 carton — one tin has a noticeable dent on the seam*
*5. Mixed leaf salad, 4kg — good condition, correct temperature*

*The chicken is on tonight's menu. What do you do with each item?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I work through each item on its own merits and I do not let the driver's schedule set my pace. Receiving is the last point at which unsafe product can be stopped, and once it goes into the coolroom it is ours.

---

**Item 1 — Chicken at 8°C: REJECT**

Chilled product must be received at **5°C or below**. At 8°C this is 3 degrees into the temperature danger zone.

**I do not know how long it has been there.** It may have been loaded warm, the vehicle unit may be failing, or it may have been sitting on the previous three drops. I have no way of establishing the cumulative time in the danger zone, and raw poultry is a high-risk product — *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter* are the two most common causes of food-borne illness notifications in Australia and both are strongly associated with poultry.

**Before rejecting I would:**
- Take **multiple readings** across the load, not one — a single warm pack at the door of the vehicle is different from the whole consignment being warm
- Check the vehicle's temperature display and whether the refrigeration is running

If the whole load is at 8°C, it is rejected.

**The complication: it is on tonight's menu.**

That is a genuine operational problem and it does not change the decision. **Unsafe product is unsafe regardless of the menu.** What I do is escalate immediately so the kitchen has the maximum time to respond:

- Reject the chicken and record it
- **Tell the chef straight away** — do not discover this at 4pm
- The options then belong to the chef: emergency order from another supplier, a substitute protein, or removing the dish. With a morning delivery there is most of a day to solve it

The one thing that would be indefensible is accepting it, saying nothing, and cooking it tonight on the reasoning that it will be cooked through. Cooking kills bacteria; it does not remove heat-stable toxins produced during the time the product spent warm.

---

**Item 2 — Prawns: REJECT**

The prawns are frozen hard **now**, but the evidence says they have not been continuously frozen.

- A **15mm layer of ice in the bottom of the box** is meltwater that has refrozen. That water came out of the product
- Prawns **frozen together in a solid block** when they should be free-flowing individually quick frozen product is the classic sign of thaw and refreeze

**Thawed and refrozen seafood is unsafe.** During the thawed period, bacteria multiplied, and refreezing stops further growth but does not undo it or destroy any toxin produced. Freezing is not a corrective action.

It is also badly quality-degraded — texture will be mushy and the product will weep on thawing.

Reject, record, photograph if the workplace practice allows, and notify the supplier. This is worth raising with the supplier as a cold chain issue, not just a single rejection — if their frozen chain is failing, it will happen again.

---

**Item 3 — Cream at 4°C, two days to use-by: ACCEPT WITH A DECISION**

**Temperature is fine** — 4°C is within spec.

**The date is the issue.** Two days of remaining shelf life on 6 litres of cream is short. This is not a safety rejection; the product is safe today. It is a **commercial and practical** decision:

- **Will we use 6 litres in two days?** If the kitchen goes through that much cream in normal service, accept it and label it clearly for priority use
- **If we will not**, most of it becomes waste and we have paid for it

**What I do:** flag it to the chef rather than deciding alone, because usage rate is their knowledge not mine. My recommendation would be to accept only what we will genuinely use and query the short dating with the supplier — receiving product this close to its use-by should not be routine, and if it is a pattern it needs addressing.

If accepted, it goes to the **front** of the coolroom, clearly labelled, and everyone on shift is told to use it first. FEFO applies — it gets used before cream received earlier with a later expiry.

---

**Item 4 — Dented tin: REJECT THE TIN**

**A dent on the seam is the serious kind.** Dents on the body of a can are generally cosmetic. **Dents on a seam or a rim can compromise the hermetic seal**, allowing micro-organisms in.

The specific concern is ***Clostridium botulinum*** — an anaerobic organism that thrives in the low-oxygen, low-acid environment inside a can. Botulism is rare but is potentially fatal and the toxin is not reliably destroyed by normal cooking.

**Reject the single tin.** The rest of the carton can be accepted after checking each tin for dents, swelling, rust, or leakage.

**Also reject any swollen tin** — swelling indicates gas production from microbial activity inside and is an absolute rejection, no exceptions.

---

**Item 5 — Salad: ACCEPT**

Good condition, correct temperature. Accept.

Store it **above raw products** in the coolroom — it is ready-to-eat and receives no further cooking step, so it must not be exposed to drip from raw meat, poultry or seafood.

Use promptly; leaf salad has short shelf life and degrades quickly once the cold chain is broken even briefly.

---

**The driver being in a hurry:**

I acknowledge it and I do not rush. Three more drops is the driver's schedule problem, not a food safety consideration.

I would be efficient and courteous — I am not making a point, I am doing the check properly — but every item still gets probed, inspected, and dated. **The pressure to hurry a delivery check is exactly the pressure that lets unsafe product into a kitchen.**

The driver must **acknowledge the rejections on the delivery documentation** before leaving. If they refuse or leave without signing, I record that fact and notify the chef and the supplier immediately.

---

**Documentation:**

| Item | Decision | Record |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken 10kg | Rejected | 8°C on receipt, multiple readings, above 5°C limit |
| Prawns 5kg | Rejected | Evidence of thaw/refreeze — ice layer, product blocked |
| Cream 6L | Referred to chef | 4°C, use-by 2 days — short dating queried |
| Tomatoes | 1 tin rejected | Seam dent, seal integrity risk. Balance accepted |
| Salad 4kg | Accepted | Correct temp and condition |

All temperatures, rejections and reasons recorded on the receiving log, driver signature obtained, chef notified about the chicken **immediately** — not at the end of the round.

---

**The principle:**

Everything I accept, I own. Once the chicken is in the coolroom it is our product, our liability, and our decision to use or discard. **The two minutes it takes to probe properly at the dock is the cheapest food safety control in the entire operation.**

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. Each item is assessed on its own criteria with correct decisions throughout. The chicken rejection is well handled: the student takes multiple readings before rejecting rather than condemning a load on one probe, correctly identifies poultry as high-risk with the relevant pathogens named, and — critically — refuses to let the menu dependency alter the decision while escalating immediately so the kitchen has maximum time to respond. The rebuttal that cooking will not remove toxins is precise. The prawn analysis correctly reads both indicators of thaw and refreeze and states clearly that refreezing is not a corrective action. The cream is correctly identified as a commercial rather than a safety decision and appropriately referred to the chef, with FEFO applied on acceptance — a distinction many candidates miss by treating every issue as a rejection. The tin analysis correctly distinguishes seam dents from body dents and identifies the specific anaerobic hazard. Salad storage placement reflects the ready-to-eat hierarchy. The handling of driver pressure is professionally judged — efficient without being hurried — and the requirement for driver acknowledgement of rejections is correctly included. The closing observation that everything accepted is owned captures exactly why this control point matters. Strong Competent.
