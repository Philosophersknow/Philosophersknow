## What This Unit Is About

Stocks and sauces are the foundation of classical and contemporary kitchen practice. A kitchen that makes good stock makes good soup, good sauce, good braises, and good risotto. A kitchen that does not is limited regardless of the skill applied downstream.

SITHCCC029 asks you to produce stocks, the mother sauces and their derivatives, and the main soup classifications — to a consistent commercial standard, safely, and within realistic service timeframes.

## Stocks

A stock is a flavoured liquid made by simmering bones, vegetables, and aromatics in water. It is the base ingredient for sauces, soups, and braises.

### Types of Stock

| Stock | French | Base | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **White stock** | Fond blanc | Un-roasted bones (veal, chicken) | Pale |
| **Brown stock** | Fond brun | Roasted bones and vegetables | Deep brown |
| **Fish stock** | Fumet | Fish bones and trim | Pale |
| **Vegetable stock** | — | Vegetables and aromatics only | Varies |
| **Court bouillon** | — | Water, acid, aromatics — no bones | Pale |

### Components

**Bones** — provide body through collagen converting to gelatin. Veal bones are prized because they are high in collagen and neutral in flavour. Chicken carcasses and wings are the practical everyday choice.

**Mirepoix** — the standard aromatic vegetable base:
- **Classic ratio: 50% onion, 25% carrot, 25% celery** (2:1:1 by weight)
- **White mirepoix** substitutes parsnip or leek for carrot, to keep white stocks pale

**Bouquet garni** — a bundle of herbs, typically thyme, bay leaf, and parsley stalks, tied or wrapped in leek for easy removal.

**Sachet d'épices** — aromatics in a muslin bag: peppercorns, bay, thyme, parsley stalks, sometimes clove and garlic.

### Method — White Stock

1. **Blanch the bones** (optional but produces a cleaner stock) — cover with cold water, bring to the boil, drain, and rinse. This removes blood and scum
2. **Cover with cold water.** Starting cold allows proteins to leach out and coagulate at the surface where they can be skimmed. Starting hot seals them into the liquid and clouds the stock
3. **Bring slowly to a simmer** — never a boil
4. **Skim** the scum and fat that rises, repeatedly, especially in the first 30 minutes
5. **Add mirepoix and aromatics** partway through — vegetables added at the start will overcook and turn bitter
6. **Simmer** for the appropriate time
7. **Strain** gently through a chinois or muslin — do not press the solids, as this clouds the stock
8. **Cool rapidly** and refrigerate (see food safety below)

### Method — Brown Stock

As above, but bones and mirepoix are **roasted first** until deeply browned. Tomato paste is often added and cooked out (*pincé*) for colour and depth. The roasting tray is **deglazed** and that liquid added to the stock — the fond on the tray carries substantial flavour.

### Simmering Times

| Stock | Time |
|---|---|
| Fish (fumet) | 20–30 minutes — longer extracts bitterness |
| Vegetable | 30–45 minutes |
| Chicken | 3–4 hours |
| Veal / beef white | 6–8 hours |
| Brown veal / beef | 8–12 hours |

### The Rules of Good Stock

**1. Never boil.** Boiling emulsifies fat and agitates particulates into the liquid, producing a cloudy, greasy stock. Simmer gently — occasional bubbles breaking the surface.

**2. Start with cold water.** For clarity, as explained above.

**3. Skim consistently.** Impurities removed early do not return.

**4. Do not salt stock.** Stock is a base ingredient that will be reduced later, sometimes by 90%. Salting at stock stage makes the final sauce inedible. Season the finished dish, not the stock.

**5. Strain gently.** Pressing the solids forces fine particles through.

## The Mother Sauces

Classical French cuisine defines a small set of foundation sauces from which many others derive.

| Mother sauce | Liquid | Thickener | Common derivatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Béchamel** | Milk | White roux | Mornay (cheese), soubise (onion), cream sauce |
| **Velouté** | White stock | Blond roux | Suprême (chicken velouté + cream), allemande, bercy |
| **Espagnole** | Brown stock | Brown roux | Demi-glace, bordelaise, chasseur, madère |
| **Tomato** | Tomato | Reduction (sometimes roux) | Provençale, creole, puttanesca |
| **Hollandaise** | Butter | Egg yolk emulsion | Béarnaise, mousseline, maltaise, choron |

### Roux

A roux is equal parts by weight of fat and flour, cooked together. It is the most common thickener in classical sauce work.

| Roux | Cooking | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **White** | Cooked briefly, no colour | Béchamel |
| **Blond** | Cooked to pale straw | Velouté |
| **Brown** | Cooked to deep brown, nutty aroma | Espagnole |

**Critical principle: the darker the roux, the less it thickens.** Prolonged cooking breaks down starch, so a brown roux has substantially less thickening power than a white roux of the same weight. A brown sauce therefore needs more roux, or additional reduction, to reach the same consistency.

**Avoiding lumps:** combine roux and liquid at **different temperatures** — hot roux with cold liquid, or cold roux with hot liquid. Adding hot to hot causes the starch to seize into lumps before it can disperse. Add liquid gradually and whisk continuously.

**Cooking out:** a sauce thickened with roux must simmer long enough to cook out the raw flour taste — generally at least 20 minutes for a béchamel.

### Other Thickening Agents

| Agent | Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Beurre manié** | Equal parts raw butter and flour kneaded together, whisked into simmering liquid at the end | Fast adjustment of consistency; must be simmered briefly to cook out |
| **Slurry** (cornflour/arrowroot + cold water) | Whisked into simmering liquid | Gluten-free; produces a glossier, more translucent result; can thin again if boiled too long |
| **Liaison** (egg yolk + cream) | Tempered into hot liquid off the boil | Enriches and lightly thickens. **Must not boil** — the yolks will scramble |
| **Reduction** | Simmering to evaporate water | Concentrates flavour as well as thickening. The most flavourful method |
| **Purée** | Blending cooked vegetables into the liquid | Common in contemporary sauces and soups |
| **Monter au beurre** | Whisking cold butter into a sauce off the heat at the end | Adds gloss, richness, and slight body |

### Hollandaise — Emulsion Technique

Hollandaise is an emulsion of butter in egg yolk, stabilised by lecithin in the yolk.

**Method:**
1. Make a reduction of vinegar, peppercorns, and shallot (or use lemon juice)
2. Whisk yolks with the reduction over a bain-marie until thick and ribboning — the *sabayon* stage
3. Remove from heat and whisk in warm clarified butter **slowly**, in a thin steady stream
4. Season and adjust with lemon juice

**Why it splits:**
- **Butter added too fast** — the emulsion cannot incorporate it
- **Too hot** — the yolks scramble; keep below approximately 65°C
- **Too cold** — the butter solidifies and the emulsion breaks

**Rescuing a split hollandaise:** start a fresh yolk with a little water in a clean bowl, whisk to a sabayon, and slowly whisk the broken sauce into it.

**Food safety note:** hollandaise contains lightly cooked egg and is held warm — squarely in the temperature danger zone. It **must not be held for extended periods**. Standard practice is to make it close to service and discard rather than hold it beyond a short window. This is a genuine food safety risk, particularly for vulnerable diners.

## Soups

### Classifications

| Category | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear — broth/bouillon** | Unthickened, flavourful liquid with garnish | Chicken broth, pho |
| **Clear — consommé** | Clarified stock, perfectly transparent | Consommé royale, consommé julienne |
| **Thick — purée** | Thickened by puréeing its own main ingredient | Pea and ham, lentil, potato and leek |
| **Thick — cream** | Velouté or béchamel base, finished with cream | Cream of mushroom, cream of chicken |
| **Thick — bisque** | Shellfish-based, traditionally thickened with rice | Prawn bisque, lobster bisque |
| **Thick — chowder** | Chunky, milk or cream base | Seafood chowder, corn chowder |
| **Cold** | Served chilled | Vichyssoise, gazpacho |
| **National / regional** | Characteristic of a cuisine | Minestrone, French onion, tom yum, laksa, borscht |

### Consommé — Clarification

Consommé is a stock clarified to complete transparency using a **clarification raft**.

**Method:**
1. Combine lean minced meat, egg whites, mirepoix, and tomato — the *clearmeat*
2. Mix into **cold** stock and stir thoroughly
3. Bring slowly to a bare simmer, stirring until it begins to set — then **stop stirring**
4. The proteins coagulate, rise, and form a raft that traps impurities as convection carries liquid through it
5. Make a small hole in the raft and simmer very gently for around an hour
6. **Ladle** the clear liquid out through the hole — do not pour, which would break the raft
7. Strain through muslin and degrease

**Why it fails:** stirring after the raft forms, boiling (which breaks the raft up), or pouring rather than ladling.

### Soup Consistency

The most common commercial soup fault is incorrect consistency.

- **Too thin** — reduce, or add a thickener; check whether it was over-diluted at the start
- **Too thick** — let down with stock, not water. Water dilutes flavour as well as texture
- **Note that soup thickens as it cools and as starches continue to swell.** A soup adjusted to perfect consistency in the pot will be too thick on the pass. Aim slightly looser than the target

**Correct consistency for a cream soup** is often described as coating the back of a spoon — thick enough to leave a clean line when a finger is drawn through, thin enough to pour freely.

### Finishing Soups

- **Season at the end**, after reduction, and taste again after adding cream (dairy mutes salt perception)
- **Cream added at the end**, off the boil — boiling cream in an acidic soup can curdle it
- **Acid to lift** — a small amount of lemon juice or vinegar brightens a flat soup remarkably. If a soup tastes dull but is correctly salted, it usually needs acid
- **Garnish appropriately** — croutons, herbs, cream swirl, oil. Garnish should relate to the soup, not simply decorate it
- **Serve at correct temperature** — hot soups at 60°C or above at service; cold soups properly chilled

## Food Safety in Stock and Soup Production

This is where stocks and soups present a **specific and serious risk**, and it is heavily assessed.

Stocks and soups are:
- High-protein and high-moisture — ideal bacterial growth media
- Produced in **large volumes**, which cool very slowly
- Frequently **cooled, stored, and reheated** — multiple passes through the danger zone

### The Cooling Requirement

Cooked food must be cooled through the danger zone within the specified times:

| Phase | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **60°C → 21°C** | Within **2 hours** |
| **21°C → 5°C** | Within a further **4 hours** |

**A large stockpot left on the bench will not achieve this.** A 20-litre pot of stock can take many hours to cool through the danger zone, spending that entire period at bacteria-optimal temperatures.

**Correct rapid cooling methods:**
- **Divide into shallow containers** — maximises surface area, the single most effective step
- **Ice bath / ice paddle** — an ice wand placed in the pot cools from the centre outward
- **Blast chiller** where available
- **Ice as part of the recipe** — adding ice to reduce temperature where dilution is acceptable
- **Stir during cooling** to break up temperature stratification

**Do not seal containers until cool** — trapped steam condenses and slows cooling.

### Reheating

Reheat **rapidly** to 60°C or above. Do not reheat slowly on a low burner — that walks the food back up through the danger zone.

**Reheat only once.** Repeated cooling and reheating cycles compound risk.

### Storage

- Label with contents and **date of production**
- Store above raw ingredients in the refrigerator
- Typical refrigerated life for stock is 3–4 days; freeze for longer storage
- **[VERIFY: Storage life against your workplace food safety program — this must follow the documented program, not general guidance.]**

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You arrive for your shift at 3pm. A 15-litre pot of chicken velouté-based cream of mushroom soup was made this morning for tonight's function. You find it sitting on the bench beside the stove, covered with a lid. The kitchen is warm. You probe it and it reads 24°C. The chef who made it has gone home. The function is at 7pm for 120 guests, and this is the entrée.*

*What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I would discard the soup and tell the head chef immediately. It cannot be served.

I know that is a serious call four hours before a 120-cover function, which is exactly why I would raise it straight away rather than deal with it myself.

---

**Why it must be discarded:**

The soup was made "this morning" and is at **24°C on a warm bench, covered, at 3pm.**

The cooling requirement is:
- 60°C → 21°C within **2 hours**
- 21°C → 5°C within a further **4 hours**

This soup has not been cooled at all. It has been left at ambient to find its own temperature.

**The volume makes it worse, not better.** Fifteen litres in a single stockpot has a very poor surface-area-to-volume ratio. A pot that size cools extremely slowly, and the **centre** of the mass will still be considerably warmer than 24°C — the probe at the edge is reading the coolest part. The core has very likely been sitting in the middle of the danger zone for most of the day.

**The lid makes it worse still** — it traps heat and slows cooling further.

Even taking the most generous view — that it came off the heat at, say, 11am — that is four hours in the danger zone with no active cooling, and it is still at 24°C with no prospect of reaching 5°C in the required window. It has exceeded the four-hour limit and must be discarded.

---

**Why reheating will not fix it:**

This is the point I would want to be clear about, because "just boil it hard, that'll kill anything" is a genuinely common belief in kitchens and it is wrong.

Reheating to 75°C will kill most vegetative bacteria. It will **not** destroy **heat-stable bacterial toxins**.

Two organisms matter particularly here:

- ***Bacillus cereus*** — associated with cooked starchy and vegetable foods held at ambient. It produces an emetic toxin that survives boiling.
- ***Staphylococcus aureus*** — introduced by food handlers, produces a heat-stable enterotoxin.

Both had ideal conditions: a high-protein, high-moisture, low-acid food, held for hours at optimal growth temperature. If toxin has been produced, no amount of reheating makes it safe.

**The soup will look, smell, and taste completely normal.** That is the trap. Sensory assessment cannot detect this.

---

**The consequences if I got this wrong:**

120 guests. A function is likely to include people in vulnerable groups — older guests, pregnant women, possibly immunocompromised people. A mass food poisoning event traced to a single service would mean serious illness, likely notification to the local council environmental health officer and state health authority, possible closure, and potential prosecution of the business.

Against that, the cost of the ingredients in the pot is trivial.

---

**What I actually do — in order:**

**1. Do not discard it yet — isolate it and get the head chef.**

I would move it out of the way, label it clearly **"DO NOT USE — TEMPERATURE BREACH"**, and find the head chef immediately. The decision to bin 15 litres of function soup is theirs, and they need to know regardless because it affects the service.

I would report it factually: *"Chef, the mushroom soup's been on the bench since this morning — it's reading 24 at the edge and it's never been chilled. It's outside the four-hour rule. I don't think we can serve it."*

**2. Document it.**

Record the time I found it, the temperature reading, the location, and the estimated time it came off the heat. This goes in the temperature log or corrective action record required under the food safety program. If we have a HACCP-based program, this is a **critical limit breach at the cooling CCP** and the corrective action is disposal.

**3. Move immediately to the replacement, because time is the real problem.**

Four hours to service is tight but workable for a cream soup — this is not a dish that needs long cooking.

- Check what stock we have. If there is good chicken stock in the walk-in, I can build a fresh velouté quickly
- If there is no stock, the fastest route is a **purée-style mushroom soup** — sweat onion and mushroom, add stock or water, simmer, blend, finish with cream. That is achievable in well under two hours
- Check mushroom stock levels in the coolroom; if short, someone needs to go to the supplier now, not at 5pm
- **Make it in two smaller pots rather than one large one** — faster to cook, faster to bring to service temperature, and easier to hold safely

**4. Get the cooling right this time.**

If any is made ahead rather than held hot, it goes straight into shallow gastronorms over an ice bath, uncovered until cool, stirred, and probed and logged on the way down.

For a function, the better approach is to **hold it hot at 60°C or above** rather than cool and reheat at all — that avoids the danger zone entirely.

**5. Raise the systemic issue afterwards.**

Not to blame the morning chef, but because the same thing will happen again. The questions worth asking:
- Is there a documented cooling procedure people actually know?
- Do we have enough shallow containers and an ice paddle, or is a single big stockpot the only option available?
- Is there a temperature log for cooling, and is anyone checking it?
- Does the closing chef have a handover point where in-progress cooling gets flagged?

**If the only vessel in the kitchen is a 15-litre stockpot, people will keep cooling in a 15-litre stockpot.** The fix is equipment and procedure, not just telling someone off.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student reaches the correct decision immediately and does not equivocate, while showing genuine awareness of the commercial pressure that makes the call difficult. The reasoning is technically precise: correct citation of the two-stage cooling requirement, and — critically — the recognition that a probe at the edge of a 15-litre pot understates the core temperature, which is the detail that makes this scenario worse than it first appears. The explanation of heat-stable toxins, naming *Bacillus cereus* and *Staphylococcus aureus* with their relevance to this specific food, correctly demolishes the "just boil it" response that this scenario is designed to elicit. The warning that the soup will taste normal addresses the sensory-assessment fallacy directly. Procedurally the student does everything right: isolates and labels rather than unilaterally discarding, escalates to the person whose decision it is, documents against the HACCP cooling CCP, and then — importantly — solves the actual operational problem with a realistic replacement plan and a sensible suggestion to hold hot rather than cool and reheat. The closing systemic analysis, particularly the observation that equipment availability drives the behaviour, demonstrates thinking well beyond the immediate incident. Strong Competent.
