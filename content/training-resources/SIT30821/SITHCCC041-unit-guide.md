## What This Unit Is About

Baking is chemistry with a deadline. Unlike most savoury cookery, where a dish can be tasted and adjusted throughout, baked goods are committed at the point of mixing — by the time you can assess the result, every decision has already been made.

This makes **accuracy** the defining skill. A cook who works by eye and feel on the savoury side must work by scale and thermometer here.

SITHCCC041 asks you to produce a range of cakes, pastries and breads to commercial standard, understand why the methods work, and diagnose faults.

---

## The Core Principle: Weigh, Do Not Measure

**Professional baking works by weight, not volume.**

A cup of flour can vary by 20% or more depending on how it is scooped, whether it is sifted, and how compacted it is. That variation destroys a formula.

**Digital scales, every time.** Small quantities — yeast, salt, raising agents — require scales accurate to 1g or better.

### Baker's Percentage

The standard notation in professional baking. **Every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the total flour weight**, and flour is always 100%.

| Ingredient | Baker's % | For 2kg flour |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100% | 2000g |
| Water | 65% | 1300g |
| Salt | 2% | 40g |
| Yeast (fresh) | 2% | 40g |

**Total percentage exceeds 100% — this is normal and correct.**

**Why it is used:** the formula scales instantly to any batch size, and recipes can be compared directly. A dough at 65% hydration behaves the same way whether you are making 2kg or 200kg.

**Scaling:** to make a batch using 3.5kg of flour, multiply each percentage by 3500 ÷ 100 = 35.
- Water: 65 × 35 = 2275g
- Salt: 2 × 35 = 70g

## Ingredient Functions

Understanding what each ingredient *does* is what allows you to diagnose a fault rather than just follow a recipe.

### Flour

| Type | Protein | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Baker's / strong / bread flour** | High (~11–13%) | Bread — develops strong gluten |
| **Plain / all-purpose** | Medium (~9–11%) | General baking |
| **Cake / soft flour** | Low (~7–9%) | Cakes — tender crumb |
| **Self-raising** | Medium, with raising agent added | Scones, quick cakes |
| **Wholemeal** | Contains bran and germ | Denser; bran cuts gluten strands |

**Gluten** forms when the proteins glutenin and gliadin hydrate and are worked. It creates the elastic network that traps gas.

**You want gluten development in bread. You want to minimise it in cakes and pastry** — over-worked cake batter and over-worked pastry both become tough. This is the single most important flour concept in the unit.

### Liquid
Hydrates flour to form gluten, dissolves salt and sugar, activates raising agents, creates steam for leavening, and controls dough consistency.

**Hydration percentage** determines dough handling. A 60% dough is firm and easy to handle; an 80% dough is slack, sticky, and produces a more open crumb.

### Fat
- **Shortens** — coats flour proteins, physically preventing gluten formation, producing tenderness (hence "shortening")
- Adds flavour and richness
- Aids aeration when creamed with sugar
- Extends shelf life
- Creates flaky layers in laminated pastry

**Butter** delivers flavour but has a low melting point and contains water. **Pastry margarine** has a higher melting point, which makes lamination easier in a warm kitchen.

### Sugar
- Sweetens
- **Tenderises** — competes with flour for water, limiting gluten development
- Aids aeration in creaming
- Contributes to browning via caramelisation and the **Maillard reaction**
- Retains moisture, extending shelf life
- Feeds yeast

### Eggs
- **Structure** — egg proteins coagulate on heating and set the crumb
- **Aeration** — whipped whole eggs or whites trap air
- **Emulsification** — lecithin in the yolk binds fat and water
- **Enrichment** — flavour, colour, moisture

### Salt
- Flavour — baked goods without salt taste flat and unfinished
- **Strengthens gluten**
- **Controls yeast activity** — slows fermentation to a manageable rate

**Salt must not contact yeast directly** in concentrated form — it will kill or damage it. Add them to opposite sides of the flour.

**Forgetting the salt in bread is a common and obvious error** — the dough ferments too fast, over-proves, and the finished loaf is pale, bland and poorly structured.

### Raising Agents

| Agent | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Yeast** | Biological — ferments sugars producing CO₂ and alcohol |
| **Bicarbonate of soda** | Chemical — requires an **acid** (buttermilk, yoghurt, lemon, honey, cocoa) to react and produce CO₂ |
| **Baking powder** | Chemical — bicarbonate plus an acid already included; "double acting" reacts on wetting and again on heating |
| **Air** | Mechanical — creamed, whisked, or folded in |
| **Steam** | Physical — water converting to steam expands and lifts (puff pastry, choux) |

**Bicarbonate without sufficient acid leaves a soapy, metallic taste and a yellow-brown discolouration.** Recipes using bicarbonate always contain an acid — if you substitute an ingredient and remove the acid, the raising fails.

## Cake Methods

| Method | Technique | Products |
|---|---|---|
| **Creaming** | Beat fat and sugar until light and aerated, add eggs gradually, fold in flour | Butter cakes, pound cake, muffins (creamed) |
| **Whisking / foaming** | Whisk eggs and sugar to a ribbon, fold in flour | Sponge, genoise, Swiss roll |
| **Melting** | Melt fat and sugar, combine with dry | Gingerbread, some muffins |
| **Rubbing in** | Rub fat into flour to breadcrumb texture | Scones, rock cakes |
| **All-in-one** | Combine all ingredients, beat briefly | Quick cakes with extra raising agent |

### Creaming
- **Fat must be at room temperature.** Cold fat will not cream; melted fat will not hold air
- Beat until pale and visibly increased in volume — this is aeration, and it is the leavening
- **Add eggs gradually.** Adding cold eggs too quickly causes **curdling** — the emulsion breaks and the batter looks split. This traps less air and gives a coarser crumb. Fix by adding a spoonful of the measured flour
- **Fold flour in gently.** Beating at this stage develops gluten and toughens the cake

### Whisking
- Eggs and sugar whisked to the **ribbon stage** — the mixture falls from the whisk and holds a visible ribbon on the surface for a few seconds
- Warming the eggs slightly increases volume
- **Fold flour in very gently** — a whisked sponge has no chemical raising agent, so all the lift is trapped air. Knocking it out cannot be recovered
- Bake immediately; the foam begins collapsing as soon as it stands

### Rubbing In
- **Cold fat**, rubbed into flour with fingertips until it resembles breadcrumbs
- **Work quickly and lightly** — warm hands melt the fat
- Add liquid and combine **minimally**. Scones toughen instantly with overworking
- Handle the dough as little as possible

## Pastry

| Pastry | Method | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Short (pâte brisée)** | Rubbing in; fat coats flour | Tarts, quiches, pies |
| **Sweet (pâte sucrée)** | Creamed, with sugar and egg | Sweet tart shells |
| **Puff** | Laminated — many layers of dough and fat | Vol-au-vents, palmiers, mille-feuille |
| **Rough puff** | Simplified lamination | Quick alternative to puff |
| **Choux** | Cooked paste, high egg, raised by steam | Profiteroles, éclairs, gougères |
| **Filo** | Very thin sheets, layered with fat | Baklava, strudel, parcels |
| **Hot water crust** | Boiling water and fat | Raised pies |

### Short Pastry — The Rules

1. **Everything cold** — fat, water, hands, bench. Warm fat melts into the flour instead of remaining as discrete pieces, giving a greasy, hard result
2. **Minimum handling** — every touch develops gluten
3. **Minimum water** — just enough to bring it together
4. **Rest before rolling** — a minimum of 30 minutes chilled. Resting relaxes the gluten (reducing shrinkage) and firms the fat
5. **Rest again after lining the tin**, before baking, for the same reason

**Shrinkage in the tin is caused by insufficient resting, or by stretching the pastry to fit rather than easing it in.** Stretched gluten contracts when heated.

### Blind Baking
Baking a pastry case before adding a wet filling, to prevent a soggy base.
- Line with baking paper and fill with baking beans or rice
- Bake until set, remove the weights, return to dry the base
- Egg wash the base and briefly return to the oven to seal it against a wet filling

### Lamination — Puff Pastry

Puff is built from alternating layers of dough (détrempe) and fat, created by repeated folding and rolling ("turns"). During baking, water in the dough turns to steam and forces the layers apart, while the fat keeps them separate.

**Critical points:**
- **Fat and dough must be the same consistency.** If the fat is harder, it breaks into pieces; if softer, it squeezes out and merges into the dough. Either way the layers are lost
- **Rest and chill between turns.** Working continuously warms the fat and develops gluten
- **Do not let the fat break through** the dough
- **Cut cleanly with a sharp knife.** A blunt blade or a rolled edge compresses and seals the layers, and the pastry will not rise at that edge
- **Do not let egg wash run down the cut edge** — it glues the layers together and prevents lift

### Choux

An unusual method: the paste is **cooked before baking**.

1. Bring water (or milk), fat and salt to the boil
2. Add flour all at once and beat over heat until the paste comes away from the pan and forms a smooth ball — this gelatinises the starch
3. Cool slightly
4. **Beat in eggs gradually** until the paste reaches dropping consistency — it should hold its shape but fall from the spoon reluctantly
5. Pipe and bake in a **hot oven**, then reduce

**Choux is raised entirely by steam.** Common faults:
- **Opening the oven early** collapses them — the structure has not set
- **Too much egg** makes the paste too slack to hold shape
- **Not enough egg** gives poor rise and a dense interior
- **Insufficient drying** after baking leaves the inside soggy — pierce and return to a low oven to dry

## Bread

### The Process

1. **Scaling** — weigh accurately
2. **Mixing** — combine and hydrate
3. **Kneading** — develop the gluten network
4. **Bulk fermentation (first prove)** — yeast produces CO₂; flavour develops
5. **Knock back** — degas, redistribute yeast and even the temperature
6. **Dividing and shaping** — create surface tension in the dough skin
7. **Final proof** — the dough rises to its finished volume
8. **Scoring** — controlled cuts direct the expansion
9. **Baking** — oven spring, then setting and crust formation
10. **Cooling** — **on a rack.** Cooling on a solid surface traps steam and softens the base

### Kneading and Gluten Development

Kneading aligns and strengthens the gluten network so it can trap gas.

**The windowpane test:** stretch a small piece of dough thin. If it stretches to a translucent membrane without tearing, gluten is adequately developed. If it tears immediately, it needs more work.

**Under-kneaded dough** cannot hold gas — poor rise, dense crumb.
**Over-kneaded dough** (rare by hand, common by machine) breaks down, becomes slack and sticky, and loses structure.

### Fermentation

**Yeast activity is temperature-dependent.** Warmer ferments faster; cooler ferments slower and develops more flavour.

- Optimal dough temperature is typically around 24–27°C
- Yeast dies at approximately 55–60°C — **liquid that is too hot will kill it**
- **Retarding** — deliberately fermenting in the refrigerator, slowing yeast activity and developing flavour over many hours

**[VERIFY: Optimal fermentation temperatures and yeast death point against a current baking reference. Figures vary slightly between sources.]**

### Judging the Proof

**The poke test:** press the proved dough gently with a floured finger.

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Springs back immediately | **Under-proved** — needs more time |
| Springs back slowly, leaving a slight indent | **Ready** |
| Does not spring back; indent remains | **Over-proved** |

**Over-proved dough has exhausted its gas-holding capacity.** The gluten is stretched beyond recovery and it will collapse in the oven, giving a flat loaf with a coarse, open, irregular crumb.

### Baking

- **Oven spring** — the rapid initial expansion as gas expands and remaining yeast has a final burst before dying
- **Steam** in the first minutes keeps the crust soft, allowing maximum expansion, and gelatinises the surface starch for a glossy crust
- **Scoring** creates a controlled weak point; without it the loaf bursts unpredictably at its weakest point
- **Internal temperature** indicates doneness more reliably than the hollow-sound test — commonly around 88–96°C for most breads

## Fault Diagnosis

Assessment in this unit almost always includes diagnosing faults.

### Cakes

| Fault | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| **Sunken centre** | Oven too cool; oven opened too early; too much raising agent; too much sugar or liquid; underbaked |
| **Peaked, cracked top** | Oven too hot; too much flour; overmixed |
| **Coarse, open crumb** | Over-creamed; too much raising agent; insufficient mixing of flour |
| **Tough, dense** | Overmixed after flour added; too much flour; too little fat or sugar |
| **Dry** | Overbaked; too much flour; insufficient fat or liquid |
| **Fruit sunk to the bottom** | Batter too slack; fruit wet or too heavy; fruit not floured |
| **Burnt outside, raw inside** | Oven too hot; tin too small for the quantity |

### Pastry

| Fault | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| **Tough, hard** | Overworked; too much water; too little fat; fat too warm |
| **Shrinks in the tin** | Insufficient resting; pastry stretched to fit |
| **Soggy base** | Not blind baked; base not sealed; filling too wet; oven too cool |
| **Puff does not rise** | Fat and dough different consistency; layers compressed by a blunt knife; egg wash on the cut edge; insufficient turns |
| **Fat leaks during baking** | Fat too soft; lamination broken; insufficient chilling |
| **Choux collapses** | Oven opened early; underbaked; too much egg |

### Bread

| Fault | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| **Poor rise, dense** | Yeast dead or old; liquid too hot; under-proved; insufficient kneading; too much salt |
| **Collapsed, flat** | Over-proved; over-hydrated; weak flour |
| **Large irregular holes** | Insufficient knock back; poor shaping |
| **Pale crust** | Oven too cool; insufficient sugar; underbaked |
| **Thick, hard crust** | Overbaked; oven too hot; insufficient steam |
| **Soggy base** | Cooled on a solid surface instead of a rack |
| **Bland** | Salt omitted or insufficient; fermentation too fast |
| **Burst side** | Not scored, or scored inadequately |

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are baking bread for a function. Your standard formula is 100% flour, 64% water, 2% salt, 1.5% dried yeast, produced as 24 rolls from 2kg of flour.*

*(a) You need 42 rolls. Calculate the quantities required.*

*(b) The batch you produced this morning came out flat and wide rather than risen and domed, with a very open, irregular, coarse crumb and a slightly sour, alcoholic smell. The kitchen was unusually warm today. Diagnose the fault and explain how you would prevent it.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

### (a) Scaling the formula

**Step 1 — Flour required.**

24 rolls come from 2000g of flour, so each roll uses 2000 ÷ 24 = 83.33g of flour.

For 42 rolls: 42 × 83.33 = **3500g flour**

*Cross-check:* 42 ÷ 24 = 1.75, and 2000 × 1.75 = 3500g. Consistent.

**Step 2 — Apply baker's percentages.**

With flour at 100% = 3500g, the multiplier is 3500 ÷ 100 = **35**.

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Calculation | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100% | 100 × 35 | **3500g** |
| Water | 64% | 64 × 35 | **2240g** |
| Salt | 2% | 2 × 35 | **70g** |
| Dried yeast | 1.5% | 1.5 × 35 | **52.5g** |
| | **167.5%** | | **5862.5g total dough** |

**Sanity check:** 5862.5g ÷ 42 rolls = **139.6g per roll**, which is a sensible dinner roll scaling weight. If that number had come out at 60g or 300g I would know I had made an error.

I would allow a small margin for dough lost to the bowl and bench, and I would scale each roll on the digital scale rather than dividing by eye — consistent size means consistent bake time and a uniform tray.

---

### (b) Fault diagnosis

**The symptoms:**
- Flat and wide rather than risen and domed
- Very open, irregular, coarse crumb
- Slightly sour, alcoholic smell
- Kitchen unusually warm

**Diagnosis: the dough was over-proved.**

Every symptom points the same way, and the warm kitchen explains the cause.

---

**Why the warm kitchen did it:**

Yeast activity is strongly temperature-dependent. A dough proving in a kitchen several degrees warmer than usual ferments substantially faster — the timings that work on a normal day are simply wrong on a hot one.

**The critical error is proving to a clock rather than to the dough.** If the formula says "prove 45 minutes" and I set a timer and walk away, on a hot day the dough reaches full proof well before 45 minutes and then keeps going.

---

**Why each symptom fits:**

**Flat and wide.** During over-proving, the gluten network is stretched beyond its elastic limit by gas it can no longer contain. It loses the strength to hold the loaf's shape, so the dough spreads outward instead of upward. There is also no gas reserve left for **oven spring** — the yeast has exhausted the available sugars and much of it has died, so nothing lifts the roll in the first minutes of baking.

**Open, irregular, coarse crumb.** As the weakened gluten fails, gas cells coalesce into larger irregular voids rather than remaining fine and even.

**Sour, alcoholic smell.** This is the most direct evidence. Fermentation produces CO₂ **and ethanol**, plus organic acids as it progresses. A noticeably alcoholic, sour aroma means fermentation went well past the intended point.

---

**Why it cannot be recovered:**

**Over-proved dough is not salvageable in the way under-proved dough is.** Under-proving is fixed by waiting. Over-proving has permanently damaged the gluten structure — knocking back and re-proving gives a slightly better shape but the crumb and flavour do not come back, and the yeast has limited food remaining.

---

**Prevention:**

**1. Prove to the dough, not to the clock.**

Use the **poke test**: press the proved dough gently with a floured finger.
- Springs back immediately → under-proved, more time
- Springs back slowly leaving a slight indent → **ready, bake now**
- Does not spring back, indent stays → over-proved

**Recipe timings are a guide calibrated to a particular temperature.** The dough is the actual indicator.

**2. Control dough temperature.**

- Measure the dough temperature after mixing and target the working range for the formula
- **Use cooler water on a hot day.** This is the simplest and most effective correction — water temperature is the main variable you control
- Prove in the coolest suitable place, not next to the ovens
- Consider **retarding in the refrigerator** if the schedule allows. Slower fermentation actually improves flavour, and it makes timing far more predictable in a busy kitchen

**3. Adjust yeast for conditions.**

On consistently warm days, reduce the yeast slightly. Less yeast fermenting at a higher temperature reaches the same point over a more controllable period.

**4. Check on it.**

The practical failure was almost certainly that the dough was set to prove and not looked at until the timer went. On an unusual day, check early and check often.

**5. Stagger production.**

For 42 rolls, proving and baking in batches means one over-proved batch does not lose the entire order — and it lets me adjust timing for the second batch based on what the first one did.

---

**What I would do about today's batch:**

The rolls are edible but not to standard — flat, coarse, and off in flavour. For a function I would not serve them.

I would tell the chef immediately rather than sending them out and hoping, explain the cause, and remake. With the diagnosis clear, the remake is straightforward: cooler water, prove somewhere cooler, and judge by the poke test rather than the clock.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The scaling is correct, worked by two independent routes, and — importantly — sanity-checked against a realistic per-roll weight, which is exactly the discipline that catches decimal errors before 3.5kg of flour is committed. The fault diagnosis is exemplary: the student reads all four symptoms as a coherent pattern rather than addressing them separately, and correctly identifies the alcoholic aroma as the most direct evidence of extended fermentation. The explanation of *why* over-proving produces flatness — gluten stretched past its elastic limit combined with exhausted gas reserve and lost oven spring — demonstrates genuine understanding of the mechanism rather than symptom matching. Correctly identifying that over-proving is irrecoverable while under-proving is not is a distinction candidates frequently get wrong. The prevention section correctly identifies the root behavioural cause as proving to a clock rather than to the dough, and the recommendation to adjust water temperature is the single most practical control available. Suggesting retarding as both a flavour and a scheduling improvement shows thinking beyond the immediate fix. The decision to report and remake rather than serve substandard product to a function is the correct professional call. Strong Competent.
