## What This Unit Is About

Every person who prepares or handles food commercially has a legal obligation to handle it safely. Food-borne illness can range from unpleasant to fatal — vulnerable groups (the elderly, infants, pregnant women, immunocompromised people) face serious risk from food contamination events that would only mildly affect a healthy adult.

SITXFSA006 is not a background unit — it is a foundational safety unit that underpins everything you do in a commercial kitchen. The knowledge and practices in this unit must become automatic. Assessors will observe your food safety practices throughout every practical assessment.

## The Legislative Framework

### Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code

The Food Standards Code is the national framework setting requirements for food safety in Australia. It is administered by Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ). The most relevant standards for food handlers are:

- **Standard 3.2.2 — Food Safety Practices and General Requirements:** Sets out the food safety practices required when handling food commercially
- **Standard 3.2.2A — Food Safety Management Statements:** Requirements for certain businesses to have food safety management systems
- **Standard 3.2.3 — Food Premises and Equipment:** Requirements for the physical environment where food is handled

### State Food Safety Legislation
Each state and territory has its own Food Act (or equivalent) that implements the Food Standards Code and sets out enforcement, licensing, and penalty provisions. These are enforced by local councils and state food safety authorities.

### Food Safety Supervisor Requirement
In most states and territories, food businesses are required to have a nominated **Food Safety Supervisor** — a person who holds a current food safety supervisor certificate. As a Certificate III cook, you may be asked to become the food safety supervisor for your workplace once qualified.

## Food-Borne Illness — Understanding the Risk

### How Food Becomes Unsafe

Food can become unsafe through:

1. **Biological contamination:** Micro-organisms — bacteria, viruses, parasites, moulds
2. **Chemical contamination:** Cleaning agents, pesticides, excessive food additives, allergens (in the wrong dish)
3. **Physical contamination:** Foreign objects — glass, metal shavings, bone fragments, packaging, personal items (hair, jewellery)

### Bacteria and Food Safety

Bacteria are the most common cause of food-borne illness. Key concepts:

**Pathogens:** Disease-causing bacteria. Common food-borne pathogens include:
- *Salmonella* — raw poultry, eggs, raw meat
- *Listeria monocytogenes* — ready-to-eat foods, soft cheeses, deli meats, smoked fish
- *Campylobacter* — raw and undercooked poultry
- *Staphylococcus aureus* — contaminated by food handlers (nose, skin, infected wounds)
- *Escherichia coli (E. coli)* — undercooked beef, contaminated produce
- *Clostridium perfringens* — cooked meats and poultry held at incorrect temperatures

**Conditions for bacterial growth:**
Bacteria need the following to multiply rapidly (the FAT TOM model):

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **F**ood | High-protein, high-moisture foods are highest risk: meat, poultry, seafood, dairy, eggs, cooked rice/pasta |
| **A**cidity | Bacteria thrive in a neutral pH (6–7); highly acidic or alkaline environments inhibit growth |
| **T**emperature | The Danger Zone: 5°C to 60°C is where bacteria multiply most rapidly |
| **T**ime | Bacteria can double in number every 20 minutes in ideal conditions |
| **O**xygen | Most food-borne pathogens require oxygen (aerobic); some (Clostridium botulinum) grow without it (anaerobic) |
| **M**oisture | High water activity (Aw) foods support bacterial growth — fresh meat, dairy, cooked rice |

**Removing or controlling any of these factors interrupts bacterial growth.** Temperature control is the primary tool in commercial kitchens.

## Temperature Control — The Core of Food Safety

### The Temperature Danger Zone
**5°C to 60°C** is the Temperature Danger Zone. Food should spend as little time as possible in this range.

### The 2-Hour / 4-Hour Rule

| Time in Danger Zone | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–2 hours | Food can be used, or refrigerated/reheated for later use |
| 2–4 hours | Food must be used immediately — cannot be returned to refrigeration |
| Over 4 hours | Food must be discarded — do not serve |

**Cumulative time:** The 2/4-hour rule applies to the total accumulated time food has spent in the danger zone — not just the current period. If food was out for 1.5 hours earlier, it has only 0.5 hours remaining before it must be used.

### Critical Temperatures

| Situation | Required Temperature |
|---|---|
| Refrigerated storage | 5°C or below |
| Frozen storage | -15°C or below (ideally -18°C) |
| Cooking (minimum core temperature) | 75°C |
| Hot holding | 60°C or above |
| Cooling (after cooking): Phase 1 | From 60°C to 21°C within 2 hours |
| Cooling (after cooking): Phase 2 | From 21°C to 5°C within a further 4 hours |
| Reheating | 60°C or above as quickly as possible |

**Probe thermometers** are essential in a commercial kitchen. Temperature must be checked in the core (centre/thickest part) of the food, not at the surface.

### Cooling Cooked Food Correctly
Placing a large quantity of hot food directly into the refrigerator is not safe — it raises the temperature inside the fridge and the food itself cools too slowly through the danger zone. Correct cooling methods:
- Divide large volumes into smaller, shallow containers (maximises surface area)
- Place over an ice bath to reduce temperature rapidly
- Use blast chillers where available
- Fan cooling in a clean environment before refrigerating
- Do not seal containers until food is cool (steam inside creates condensation and can raise temperature)

## Cross-Contamination — What It Is and How to Prevent It

Cross-contamination occurs when bacteria or allergens are transferred from one food, surface, or person to another.

### Types of Cross-Contamination

**Direct cross-contamination:** Raw food comes into direct contact with ready-to-eat food
- Example: raw chicken drips onto a salad

**Indirect cross-contamination:** Bacteria or allergens are transferred via a surface, equipment, or hands
- Example: raw chicken cut on a board, then vegetables cut on the same board without cleaning
- Example: allergen residue on a blender used for an allergen-free dish

### Preventing Cross-Contamination

**Colour-coded chopping boards:**
Most commercial kitchens use a colour-coded system to separate cutting surfaces by food type:
- **Red:** Raw meat
- **Yellow:** Raw poultry
- **Blue:** Raw seafood
- **Green:** Fruit and vegetables / salads
- **White:** Bakery / dairy / general
- **Brown:** Cooked meat

**Storage hierarchy in the refrigerator:**
Store food in the correct order from top to bottom based on required cooking temperature:
1. **Top shelves:** Ready-to-eat food, cooked food, dairy
2. **Middle:** Whole cuts of beef and pork (higher cooking temp required)
3. **Below that:** Ground meat and fish (highest risk — minced surfaces)
4. **Bottom:** Raw poultry (highest required cooking temperature — 75°C core)

**This prevents drip contamination** from higher-risk raw foods onto lower-risk or ready-to-eat foods.

## Personal Hygiene for Food Handlers

Food handlers are a significant source of contamination. Requirements:

### Handwashing
Handwashing is the single most effective personal hygiene control.

**When to wash hands:**
- Before handling food
- After handling raw meat, poultry, seafood, or eggs
- After using the toilet
- After touching your face, hair, nose, or mouth
- After handling rubbish or cleaning chemicals
- After handling money
- After handling animals
- After blowing your nose

**Correct handwashing procedure:**
1. Wet hands under warm running water
2. Apply soap and lather thoroughly (back of hands, between fingers, thumbs, wrists, under nails) for at least 20 seconds
3. Rinse thoroughly
4. Dry with a single-use paper towel (cloth towels are a contamination risk)

### Other Personal Hygiene Requirements

- **Illness:** Do not work with food if you have diarrhoea, vomiting, jaundice, or an infected wound that cannot be covered. Report illness to your supervisor. A food handler with a gastroenteric illness who continues to work can contaminate food for many people.
- **Cuts and wounds:** Cover with a brightly coloured (blue) waterproof bandage so it is visible if it falls into food; wear a glove over it
- **Hair:** Keep long hair tied back; wear a hair net in high-risk environments; do not touch your hair while handling food
- **Jewellery:** Remove rings, bracelets, and watches before handling food — bacteria harbour in these items
- **Smoking:** Do not smoke anywhere near food preparation areas
- **PPE:** Wear clean protective clothing (apron, chef's whites); change if soiled

## Allergen Management

Food allergies can be fatal. The **14 declarable allergens** regulated in Australia include:

1. Peanuts
2. Tree nuts (almonds, cashews, hazelnuts, pecans, pistachios, walnuts, etc.)
3. Milk
4. Eggs
5. Sesame seeds
6. Fish
7. Shellfish (crustacea)
8. Soy
9. Wheat/gluten
10. Lupin
11. Royal jelly
12. Bee pollen
13. Sulphites (>10mg/kg)
14. Propolis

**Your obligations as a food handler:**
- Know which allergens are present in each dish you prepare
- Never assume a dish is allergen-free — check the recipe and the ingredients
- When a customer or resident discloses a food allergy, escalate to the chef in charge
- Prevent cross-contact: separate equipment, utensils, and preparation areas for allergen-free dishes; do not serve an allergen-containing garnish on an allergen-free dish

**Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency.** If a customer shows signs of a severe allergic reaction (difficulty breathing, swelling of throat/tongue, loss of consciousness), call 000 immediately.

## HACCP in the Commercial Kitchen

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is the framework for systematic food safety management. Understanding it allows you to participate effectively in your workplace's food safety system.

**The seven HACCP principles:**
1. Conduct a hazard analysis — identify all biological, chemical, and physical hazards
2. Identify Critical Control Points (CCPs) — the steps where control is essential
3. Establish critical limits — the measurable limits (e.g., cooking to 75°C) at each CCP
4. Establish monitoring procedures — how and how often you will check each CCP
5. Establish corrective actions — what to do when monitoring shows a limit is breached
6. Establish verification procedures — confirming the system is working
7. Establish record-keeping — documenting monitoring results

**Common CCPs in a commercial kitchen:**
- Receiving (checking temperature of delivered food)
- Cold storage (monitoring refrigerator temperatures)
- Thawing (controlled thawing in the refrigerator, not at room temperature)
- Cooking (checking core temperature reaches 75°C)
- Cooling (checking food cools within the required timeframe)
- Hot holding (checking hot held food stays at 60°C+)
- Reheating (checking reheated food reaches 60°C+)

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*A batch of chicken soup was prepared yesterday afternoon. It was left on the stove to cool overnight. This morning, the chef wants to reheat it for today's lunch service. A food handler checks the temperature and finds the soup is currently at 18°C. Is it safe to reheat and serve? Explain your answer with reference to food safety principles.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

The soup is not safe to reheat and serve. It should be discarded.

**Reasoning:**

The soup was cooked yesterday afternoon and left to cool overnight on the stove. This means it spent an extended and uncontrolled period in the Temperature Danger Zone (5°C–60°C) while cooling.

The current temperature is 18°C — well within the danger zone. However, the problem is not the current temperature; it is the time the soup has already spent in the danger zone overnight.

**Applying the 2/4-hour rule:**
If the soup was placed on the stove (above 60°C) in the afternoon and cooled to room temperature overnight, it spent many hours in the danger zone. Even conservatively, the time in the danger zone far exceeds the 4-hour maximum after which food must be discarded.

**Reheating does not make it safe:**
While reheating to 75°C or above will kill many bacteria, it will not destroy bacterial **toxins** — waste products produced by bacteria during their growth period. Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus, both common in cooked protein foods and grains, produce heat-stable toxins that survive cooking temperatures. A soup that has had bacterial growth over many hours may contain toxins that reheating cannot eliminate.

**Decision:** Discard the soup. Inform the chef. Do not serve food that has spent more than 4 cumulative hours in the Temperature Danger Zone.

**Correct procedure for tomorrow:** After cooking, the soup should be cooled rapidly — divided into small shallow containers, placed over an ice bath to reduce to 21°C within 2 hours, then refrigerated to reach 5°C within a further 4 hours, and stored at 5°C or below until service.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly applies the 2/4-hour rule, correctly identifies that reheating does not eliminate heat-stable toxins, discards the food without equivocation, and explains the correct procedure for future batches. The student does not confuse "it looks and smells fine" with "it is safe." Strong Competent.
