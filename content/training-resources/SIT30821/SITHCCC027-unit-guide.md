## What This Unit Is About

The methods of cookery are the fundamental vocabulary of the professional kitchen. Understanding what each method does to food — how it transfers heat, what effect it has on texture, moisture, and flavour — allows a cook to select the right method for any ingredient and to understand why something works or does not work.

SITHCCC027 requires you to demonstrate that you can apply the basic methods of cookery correctly, select the appropriate method for given ingredients and dishes, and produce food that meets commercial quality standards.

## The Basic Methods of Cookery

### 1. Boiling
**Definition:** Cooking food submerged in liquid (typically water or stock) at 100°C at sea level.

**Heat transfer:** Conduction (from liquid to food).

**Used for:** Vegetables, pasta, legumes, eggs, grains, certain proteins (whole chicken for stock, corned beef).

**Key principles:**
- Bring liquid to a rolling boil before adding food where a rapid start is needed (pasta, green vegetables)
- Green vegetables benefit from salted boiling water and immediate cooling in ice water (refreshing) to preserve colour and stop cooking
- Do not boil proteins violently — proteins toughen; simmer instead for most protein applications

---

### 2. Simmering
**Definition:** Cooking food in liquid at a temperature just below boiling (approximately 85–95°C) — bubbles rise gently rather than breaking the surface vigorously.

**Used for:** Stocks, soups, braised meats, legumes, risotto.

**Key principles:**
- Simmering extracts flavour gently over time without clouding stock or toughening proteins
- Most stocks should never boil — boiling emulsifies fat and proteins into the liquid, producing a cloudy, greasy result

---

### 3. Poaching
**Definition:** Cooking food in liquid at a very gentle temperature (70–85°C) — no visible bubbling.

**Used for:** Eggs, delicate fish, poultry, fruit.

**Key principles:**
- The gentle heat preserves the structure and moisture of delicate proteins
- Poaching liquid can be flavoured (court bouillon for fish) or plain
- Shallow poaching (food partially submerged, covered) vs. deep poaching (fully submerged)

---

### 4. Steaming
**Definition:** Cooking food using steam (100°C or above) without the food being in direct contact with water.

**Used for:** Vegetables, fish, dumplings, puddings, rice (various methods).

**Key principles:**
- Steaming preserves more water-soluble nutrients (vitamins B and C) than boiling because the food is not immersed in water
- Steaming preserves colour and texture well
- Pressure steaming (higher temperatures) is used in commercial operations for speed

---

### 5. Blanching
**Definition:** Briefly immersing food in boiling water, then immediately plunging into ice water to stop cooking.

**Used for:** Partially cooking vegetables before finishing by another method; loosening skins (tomatoes, peaches); preserving colour before freezing; inactivating enzymes that cause browning or deterioration.

**Key principles:**
- Blanching time varies by vegetable — too long and it begins to cook the vegetable; too short and enzymes are not deactivated
- The ice bath is essential — it stops cooking immediately and sets the colour

---

### 6. Frying (Deep Frying)
**Definition:** Cooking food submerged in hot oil (typically 160–190°C depending on the product).

**Used for:** Chips/fries, battered fish, crumbed meats, doughnuts, spring rolls.

**Heat transfer:** Conduction (oil to food surface) creating a crust; steam forms inside and cooks the interior.

**Key principles:**
- Oil temperature is critical — too cool and food absorbs oil and is greasy; too hot and exterior burns before interior is cooked
- Dry food before frying — water in the fryer causes violent spitting
- Do not overcrowd the fryer — this drops oil temperature and causes uneven cooking
- Use a probe thermometer to verify internal temperature of products like chicken (75°C core)
- Food safety: used oil degrades and should be filtered and changed according to the establishment's procedures; burned or rancid oil produces toxic compounds

---

### 7. Shallow Frying and Pan Frying
**Definition:** Cooking food in a small amount of oil in a pan (food not submerged).

**Used for:** Fillets of fish, chicken breast, escalopes, eggs, vegetables.

**Key principles:**
- The presentation side goes down first — the first side in the pan receives direct heat and forms the crust; the second side is less developed
- Allow the pan and oil to reach temperature before adding food — otherwise food sticks and the crust does not develop
- Do not move food immediately after placing — let it release naturally as the crust forms

---

### 8. Sautéing
**Definition:** Cooking food quickly in a small amount of fat over high heat, with continuous movement (from the French *sauter* — to jump).

**Used for:** Vegetables, sliced proteins, mushrooms, aromatics (onion, garlic).

**Key principles:**
- High heat and small pieces — sautéing is a quick technique; if pieces are too large they will not cook through before the outside burns
- The pan must be hot before adding food
- Movement prevents burning and promotes even cooking

---

### 9. Stir-Frying
**Definition:** A high-heat technique similar to sautéing, using a wok or heavy pan, with continuous stirring.

**Used for:** Asian cuisine preparations; vegetables and proteins cut into small uniform pieces.

**Key principles:**
- Very high heat (ideally a wok burner) is essential to achieve the characteristic smoky flavour (*wok hei*)
- All ingredients must be prepared and ready before cooking begins — stir-frying is too fast to pause
- Proteins are cooked first and removed; vegetables added by cooking time (longest first); protein returned at end

---

### 10. Grilling
**Definition:** Cooking food on a grill (bars, grate) with radiant heat from below (gas or charcoal).

**Used for:** Steaks, chops, fish fillets, vegetables, burgers.

**Heat transfer:** Radiation (from heat source) and conduction (from grill bars).

**Key principles:**
- Preheat the grill thoroughly — a cold grill causes food to stick and does not create grill marks
- Oil the food, not the grill (oiling a very hot grill burns immediately)
- Grill marks: place food at a 45° angle to the bars; rotate 90° for a crosshatch pattern
- Use the touch test or probe thermometer to assess doneness — pressing the meat and comparing to the feel of the base of your thumb (relaxed = raw; pinched A = medium; tense = well done)

---

### 11. Griddling / Char-grilling
**Definition:** Similar to grilling but using a solid flat plate (griddle) or ribbed cast iron plate rather than bars.

**Used for:** Steaks, burgers, pancakes, sandwiches.

---

### 12. Roasting
**Definition:** Cooking food uncovered in dry heat (oven) using hot air convection.

**Used for:** Large joints of meat, whole poultry, root vegetables.

**Heat transfer:** Convection (hot air around food) and radiation (from oven walls and floor).

**Key principles:**
- Basting adds moisture and flavour to the exterior
- Resting after roasting is essential — it allows the juices to redistribute throughout the meat rather than running out when cut
- Probe thermometer use is essential for roasting — surface colour alone does not indicate doneness

---

### 13. Baking
**Definition:** Cooking food in an oven using dry heat. Typically applied to bread, pastry, and cakes (where the structure change is central to the product).

**Used for:** Bread, cakes, pastries, biscuits, quiches, pasta bakes.

**Key principles:**
- Oven temperature accuracy is critical — baking is chemistry; small temperature variations produce different results
- Do not open the oven door during baking of raised products (soufflés, bread, cakes) — the temperature drop can cause collapse
- Convection (fan) ovens cook faster than conventional ovens — recipes may need time or temperature adjustment

---

### 14. Braising
**Definition:** A slow, moist cooking method in which food (usually a tougher cut of meat or vegetables) is browned first and then cooked slowly in a small amount of liquid, covered.

**Used for:** Tougher cuts (lamb shoulder, beef brisket, oxtail), root vegetables, poultry legs.

**Key principles:**
- Browning first (Maillard reaction) develops flavour in the crust and the sauce
- Low and slow — braising temperatures typically 140–165°C oven; the long time converts collagen in tough cuts to gelatin, which gives the sauce body and makes the meat tender
- Food should be half-submerged — it is not poached; the top is exposed to steam
- Braised dishes improve overnight — flavours develop as the dish rests

---

### 15. Stewing
**Definition:** Similar to braising but food is cut smaller and fully submerged in liquid throughout cooking.

**Used for:** Stews, casseroles, tagines, curries.

**Key principles:**
- Similar principles to braising — low, slow cooking breaks down connective tissue
- The cooking liquid becomes the sauce — it should be flavourful

---

## Selecting the Correct Method

The method of cookery should be matched to:

| Factor | Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Cut / portion size** | Tender cuts (fillet, loin) → fast high-heat methods (grill, pan-fry). Tough cuts (shoulder, shank) → slow moist methods (braise, stew) |
| **Moisture content** | Lean proteins benefit from moist methods or careful temperature control; fatty cuts can withstand higher heat |
| **End result required** | Crisp exterior: dry heat. Tender interior: slow moist. Preserved colour/nutrients: steaming |
| **The dish being made** | The method is usually specified by the dish — a bouillabaisse requires simmering; a béarnaise requires emulsification |
| **Service timing** | Some methods lend themselves to batch cooking and holding; others (soufflés, eggs) must be made to order |

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are asked to prepare osso bucco using braised veal shanks. The recipe requires the shanks to be browned first and then cooked slowly in a tomato and white wine braising liquid for 2–3 hours. Explain why braising is the appropriate method for this dish, what happens to the meat during the cooking process, and what would be lost if you cooked the shanks quickly at high heat instead.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

**Why braising is appropriate:**

Veal shanks (osso bucco) are a working muscle — they are used for movement, which means they contain significant amounts of connective tissue (collagen) and are tough if cooked quickly. Braising is ideal for tough cuts because the long, slow, moist heat converts the collagen into gelatin. This gelatin lubricates the muscle fibres, making the meat tender and producing a rich, silky sauce. The marrow in the bone also melts into the braising liquid, adding further richness.

**What happens during braising:**

1. **Browning (Maillard reaction):** The initial high-heat searing of the shanks before adding liquid creates hundreds of new flavour compounds on the surface of the meat and in the pan. The fond (the browned residue on the pan bottom) is deglazed with wine and becomes part of the braising liquid — this is where much of the dish's depth of flavour originates.

2. **Collagen breakdown:** At sustained temperatures above approximately 70°C, collagen begins to break down into gelatin. In a 2–3 hour braise at around 160°C (oven temperature producing a liquid temperature of approximately 85–95°C), virtually all the collagen in the shank converts. The result is meat that falls away from the bone.

3. **Flavour exchange:** During the extended cooking, flavour compounds move between the meat and the braising liquid in both directions — the meat absorbs aromatics from the liquid; the liquid absorbs flavour from the meat. This is why braised dishes often taste better the next day — resting allows further equilibration of flavours.

**If you cooked the shanks quickly at high heat:**

A veal shank cooked quickly (grilled or pan-fried at high heat for 15–20 minutes) would:
- Have a toughened, chewy exterior from protein contraction
- Have a still-tough, undercooked interior — the muscle fibres would not have had time to soften and the collagen would not have converted to gelatin
- Lack the rich, integrated flavour that develops over long braising
- Essentially be inedible — this is the wrong cut for high-heat fast cooking

**Examiner Annotation:** Excellent. The student correctly explains why the cut requires braising (connective tissue/collagen), correctly describes the Maillard reaction and its culinary importance, explains collagen-to-gelatin conversion accurately, and clearly explains why a high-heat method would produce an inferior result. This level of understanding — not just following the recipe but understanding why — is what distinguishes a chef from a technician. Strong Competent.
