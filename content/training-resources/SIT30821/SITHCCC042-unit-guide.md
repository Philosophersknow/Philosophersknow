## What This Unit Is About

This is the unit where a mistake can kill someone.

Most cookery units assess whether food is good. This one assesses whether food is **safe for a specific person**. A chef who plates a dish containing peanut for a customer with a peanut allergy has not made a quality error — they have caused a potentially fatal medical emergency.

SITHCCC042 asks you to understand why people have dietary requirements, identify what a request actually means, prepare food that genuinely meets it, and communicate accurately about what is in a dish.

---

## Categories of Dietary Requirement

Understanding **why** someone has a requirement determines how strictly it must be applied.

| Category | Driver | Consequence of error |
|---|---|---|
| **Allergy** | Immune response | **Potentially fatal** — anaphylaxis |
| **Intolerance** | Digestive/metabolic | Illness, pain, distress — not usually life-threatening |
| **Coeliac disease** | Autoimmune | Intestinal damage; cumulative harm even without immediate symptoms |
| **Medical / therapeutic** | Managing a condition | Can be serious — e.g. renal, diabetic, low-sodium diets |
| **Religious / cultural** | Belief and observance | Serious personal and cultural harm; a profound breach of trust |
| **Ethical** | Values (vegan, vegetarian) | Distress and breach of trust |
| **Lifestyle / preference** | Choice | Dissatisfaction |

**The professional position:** treat every stated requirement as genuine and important. It is not your job to assess whether someone's requirement is "real enough". A customer who says they cannot eat something is telling you what to do.

**But you must distinguish allergy from preference in one respect** — the *handling* required. A vegetarian dish accidentally cooked in the same pan as meat is a serious service failure. A "gluten-free" dish contaminated with wheat can cause genuine intestinal damage to a coeliac. An allergen-contaminated dish can kill. The kitchen response must scale accordingly, which is why you must **ask**.

## Allergy vs Intolerance — The Critical Distinction

### Food Allergy
An **immune system response**. The body identifies a food protein as a threat and mounts an immune reaction.

- Reaction can occur to **trace amounts** — micrograms
- Onset is typically rapid — minutes to two hours
- Can progress to **anaphylaxis**, which is life-threatening
- There is **no safe small amount**

### Food Intolerance
A **digestive or metabolic** response — the body cannot properly process a food component.

- Generally **dose-dependent** — a small amount may be tolerated
- Onset is usually slower
- Causes discomfort, pain, and illness, but is not typically life-threatening
- Example: lactose intolerance — insufficient lactase enzyme

### Coeliac Disease — Neither, and Frequently Misunderstood
Coeliac disease is an **autoimmune condition**, not an allergy or an intolerance.

- Gluten triggers the immune system to damage the small intestine lining
- **Damage occurs whether or not the person feels symptoms** — many coeliacs have no immediate reaction but sustain intestinal damage regardless
- There is **no safe amount** — trace contamination causes harm
- It is not a preference or a fad, and "a little bit won't hurt" is factually wrong

**Practical implication:** coeliac requires the same rigour of contamination control as an allergy, even though it will not cause anaphylaxis.

## The Declarable Allergens

Australian food law requires declaration of specified allergens. The current list:

1. **Peanuts**
2. **Tree nuts** — almond, Brazil, cashew, hazelnut, macadamia, pecan, pine nut, pistachio, walnut
3. **Milk**
4. **Eggs**
5. **Sesame**
6. **Fish**
7. **Crustacea** — prawn, crab, lobster
8. **Molluscs** — oyster, mussel, squid, octopus
9. **Soy**
10. **Cereals containing gluten** — wheat, rye, barley, oats, spelt
11. **Lupin**
12. **Sulphites** (added, at 10 mg/kg or more)

**[VERIFY: The declarable allergen list and labelling requirements are set by the Food Standards Code and have been amended — notably the separation of molluscs from crustacea and changes to declaration format under Plain English Allergen Labelling. Confirm the current requirements with FSANZ.]**

**Note that peanuts and tree nuts are separate categories.** A person allergic to peanuts (a legume) is not necessarily allergic to tree nuts, and vice versa — but many are allergic to both. **Always ask which.**

### Hidden Allergen Sources

This is where errors happen. Allergens appear in products where they are not expected:

| Allergen | Hidden in |
|---|---|
| **Milk** | Some breads, margarine, "non-dairy" creamers, processed meats, some stocks, caramel, some crisps |
| **Egg** | Mayonnaise, some pasta, glazes, some ice creams, mousses, some batters, meringue-based items |
| **Soy** | Soy sauce, many Asian sauces, vegetable oils, emulsifiers (lecithin), many processed foods, some breads |
| **Wheat/gluten** | Soy sauce, thickeners, stock cubes, some sausages, some processed meats, malt vinegar, some icing sugar mixtures, dusting on chips |
| **Fish** | Worcestershire sauce (anchovy), Caesar dressing, some Asian sauces, XO sauce |
| **Crustacea** | Some Asian pastes, some stocks, fish sauce blends |
| **Sesame** | Tahini, hummus, some breads, dukkah, some oils, burger buns |
| **Nuts** | Pesto (pine nut), some curry pastes, praline, some breads, some oils, marzipan |
| **Sulphites** | Dried fruit, wine, some vinegars, processed potato products, some seafood |

**The rule: read the label, every time, on every product, including ones you have used before.** Manufacturers reformulate without notice.

## Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a **severe, rapid, life-threatening allergic reaction**.

### Signs — Mild to Moderate
- Swelling of lips, face, eyes
- Hives or welts
- Tingling mouth
- Abdominal pain, vomiting

### Signs — Anaphylaxis (Severe)
- **Difficult or noisy breathing**
- **Swelling of the tongue**
- **Swelling or tightness in the throat**
- **Difficulty talking or hoarse voice**
- **Wheeze or persistent cough**
- **Persistent dizziness or collapse**
- **Pale and floppy** (young children)

### Response
1. **Lay the person flat** — do not allow them to stand or walk. **Standing up can cause fatal cardiac collapse in anaphylaxis.** If breathing is difficult, allow them to sit, but do not stand them up
2. **Administer adrenaline autoinjector** (EpiPen / Anapen) into the outer mid-thigh, if available
3. **Call 000** — always, even if the person improves after adrenaline
4. **Note the time** adrenaline was given
5. A **further dose** may be given after 5 minutes if there is no improvement
6. Stay with the person
7. **Retain and secure the food** — it may be needed for investigation

**[VERIFY: Anaphylaxis first aid procedure against current ASCIA (Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy) Action Plans. ASCIA is the authoritative Australian source and their guidance should be followed directly.]**

**Every commercial kitchen should know** where the first aid kit is, whether any staff are trained in anaphylaxis response, and what the venue's emergency procedure is.

## Preventing Cross-Contact

**Cross-contact** (the allergen equivalent of cross-contamination) is the transfer of an allergen from one food to another. It is the leading cause of allergic reactions in food service.

### Where It Happens

| Vector | Example |
|---|---|
| **Shared equipment** | Same tongs used for a regular and a gluten-free dish |
| **Shared oil** | Fried gluten-free chips in the same fryer as crumbed products — **the oil carries gluten** |
| **Shared surfaces** | Preparing on a bench where flour was used |
| **Shared utensils** | The same spoon in multiple containers |
| **Airborne flour** | Flour dust settles across a section |
| **Hands and gloves** | Handling bread then plating an allergen-free dish |
| **Garnish** | Sesame seeds on a bun; a nut-based crumb on the plate |
| **Shared serving spoons** | Buffet service — a spoon moved between dishes |
| **Splash and spill** | Sauce splashing between plates on the pass |
| **Cloths and aprons** | Wiping hands on a cloth used for something else |

### Controls

**Before preparation:**
- **Clean and sanitise** the work area, boards, and equipment. **Cleaning removes allergen; sanitiser alone does not.** Allergens are proteins, not microbes — you must physically remove them
- Use **dedicated equipment** where available — many kitchens use purple-coded boards, tongs, and utensils for allergen-free preparation
- **Wash hands** and change gloves
- Change apron if contaminated
- Use **fresh ingredients from sealed containers**, not from open bulk containers that may have been cross-contaminated by shared scoops

**During preparation:**
- Prepare allergen-free dishes **first**, before the section is contaminated, where service allows
- Use a **separate area** away from flour and airborne allergens
- **Dedicated fryer** for gluten-free — never share
- Cover the dish
- **Never remove an allergen from a finished dish.** Picking nuts off a salad does not make it safe — the oils and proteins have transferred

**During service:**
- **Communicate clearly** — the allergen order must be flagged verbally and on the docket
- Use a **visible marker** — many kitchens use a coloured flag, ticket, or plate cover
- **Hand the plate directly** to the server with a verbal statement of what it is
- The server must **deliver it directly to the correct person** — not place it on the table for guests to distribute

## Common Dietary Requirements in Practice

### Gluten-Free
**Avoid:** wheat, rye, barley, oats (unless certified gluten-free), spelt, triticale, and anything derived from them — including many soy sauces, stock powders, thickeners, malt vinegar, and beer.

**Use:** rice, corn, quinoa, buckwheat (despite the name, it is not wheat), potato, gluten-free flours, tamari instead of soy sauce, cornflour instead of wheat flour as a thickener.

**Watch:** flour dusting, shared fryers, shared toasters, shared pasta water, thickened sauces.

### Dairy-Free / Lactose-Free
**These are different requirements.**
- **Dairy-free** — no milk protein at all (allergy or vegan)
- **Lactose-free** — no lactose sugar; hard cheeses and butter may be tolerated by some

**Alternatives:** oat, soy, almond, rice, coconut milk. **Note that nut milks are unsuitable for nut allergies** — a common error when substituting.

### Vegetarian and Vegan

| | Vegetarian | Vegan |
|---|---|---|
| Meat, poultry, fish | No | No |
| Dairy | Yes | No |
| Eggs | Yes | No |
| Honey | Usually | No |
| Gelatine | No | No |
| Rennet (animal) | No | No |
| Fish sauce / anchovy | No | No |
| Worcestershire sauce | Check — contains anchovy | No |

**Frequently missed animal products:** gelatine (in some desserts, marshmallow, some yoghurts), animal rennet in cheese, fish sauce, anchovy in Worcestershire and Caesar dressing, lard or beef tallow in pastry and frying, isinglass in some wines and beers, carmine/cochineal colouring, chicken stock in "vegetable" soup.

**"Vegetarian" stock is a common failure point** — check that the stock actually is.

### Religious Requirements

| Requirement | Key Restrictions |
|---|---|
| **Halal** | No pork or pork products; no alcohol; meat must be slaughtered according to Islamic requirements and certified; no cross-contact with non-halal |
| **Kosher** | No pork or shellfish; **meat and dairy must not be combined** or share equipment; meat must be certified kosher; requires certified kitchen practice for strict observance |
| **Hindu** | Many are vegetarian; **beef is prohibited** for most |
| **Buddhist** | Many vegetarian; some avoid pungent vegetables (onion, garlic) |
| **Jain** | Strict vegetarian; **no root vegetables** (potato, onion, garlic, carrot) as harvesting kills the plant |
| **Seventh-day Adventist** | Often vegetarian; many avoid alcohol and caffeine |

**Important honesty point:** a standard commercial kitchen generally **cannot** provide genuinely halal or kosher food to a strict observance standard, because that requires certified ingredients and certified separation of equipment. **What you can do is provide a dish free of prohibited ingredients and prepared with separated equipment — and say exactly that.** Claiming a dish is "halal" when the kitchen is not certified is dishonest and a serious breach of trust.

### Medical and Therapeutic Diets

| Diet | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Diabetic** | Managed carbohydrate; consistent timing; not simply "sugar-free" |
| **Low sodium** | Cardiac, renal, hypertension — no added salt, careful with stocks, sauces, and processed items |
| **Renal** | Restricted potassium, phosphate, protein, and fluid — genuinely complex, requires dietitian guidance |
| **Low FODMAP** | IBS management — restricts specific carbohydrates including onion and garlic |
| **Texture-modified** | Dysphagia — see below |

### Texture-Modified Diets and Dysphagia

People with **dysphagia** (swallowing difficulty) require food and drink modified in texture to prevent choking and aspiration. This is common in aged care and health settings — a significant employment area for Certificate III cooks.

The **IDDSI framework** (International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative) defines standardised levels from 0 (thin liquid) to 7 (regular), with defined testing methods for each.

**This is a genuine safety issue.** Serving food at the wrong texture level to a person with dysphagia can cause choking or aspiration pneumonia. Where you work in a setting using IDDSI, the levels must be followed exactly and tested using the prescribed methods.

## Communication — The Most Important Skill

### Taking the Order

When a dietary requirement is raised, **ask specifically**:

1. **"Is that an allergy, an intolerance, or a preference?"** — this determines the handling protocol
2. **"Which specific foods?"** — "nuts" could mean peanuts, tree nuts, or both
3. **"How severe is it?"** — trace amounts, or dose-dependent?
4. **"Is there anything else?"** — people often mention one and forget another

**Never guess. Never assume. Never say "it should be fine."**

### The Golden Rule of Honesty

**If you cannot guarantee a dish is free of an allergen, say so.**

The correct response is: *"I can't guarantee this kitchen is free of traces of peanut — we handle it in this kitchen and I can't rule out cross-contact. I can prepare something with no peanut ingredients on clean equipment, but I can't promise trace-free. Would you like me to do that, or would you prefer to choose something else?"*

**That is a professional answer.** It is honest, it gives the customer the information they need to make their own decision, and it does not expose them to a risk they have not consented to.

**"It should be fine"** is the most dangerous sentence in food service.

### Escalation

If you are unsure, **stop and ask the chef in charge**. Never guess on an allergen question. There is no situation in which guessing is better than asking.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are working the entrée section during a busy Saturday service. A docket comes through marked "TABLE 12 — 1 x Thai beef salad — NUT ALLERGY".*

*The Thai beef salad on your menu contains crushed peanuts as a garnish and the dressing is made with a house nam jim that contains peanut oil. Both are already made up in bulk for service.*

*The server is waiting. What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I stop, and I do not attempt to modify this dish. Then I get the information I actually need before anything goes near a plate.

---

**Why I cannot simply "leave the nuts off":**

The docket says nut allergy, and this dish has peanut in **two** places — the visible garnish and the dressing.

**Removing the garnish does not make it safe.** Even if I made this salad with no crushed peanut on top, the nam jim dressing contains peanut oil and would still be in the dish. Peanut protein can be present in peanut oil depending on how it is refined, and I have no way of verifying that in the middle of service.

And more fundamentally: **you never make an allergen dish safe by taking the allergen off.** The proteins and oils have already transferred through the bulk container, the ladle, and the mixing bowl.

---

**What I do — in order:**

**1. Flag it immediately and stop the dish.**

I tell the server: *"Hold on — that dish has peanut in the garnish and in the dressing. I can't make it safe. I need to know exactly what the allergy is before we go any further."*

**2. Get the specific information.**

I need the server to go back to the table and ask:

- **Peanut, tree nut, or both?** Peanuts are legumes; tree nuts are a separate category. Many people are allergic to one and not the other, and many to both. The answer changes what I can offer
- **Is it an allergy or an intolerance?** The docket says allergy, but I want it confirmed, because the handling protocol is different
- **How severe — does the customer react to traces?** This determines whether I can offer anything at all from a kitchen that handles peanut

**3. Be honest about what this kitchen can guarantee.**

This is the part that matters most. We use peanuts in this section, in bulk, during service. There is peanut dust and residue in this environment.

The honest message back to the table is:

*"We handle peanuts in this kitchen, so I can't guarantee any dish is completely trace-free. What I can do is prepare a dish with no peanut ingredients, on cleaned equipment, with fresh utensils. If the customer reacts to traces, they need to know that before they decide."*

**The customer makes that decision, not me.** My job is to give them accurate information. Saying "yeah I'll do it without the nuts, should be fine" would be taking a decision about someone else's safety without their knowledge or consent, on incomplete information, in the middle of a busy service.

**4. Offer a genuine alternative.**

Rather than trying to rescue a dish that is fundamentally built on peanut, I would look at what else on the menu is naturally free of nuts and can be prepared cleanly — and offer that. It is faster, safer, and usually better food than a stripped-back version of something that has had its defining components removed.

**5. If we proceed — the preparation protocol.**

Assuming the customer confirms it is peanut only, is not trace-sensitive, and wants a dish prepared:

- **Clean and sanitise** the bench, board, and equipment — cleaning first, because allergen removal is physical, not antimicrobial. Sanitiser does not destroy peanut protein
- **Wash hands, change gloves, change apron**
- **Fresh utensils and a clean board** — dedicated allergen-free equipment if we have it
- **Fresh ingredients from sealed containers**, not from the open bulk containers on the section that have had shared scoops in them
- **Prepare in a separate area**, away from where peanut is being handled
- **A completely different dressing** — made fresh, not the nam jim, not a modified version of it
- **Cover the plate** once assembled
- **Mark it clearly** and hand it directly to the server with a verbal statement: *"This is the allergy plate for table 12, seat 3."*
- The server **delivers it directly to that person** — not onto the table for guests to pass around

**6. If I am unsure at any point, I ask the chef in charge.**

There is no version of this where guessing is acceptable, and there is no service so busy that it justifies it.

---

**The thing I would hold onto:**

The pressure in this scenario is real — Saturday service, a server waiting, a docket in the rail, and the fastest thing to do is scrape the peanuts off and send it. That is exactly the pressure that kills people.

A delayed entrée is an inconvenience. Anaphylaxis at table 12 is an ambulance, a possible death, and the end of the venue. **The two are not comparable, and the decision is not close.**

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student immediately identifies that the allergen is present in two places and that removal is not an option — correctly applying the principle that an allergen cannot be taken out of a finished dish. The questions asked are exactly the right ones, and the peanut/tree nut distinction is correctly explained rather than merely stated. The strongest element is the honesty about kitchen limitations: the student recognises that a kitchen handling peanut in bulk cannot promise trace-free, states this plainly, and — critically — frames the decision as the customer's to make with accurate information rather than the chef's to make on their behalf. That is the correct ethical and professional position and it is the point of this unit. The preparation protocol is thorough and correctly sequenced, including the distinction between cleaning and sanitising for allergen removal, use of sealed rather than bulk ingredients, and direct hand-to-server-to-person delivery. The suggestion to offer a naturally suitable alternative rather than a stripped-down version shows both safety judgement and respect for the customer's experience. The closing statement on relative consequences is the correct professional framing. Strong Competent.
