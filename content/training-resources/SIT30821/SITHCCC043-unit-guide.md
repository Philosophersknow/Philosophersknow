## What This Unit Is About

Every other unit in this qualification teaches you to produce food. This one teaches you to produce it **on time, at volume, to consistent standard, alongside other people, without falling apart** — which is what a commercial kitchen actually requires.

SITHCCC043 is frequently the capstone unit. It is assessed over multiple complete services, and it is where students who can cook well in isolation discover that cooking well under service conditions is a different skill.

## Kitchen Structure — The Brigade

The classical brigade system, adapted to modern operations, defines who does what.

| Role | French | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive Chef / Head Chef** | Chef de cuisine | Overall kitchen leadership, menu, costing, staffing |
| **Second in charge** | Sous chef | Runs service, supervises sections, deputises |
| **Section chef** | Chef de partie (CDP) | Runs a specific section |
| **Assistant section chef** | Demi chef | Assists a CDP |
| **Junior cook** | Commis chef | Works under a CDP; entry qualified level |
| **Apprentice** | — | Learning across sections |
| **Kitchen hand** | — | Cleaning, dishwashing, basic prep |

**Common sections:**

| Section | French | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Sauces** | Saucier | Sauces, sautéed items, often the senior section |
| **Fish** | Poissonnier | Seafood |
| **Roast** | Rôtisseur | Roasted and grilled meats |
| **Vegetables** | Entremetier | Vegetables, soups, eggs, starches |
| **Pantry / cold** | Garde manger | Cold dishes, salads, charcuterie |
| **Pastry** | Pâtissier | Desserts, breads |
| **Relief** | Tournant | Floats between sections |

**Why the hierarchy matters practically:** it establishes who you take direction from, who you escalate to, and who speaks for the kitchen. In service, a clear chain of command is a safety and speed mechanism, not ceremony.

## Mise en Place

*Mise en place* — "everything in its place" — is the preparation completed before service so that during service you assemble rather than prepare.

**Good mise en place is the difference between a cook who copes with service and one who does not.** Almost every service failure traces back to inadequate prep.

### What It Includes

- All components prepped, portioned, and within reach
- Sauces made and held at correct temperature
- Garnishes ready
- Proteins portioned and stored correctly
- Station set up in a consistent, repeatable layout
- Equipment on and at temperature
- Clean cloths, spare containers, sanitiser to hand
- Bin within reach

### Planning Mise en Place

**Working backwards from service:**

1. **Read the menu and the section's dishes.** What components does each require?
2. **List every component** across all dishes on your section
3. **Identify shared components** — one batch of a sauce may serve three dishes
4. **Sequence by duration** — start the longest items first. A stock that needs four hours cannot be started at hour three
5. **Sequence by dependency** — you cannot make the velouté before the stock
6. **Identify what must be fresh** and prepare it late
7. **Estimate quantities** from covers booked, historical sales mix, and par levels
8. **Allow for contingency** — running out mid-service is worse than a small surplus

### Estimating Quantities

**Par levels** — a standard quantity to have on hand for a given service. Established from historical data and adjusted for bookings, day of week, weather, and events.

**Sales mix** — the historical proportion of each dish sold. If the fish is typically 20% of mains and there are 100 covers booked, prep for approximately 20 portions plus contingency.

**Under-prepping** means running out and disappointing customers. **Over-prepping** means waste and cost. Both are failures; the skill is calibration, and it improves with attention rather than time.

## Working Through Service

### The Docket System

Orders arrive as dockets (printed or on a screen). The **pass** is where dishes are assembled, checked, and released to service.

**The expeditor** (often the head chef or sous chef) calls the orders, coordinates timing across sections, and checks every plate before it leaves.

**Standard calls:**

| Call | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **"Check on"** / "Ordering" | New order — start it |
| **"Away"** | Send it now |
| **"Two minutes"** | Time check — how long |
| **"On the fly"** | Urgent — a mistake or a missed order, needed immediately |
| **"All day"** | Total outstanding across all dockets — "six salmon all day" |
| **"Hands"** | Need someone to carry plates |
| **"Yes chef"** | Acknowledgement — **required, every time** |

**Acknowledging a call is not optional.** An unacknowledged instruction is an instruction that may not have been heard, and the expeditor has no way of knowing. Silence in a kitchen is a communication failure.

### Timing and Coordination

The hardest part of service is that **a table's dishes must arrive together**, and they take different times.

If a table orders a steak (12 minutes), a fish (7 minutes), and a pasta (5 minutes), the sections must start at staggered times so all three finish together. The expeditor manages this; the section cook must give **accurate time estimates** and hit them.

**Giving an inaccurate time is worse than giving a long one.** If you say four minutes and take eight, everything else on that table is sitting under the lamp going cold.

### Working Clean

- **Clean as you go.** A station that becomes chaotic mid-service cannot be recovered while service continues
- Wipe down between dishes
- Return items to their place immediately
- Keep the bin clear and the floor clear
- **A tidy station is a fast station** — you can find things

### Managing Pressure

- **Prioritise by time, not by order of arrival.** The dish that takes longest starts first, regardless of when the docket came in
- **Communicate early when you are behind.** A cook who says "I'm two behind on the grill" at the moment they realise gives everyone a chance to adjust. A cook who says nothing until the plates do not arrive has created a bigger problem
- **Ask for help before it is critical**, not after
- **Do not send substandard food to save time.** It will come back, costing more time than doing it properly, and it damages the venue's reputation

## Consistency and Standards

### Standard Recipes

A standard recipe specifies ingredients, quantities, method, yield, portion size, and presentation. Its purpose is **consistency** — the dish is the same whoever makes it, on any day.

**Follow the standard recipe.** Improvising on a menu dish means the customer who liked it last time gets something different, and the food cost calculation no longer holds.

**If you believe a recipe can be improved, raise it with the chef** — do not change it unilaterally.

### Portion Control

Portion control matters for:
- **Consistency** — the customer gets what they paid for
- **Cost** — over-portioning destroys gross profit
- **Presentation** — plates are designed around a portion size

**Tools:** scales, portion scoops, ladles of known volume, moulds, pre-portioning during prep.

**A 10% over-portion on a dish with a 30% food cost is a significant margin loss across a service.** Portioning by eye is a skill that must be calibrated against scales, not a substitute for them.

### Quality Checks

Before any plate leaves:
- **Correct dish** for the docket, including modifications
- **Temperature** — hot food hot, cold food cold
- **Seasoning** — tasted, where it can be tasted safely with a clean utensil
- **Presentation** — as per the standard, clean plate rim, no drips or fingerprints
- **Portion** correct
- **Allergen and dietary requirements** correctly handled and flagged

## Waste and Cost

Food cost is the largest controllable cost in a kitchen, and cooks control most of it.

### Sources of Waste

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Preparation waste** | Excessive trim, poor butchery, over-peeling |
| **Spoilage** | Poor rotation, incorrect storage, over-ordering |
| **Over-production** | Prepping more than is sold |
| **Over-portioning** | Consistently generous portions |
| **Errors** | Dishes returned, wrong orders, burnt items |
| **Plate waste** | Portions too large for customers to finish |

### Controls

- **FIFO — First In, First Out.** Older stock used first. Label everything with the date it was made or opened
- **Correct storage** at correct temperatures
- **Use trim** — vegetable trim to stock, bones to stock, bread to crumbs
- **Yield awareness** — know how much usable product comes from a raw weight
- **Accurate prep quantities**
- **Record waste** where the kitchen tracks it — you cannot manage what you do not measure

## Working With Others

### Taking Direction
- **Acknowledge every instruction.** "Yes chef"
- **Ask if unclear** — a clarifying question takes five seconds; a wrong dish takes ten minutes
- **Do not argue during service.** If you disagree, do it, then raise it afterwards
- Accept correction without defensiveness. Being told your plate is not right is information, not an attack

### Supporting the Team
- **Help other sections when your own is clear.** A section that is struggling holds up every table it is on
- Communicate what you are doing so others can plan
- **Do not leave your station in a state that makes the next shift's job harder**

### Handover
At the end of a shift, communicate to the incoming team:
- What prep is done and what is outstanding
- What is running low
- Anything wasted or discarded and why
- Any equipment faults
- Anything unusual — a customer issue, a delivery problem

### Feedback
Kitchens deliver feedback bluntly and immediately. That is a function of the environment — there is no time for a conversation mid-service.

**Blunt is acceptable. Abusive is not.** Being told "that's not good enough, do it again" is legitimate direction. Being humiliated, sworn at personally, or threatened is bullying, and the culture that normalises it is not something you are required to accept. See SITXWHS005.

## Professional Development

- **Learn every section.** Breadth makes you employable and eventually promotable
- **Ask why**, not just how — understanding why a method works lets you adapt it
- **Read** — technique references, not just recipes
- **Taste constantly** and build a memory for what correct tastes like
- **Watch senior cooks** — efficiency of movement is learned by observation
- **Keep a notebook** — recipes, ratios, timings, corrections
- **Accept that early years are hard.** Hours are long and the work is physical. That is a reason to look after yourself, not a reason to accept mistreatment

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You are the commis on the entrée section for a Saturday dinner service. 90 covers are booked. Your section has four dishes: a soup, a scallop dish, a terrine, and a salad.*

*By 8:15pm you realise you are going to run out of the scallop entrée — you have six portions left and there are still around 30 covers to come through. Historically the scallops sell to about 25% of entrée orders.*

*What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

I tell the sous chef immediately. That is the whole answer to the urgent part, and every minute I spend working out what to do before telling someone is a minute the kitchen cannot use.

---

**First — the arithmetic, quickly:**

Around 30 covers remaining. Historically scallops run at about 25% of entrée orders.

Expected demand ≈ 30 × 0.25 = **7 to 8 portions**

I have **6**.

So I am roughly one to two portions short on the historical average — but that average is exactly that, an average. A single table of four all ordering scallops puts me two-thirds through my remaining stock in one docket. **The shortfall could be worse than the arithmetic suggests, and it could arrive all at once.**

That is why this gets raised now, at six remaining, rather than at one.

---

**Second — tell the sous chef immediately:**

*"Chef, I'm down to six scallops with about thirty to come. On the usual mix that's seven or eight — I'm going to run out. Do you want to eighty-six it now, or do you want me to hold what's left?"*

Short, factual, includes the numbers, and offers the decision rather than just presenting a problem.

**Why immediately matters:** the decisions available at six portions are much better than the decisions available at zero.

At six portions, the kitchen can:
- **Eighty-six the dish now** and have front of house stop selling it before anyone is disappointed
- **Check whether more product is available** — is there anything in the coolroom, the freezer, or on another section?
- **Prep a replacement** — with 30 covers still to come, there may be time to get an alternative entrée on
- **Hold the remaining six for tables already seated** and stop selling to new arrivals

At zero portions, the only option left is telling a table that has already ordered that they cannot have what they chose. That is the worst outcome and it is the one that happens when someone waits.

---

**Third — what I check while waiting for the decision:**

- **Is there any more product?** Check the coolroom, check whether any was allocated to another section or to a function, check the freezer. Frozen scallops are not ideal but may be usable if there is time to thaw safely — and that decision belongs to the chef, not me
- **Can I stretch the portion?** Only if the chef authorises it. **I do not unilaterally reduce portion size** — that changes the dish the customer paid for and it is the chef's call, not mine
- **Is there a viable substitute** using what I already have prepped on the section?

I present these as options; I do not act on any of them without being told to.

---

**Fourth — what I do not do:**

- **I do not say nothing and hope.** Hoping is not a plan, and the shortfall does not resolve itself
- **I do not quietly shrink the portions** to make six become eight. The customer receiving a two-thirds portion has been short-changed without being told
- **I do not substitute an ingredient** without authorisation — beyond being a quality and honesty issue, an unauthorised substitution is a genuine **allergen risk** if the customer was not told what is in the dish
- **I do not blame the prep.** Whether I under-prepped or the mix ran hot tonight is a conversation for after service, not during it

---

**Fifth — after service, the actual lesson:**

This is where the value is, and it is a par level question.

**What I would review:**
- **What did I prep, and what did I sell?** 90 covers at 25% suggests roughly 22–23 portions plus contingency. What did I actually put up?
- **Was the sales mix unusual tonight?** Saturday dinner may skew differently from the historical average. Weather, a promotion, a special, or a large group can all shift it
- **Was my contingency adequate?** Prepping to the exact expected number leaves no margin for a heavy night. A sensible buffer on a dish that holds well is cheap insurance
- **Does the par level need adjusting** for Saturday dinner specifically, as distinct from the weekly average?

I would raise this with the CDP or sous: *"I ran short on scallops tonight — I prepped [x] on the 25% mix. Should the Saturday par be higher?"*

**That is a better conversation than being told.** It shows I have worked out why it happened rather than treating it as bad luck.

---

**The underlying principle:**

Running out is a prep failure, but **not reporting it early is a service failure**, and the second is much more damaging than the first. Kitchens run short of things regularly — product arrives late, a night runs unusually, something gets dropped. That is normal and manageable.

**What is not manageable is finding out at the pass.** The information is only useful while there is still time to act on it, and the person holding that information is me.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student's first action is to communicate, and they explicitly articulate why early reporting expands the available options — which is the central judgement this scenario tests. The arithmetic is correct and, more importantly, the student recognises the limitation of an average: that a single large table could consume the remaining stock at once, making the risk worse than the expected value suggests. The script offered to the sous chef is well calibrated for mid-service — brief, quantified, and presenting the decision rather than dumping a problem. The distinction between investigating options and acting on them without authority is precise, and the recognition that unauthorised substitution creates an allergen risk rather than merely a quality issue shows integration with SITHCCC042. The refusal to quietly reduce portions correctly frames it as short-changing a paying customer. The post-service review moves from the incident to the par level and sales mix that caused it, and the student volunteers that analysis rather than waiting to be corrected. The closing distinction — that running short is a prep failure but concealing it is a service failure — is exactly right. Strong Competent.
