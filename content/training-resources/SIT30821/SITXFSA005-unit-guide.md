## What This Unit Is About

SITXFSA006 covers the systems of food safety — temperatures, HACCP, storage, and the science of contamination. SITXFSA005 covers **you**: your personal hygiene, your work practices, your clothing, your health, and your habits.

The distinction matters. A kitchen can have a perfect HACCP plan, calibrated thermometers, and colour-coded boards — and still poison customers because one food handler came to work with gastroenteritis, or scratched their nose and then plated a salad.

Food handlers are one of the most significant sources of food contamination in commercial kitchens. This unit is about making safe personal practice automatic.

---

## Your Legal Obligations as a Food Handler

Under **Standard 3.2.2 of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code**, a food handler must:

- Take all reasonable measures not to handle food or surfaces likely to come into contact with food in a way that is likely to contaminate it
- Not eat over unprotected food or surfaces likely to come into contact with food
- Not sneeze, blow, or cough over unprotected food or food contact surfaces
- Not spit, smoke, or use tobacco in areas where food is handled
- Not urinate or defecate except in a toilet
- Wash their hands whenever their hands are likely to be a source of contamination
- Ensure that any exposed part of the body that comes into contact with food is clean
- Wear clean outer clothing appropriate to the food handling activity
- Take all practicable measures to ensure that anything from the body, or anything worn, does not contaminate food

**These are legal obligations, not workplace preferences.** Breaching them can result in enforcement action against the business and, in serious cases, personal liability.

## Personal Hygiene — The Core Practices

### Handwashing — The Single Most Important Control

**When you MUST wash your hands:**

| Situation | Why |
|---|---|
| Before starting work / entering the kitchen | You have touched doors, transport surfaces, your phone |
| Before handling ready-to-eat food | Ready-to-eat food receives no further cooking to kill pathogens |
| After handling raw meat, poultry, seafood, or eggs | These carry Salmonella, Campylobacter, E. coli |
| After using the toilet | Faecal-oral transmission is a primary route for food-borne illness |
| After touching your face, hair, nose, mouth, or ears | Staphylococcus aureus lives in the nose and on skin |
| After coughing, sneezing, or blowing your nose | Respiratory droplets carry pathogens |
| After handling rubbish or waste | Waste is heavily contaminated |
| After cleaning or handling chemicals | Chemical contamination risk |
| After handling money | Currency is heavily contaminated |
| After eating, drinking, or smoking | Saliva transfer |
| After handling animals | Animals carry Salmonella and other pathogens |
| After any break | You have left the controlled environment |

**Correct handwashing technique:**

1. Wet hands and forearms with warm running water
2. Apply liquid soap
3. Lather thoroughly for **at least 20 seconds** — covering:
   - Palms
   - Backs of hands
   - Between fingers
   - Thumbs (commonly missed)
   - Fingertips and under nails
   - Wrists and forearms
4. Rinse thoroughly under running water
5. Dry with **single-use paper towel** or an air dryer
6. Use the paper towel to turn off the tap if it is not hands-free

**Why single-use towels:** A cloth towel used repeatedly becomes a reservoir for bacteria. Drying clean hands on a contaminated cloth undoes the handwash entirely.

**Designated handwash basins:** Commercial kitchens must have dedicated handwashing basins, separate from food preparation sinks and pot wash sinks. Handwashing must be done at these basins only — not in a sink where food is being washed or prepared.

### Gloves — Understanding Their Limits

Gloves are **not** a substitute for handwashing. Common misconceptions:

**Wrong:** "I'm wearing gloves so my hands are clean."
**Right:** Gloves become contaminated exactly as hands do. A gloved hand that touches raw chicken and then a salad has cross-contaminated the salad just as a bare hand would.

**Correct glove use:**
- Wash hands **before** putting on gloves
- Change gloves whenever you would wash your hands
- Change gloves between handling raw and ready-to-eat food
- Change gloves if they are torn or contaminated
- Never wash and reuse single-use gloves
- Dispose of gloves in the bin, not on a bench

**When gloves are required:** Where there is a cut or wound on the hand (over a waterproof dressing), and where the workplace food safety program specifies them.

## Illness and Food Handling

### When You Must NOT Handle Food

You must report to your supervisor and must not handle food if you have or are suffering from:

- **Diarrhoea**
- **Vomiting**
- **Fever**
- **Sore throat with fever**
- **Jaundice** (yellowing of skin or eyes)
- **Any discharge from the eyes, ears, or nose**
- **Infected skin lesions** (boils, cuts, sores, infected wounds) that cannot be effectively covered

### The Exclusion Period

A food handler who has had **gastroenteritis (vomiting or diarrhoea) must not handle food until at least 48 hours after symptoms have completely stopped.** This is because the pathogens continue to be shed after symptoms resolve.

**[VERIFY: Exclusion periods can vary by state health authority guidance and by the specific pathogen involved. Check your state health department's current guidance and your workplace's food safety program.]**

### Reporting Illness

You have a **legal obligation** to tell your supervisor if you are suffering from a condition that could contaminate food. This is not optional and it is not something to hide because you need the shift.

**A food handler who works through a gastroenteric illness can cause an outbreak affecting hundreds of people.** Outbreak investigations trace back to individual food handlers, and businesses have been closed and prosecuted as a result.

### Cuts, Wounds, and Skin Conditions

- Cover any cut, wound, or lesion on exposed skin with a **brightly coloured (typically blue) waterproof dressing**
- Blue is used because it is a colour not found in food — if the dressing falls off into food, it is immediately visible
- Wear a glove over a hand dressing
- If a wound is infected (red, swollen, weeping, painful), you should not handle food — report it

## Clothing and Presentation

### Uniform and Protective Clothing

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Chef's jacket / whites** | Clean at the start of each shift; change if soiled during service; long sleeves protect arms from burns |
| **Apron** | Protects clothing; remove before leaving the kitchen (especially before using the toilet); change if soiled |
| **Trousers** | Clean; typically chef check or plain; must not have external pockets above the waist that could drop items into food |
| **Footwear** | Enclosed, non-slip, with a protective toe; no open shoes in a commercial kitchen |
| **Headwear** | Hat, cap, or hair net; all hair must be contained |
| **Beard covering** | Required in many operations for facial hair |

**Changing on-site:** Best practice is to change into work clothes at the workplace, so uniform is not exposed to outside contamination on the journey to work.

### Jewellery

**Remove before handling food:**
- Rings (except a plain wedding band in some jurisdictions — check your workplace policy)
- Watches
- Bracelets
- Necklaces (can fall into food)
- Earrings (particularly hoops and drops)
- Facial piercings where they can be removed

**Why:** Jewellery harbours bacteria in crevices, cannot be effectively cleaned during handwashing, and presents a **physical contamination hazard** if it falls into food.

### Nails
- Keep nails short and clean
- **No nail polish** — it can chip into food and hides dirt underneath
- **No false nails or nail extensions** — they harbour bacteria and can detach into food

### Personal Habits to Avoid in the Kitchen

- Touching your face, hair, or nose
- Chewing gum
- Eating or drinking in food preparation areas (use designated areas)
- Tasting food with a used utensil and returning it to the pot — always use a clean spoon each time, or decant into a separate tasting spoon
- Licking fingers
- Blowing into bags or containers to open them
- Sitting or leaning on food preparation benches
- Using a mobile phone during food handling (phones are heavily contaminated; if you use one, wash hands afterward)

## Cleaning and Sanitising

Cleaning and sanitising are **two different processes** — and both are required. Confusing them is a common assessment error.

| Process | What It Does | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Cleaning** | Removes visible dirt, grease, and food residue | Detergent + hot water + physical scrubbing + rinse |
| **Sanitising** | Reduces micro-organisms to a safe level | Chemical sanitiser or heat (≥77°C water, or dishwasher sanitising rinse) |

**You cannot sanitise a dirty surface.** Organic matter (food residue, grease) protects bacteria from sanitiser and deactivates many chemical sanitisers. Cleaning must always come first.

### The Six-Step Cleaning and Sanitising Process

1. **Pre-clean** — scrape or wipe off loose food and debris
2. **Wash** — apply detergent and hot water; scrub to remove grease and residue
3. **Rinse** — remove detergent and loosened soil with clean water
4. **Sanitise** — apply sanitiser at the correct concentration and leave for the required contact time (or use heat)
5. **Final rinse** (if required by the sanitiser's instructions — many food-grade sanitisers are no-rinse)
6. **Dry** — air dry where possible; cloth drying can re-contaminate

### Sanitiser Types

| Sanitiser | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Chlorine (bleach-based)** | Effective and cheap; corrosive to some metals; deactivated by organic matter; concentration and contact time matter |
| **Quaternary ammonium compounds (QUATs)** | Less corrosive; longer contact time; not effective against all organisms |
| **Alcohol-based** | Fast-acting; used for hands and small equipment; flammable |
| **Heat** | Water at 77°C+ for the required time; commercial dishwasher sanitising rinse cycle |

**Always follow the manufacturer's instructions** for dilution and contact time. Too weak and it does not sanitise; too strong and it is a chemical contamination risk and may corrode equipment.

### Cleaning Schedules

A commercial kitchen must have a documented cleaning schedule specifying:
- What is to be cleaned
- How often
- Who is responsible
- What method and chemicals to use
- How it is recorded and verified

Cleaning schedules typically cover: daily (benches, floors, equipment in use), weekly (extraction filters, behind equipment, cool room shelving), and periodic (deep clean, oven degrease, duct cleaning).

## Pest Control

Pests are a major food contamination risk and an indicator of poor hygiene practice.

**Common kitchen pests:** Cockroaches, rodents (mice, rats), flies, ants, stored product insects (weevils, moths), birds.

**Signs of pest activity to report:**
- Droppings (rodent or cockroach)
- Gnaw marks on packaging or structures
- Nests or nesting materials
- Live or dead insects
- Smear marks along walls (rodents)
- Damaged or opened stock packaging
- Unusual smells

**Your role as a food handler:**
- Keep the workplace clean — food debris is what attracts pests
- Store food in sealed containers off the floor
- Remove waste promptly and keep bins closed
- Keep external doors closed or fitted with screens/strip curtains
- **Report any signs of pest activity immediately** — do not attempt to deal with an infestation yourself
- Do not use domestic insecticides in a food area — pest control in a food business must be done by a licensed pest controller using approved methods

---

## Waste Management

- Remove waste from food preparation areas frequently — do not allow bins to overflow
- Keep bins covered
- Wash hands after handling waste
- Separate waste streams as required (general, recyclable, organic, oil)
- Used cooking oil must be stored and collected appropriately — never poured down drains
- Clean bins regularly; a dirty bin is a pest attractant and odour source
- External waste storage areas must be kept clean and bins closed

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You arrive for your shift at 4pm. During the shift, you observe the following:*

*1. A colleague takes a mobile phone call while portioning cooked chicken, holding the phone to their ear with one gloved hand and continuing to portion with the other.*
*2. The same colleague mentions they were "up all night with a dodgy stomach" but felt "mostly better by lunchtime."*
*3. A tasting spoon is sitting in a container of sauce on the pass, and you see the sous chef taste from it and return it to the sauce.*
*4. The handwash basin has no paper towel in the dispenser; staff are drying their hands on their aprons.*

*Identify the food safety issues and describe what you would do.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

There are four distinct breaches here, and one of them is serious enough to require immediate escalation.

---

**Issue 1 — Phone use during food handling**

The mobile phone is a heavily contaminated surface. Holding it against the face and then continuing to handle cooked chicken with the other gloved hand risks transferring pathogens — particularly *Staphylococcus aureus*, which is carried in the nose and on facial skin — onto ready-to-eat food.

Cooked chicken being portioned is **ready-to-eat** — it will receive no further cooking step to kill pathogens introduced at this stage. This makes contamination here directly a risk to the customer.

**Response:** I would say something at the time — "Mate, can you finish the call and re-glove? That's ready-to-eat." Both gloves should be discarded, hands washed, and fresh gloves put on. If the chicken has been contaminated by direct contact, it should be discarded.

---

**Issue 2 — Colleague working after gastroenteritis (MOST SERIOUS)**

This is the most serious issue and requires escalation to the chef in charge immediately.

A food handler who has had vomiting or diarrhoea must not handle food until **at least 48 hours after symptoms have completely stopped**. My colleague's symptoms stopped "around lunchtime" today — that is a matter of hours, not 48 hours. They should not be handling food at all.

The reason is that pathogens such as norovirus, *Salmonella*, and *Campylobacter* continue to be shed in faeces after symptoms resolve. Norovirus in particular is highly infectious — a very small number of viral particles can cause infection, and it is a leading cause of food-borne outbreaks traced to food handlers.

**Response:** I would raise this with the chef in charge immediately and privately. This is not something I can resolve myself, and it is not something I can let pass because we are short-staffed. I would frame it factually: "Chef, [name] mentioned they had vomiting and diarrhoea overnight and it only settled at lunchtime. Under the food safety exclusion they shouldn't be handling food for 48 hours after symptoms stop." The decision about the roster is the chef's, but the information must reach them.

I would also note that the ready-to-eat chicken this person has been portioning may need to be assessed by the chef.

---

**Issue 3 — Tasting spoon returned to the sauce**

Tasting with a spoon and returning it to the food transfers saliva — and any pathogens in the mouth and throat — directly into the food. This is a classic and very common breach.

**Correct practice:** Decant a small amount into a separate tasting spoon or dish, taste from that, and do not return the utensil to the food. If tasting directly with a spoon, that spoon goes straight into the dirty utensil tray — it is never returned to the food.

**Response:** This is a sous chef — someone senior to me — so I would raise it carefully but I would still raise it, because the risk is real. If there is a culture where this is normal, that is a food safety program issue that the chef in charge needs to be aware of. The sauce that has been double-dipped should be discarded.

---

**Issue 4 — No paper towel at the handwash basin**

Drying clean hands on an apron completely undoes the handwash. Aprons are contaminated — they are wiped on throughout service. Under Standard 3.2.2 and Standard 3.2.3, a handwash basin must be supplied with soap and a means of hygienic hand drying.

**Response:** This one I can fix immediately. I would restock the paper towel dispenser, and if there is no stock, report it to the chef in charge as a supply issue that needs resolving before service continues. A handwash basin without hand drying is effectively a non-functional handwash basin.

---

**Summary of my actions:**

| Issue | Action | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Colleague post-gastro | Escalate to chef in charge immediately | **Immediate** |
| Phone during handling | Address directly at the time; discard affected food | Immediate |
| Tasting spoon | Address at the time; discard affected sauce | Immediate |
| No paper towel | Restock myself; report if no stock | Immediate but self-resolvable |

I would not stay silent on any of these. Food safety is a shared responsibility, and a food handler who observes a breach and says nothing has failed in their own obligation.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student identifies all four breaches correctly, correctly prioritises the gastroenteritis exclusion as the most serious, cites the 48-hour rule and explains the pathogen shedding reason, correctly identifies cooked chicken as ready-to-eat and therefore high-risk, handles the seniority issue with the sous chef appropriately (raises it anyway), distinguishes between what they can fix themselves and what must be escalated, and states clearly that silence is not an option. This response demonstrates genuine food safety culture, not just recall of rules. Strong Competent.
