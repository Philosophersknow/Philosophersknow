## What This Unit Is About

The creative industries operate differently from most other employment sectors. Work is often project-based rather than ongoing. Many practitioners are freelance or work through short-term contracts. Reputation and networks drive opportunity more than formal applications. Income is irregular. Rights and ownership over what you create are commercially significant and legally complex.

CUAIND312 asks you to understand how this industry actually works — its structures, its employment realities, its legal framework, and the professional practices that let people build sustainable careers within it.

## Industry Structure

### Where Screen and Media Work Happens

| Sector | Typical Work | Employment Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature film** | Drama, documentary features | Project-based crew engagement, weeks to months |
| **Television** | Drama, factual, reality, news, sport | Series-based contracts; some ongoing roles in news/broadcast |
| **Commercial / advertising** | TVCs, online campaigns, brand content | Short intensive projects, often days |
| **Corporate** | Training, internal comms, event coverage | Freelance or in-house communications teams |
| **Digital / social content** | Online video, podcasts, social campaigns | Retainer, freelance, or in-house; increasingly the largest entry point |
| **Broadcast operations** | Playout, live production, studio ops | More likely to be ongoing salaried employment |
| **Post-production houses** | Editing, grading, sound, VFX | Mix of staff and freelance |
| **Games / interactive** | Cinematics, motion capture, trailers | Studio employment or contract |

### Funding and Support Bodies

Australian screen production is significantly supported by public funding and incentives. Understanding this landscape matters if you intend to work in independent production:

- **Screen Australia** — the federal agency supporting development, production, and distribution of Australian screen content
- **State screen agencies** — Screen NSW, VicScreen, Screen Queensland, South Australian Film Corporation, Screenwest, Screen Territory, Screen Tasmania — provide funding, attraction incentives, and industry development
- **Producer Offset / Location Offset / PDV Offset** — federal tax offsets for qualifying Australian production expenditure
- **Australia Council / Creative Australia** — broader arts funding, including some screen and digital work

**[VERIFY: Funding body names, offset rates, and program structures change with government policy. Confirm current programs and rates with Screen Australia and the relevant state agency before relying on this in a funding application.]**

### Industry Bodies and Unions

| Body | Represents |
|---|---|
| **MEAA (Media, Entertainment and Arts Alliance)** | Performers, journalists, and crew; negotiates industry awards and agreements |
| **ASE (Australian Screen Editors)** | Editors |
| **ACS (Australian Cinematographers Society)** | Cinematographers and camera crew |
| **AWG (Australian Writers' Guild)** | Screenwriters |
| **ADG (Australian Directors' Guild)** | Directors |
| **SPA (Screen Producers Australia)** | Production companies and producers |

Membership is typically voluntary but provides industry connection, professional development, awards, and in the case of MEAA, industrial representation.

## Employment Structures — Understanding Your Status

This is one of the most practically important areas of the unit, and one where new entrants are most often disadvantaged through not knowing their position.

### Employee vs Contractor

| | **Employee** | **Independent Contractor** |
|---|---|---|
| **Control** | Employer directs how work is done | Contractor decides how work is done |
| **Tools/equipment** | Usually provided by employer | Usually supplied by contractor |
| **Payment** | Wage or salary; PAYG tax withheld | Invoice for the job or agreed rate; responsible for own tax |
| **Superannuation** | Employer must pay super | Contractor generally responsible; but **see below** |
| **Leave entitlements** | Annual, personal, public holidays | None |
| **Insurance** | Covered by employer's workers compensation | Usually responsible for own insurance |
| **ABN** | Not required | Required |

**Critical point:** In the screen industry, contractors who are engaged principally for their **labour** may still be entitled to superannuation under the Superannuation Guarantee legislation, even if they invoice with an ABN. Many crew are engaged as contractors but legally meet the definition for super purposes.

**Sham contracting** — where a worker who is genuinely an employee is engaged as a contractor to avoid entitlements — is unlawful under the Fair Work Act 2009. It is unfortunately not rare in creative industries.

**[VERIFY: Superannuation Guarantee rate and the specific tests for contractor super entitlement. The ATO publishes current guidance and a decision tool.]**

### Awards and Agreements

Screen production work may be covered by:
- **Broadcasting, Recorded Entertainment and Cinemas Award 2020** — the general modern award for much of the sector
- **Motion Picture Production Agreement (MPPA)** — negotiated between MEAA and SPA, covering crew on feature and TV drama
- **Enterprise agreements** — with individual broadcasters or production companies

These set minimum rates, hours, penalty rates, meal breaks, and turnaround (minimum rest between shifts). Long hours are common in production, which makes turnaround and overtime provisions commercially and physically significant.

**[VERIFY: Current award and agreement names, rates, and coverage. Check the Fair Work Ombudsman and MEAA for current instruments.]**

### Understanding Your Rate

Freelance rates should account for what an employee's salary covers implicitly:
- No paid leave (annual, sick, public holidays)
- No employer superannuation (unless it applies as above)
- Periods between jobs with no income
- Own equipment, insurance, and professional development
- Own tax and BAS obligations

A common rule of thumb is that a freelance day rate needs to be substantially higher than the equivalent salaried daily rate to reach the same effective income. Working out your minimum viable rate is a professional skill, not a commercial afterthought.

## Copyright and Moral Rights

### Copyright in Screen Works

Under the **Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)**, a film (which legally includes video and most screen content) is protected as a "cinematograph film." Separate copyright may also exist in:
- The screenplay (literary work)
- The music (musical work and sound recording)
- Artwork, graphics, and photographs used
- The sound recording

**Default ownership:** Generally, the **maker** of the film owns copyright — usually the producer or the commissioning party. Where work is created by an employee in the course of employment, the employer typically owns copyright.

**For freelancers this matters enormously:** Unless your contract says otherwise, work you create as an independent contractor may vest copyright in you — or in the client, if the contract assigns it. **Read the copyright clause in every contract.** A contract that assigns all copyright to the client is common and often reasonable; one that does so while also restricting your right to use the work in your own showreel is worth negotiating.

### Moral Rights

Moral rights are personal rights that belong to the individual creator and **cannot be assigned or sold** (though they can be consented to being infringed). Under Australian law, creators have:

1. **The right of attribution** — to be identified as the creator of the work
2. **The right against false attribution** — not to have someone else named as creator
3. **The right of integrity** — not to have the work subjected to derogatory treatment that prejudices your honour or reputation

In screen production, moral rights are commonly addressed through a **moral rights consent** clause in crew contracts, in which the crew member consents to specified acts (such as editing, cutting for time, or omission of credit in certain formats). This is standard and generally reasonable — but read what you are consenting to.

### Clearances

A production cannot be legally distributed without clearing the rights in everything it contains:

| Element | Clearance Required |
|---|---|
| **Music** | Synchronisation licence + master recording licence (two separate rights) |
| **Archive footage** | Licence from the rights holder, for the specific territory, media, and term |
| **Artwork, posters, logos in shot** | May require permission; incidental background use may be defensible but is a risk |
| **Locations** | Location release / permit |
| **Performers and contributors** | Release form covering use of their performance and likeness |
| **Trademarks and brands** | Depiction may require clearance, particularly if the depiction could be seen as endorsement or disparagement |

**Errors and Omissions (E&O) insurance** is commonly required by broadcasters and distributors — and insurers will require evidence that clearances have been obtained. A production that cannot demonstrate clearance chain often cannot be distributed at all.

## Working Professionally in Production

### Set Etiquette

The screen industry runs on reputation, and reputation is built on set behaviour as much as technical skill.

- **Be early.** "On time" in production usually means ten minutes before call.
- **Know the hierarchy and use it.** Do not go over someone's head. If you have a camera question, it goes to the DOP through the camera department, not directly to the director.
- **Do not speak on set during a take.** "Rolling" and "quiet on set" mean exactly that.
- **Stay in your lane, but stay useful.** Do not touch other departments' equipment. Do offer to help when your department is idle and another is under pressure.
- **Learn and use people's names and roles.**
- **Never repeat information you overhear.** Sets involve confidential creative and commercial information.
- **Take direction without argument in the moment.** If you disagree, raise it afterwards and privately.
- **Radio discipline.** Keep transmissions short and clear; do not use the channel for conversation.

### Building a Career

- **Showreel:** A short, tightly edited demonstration of your best work. Quality over length — 60–90 seconds of excellent work beats five minutes of adequate work. Confirm you have the right to include material before you do.
- **Credits:** Keep an accurate record of every production, your role, the production company, and dates. This is your professional history.
- **Networks:** The majority of screen work is obtained through relationships and referral rather than advertised positions. Being reliable, pleasant, and competent on one job is the most effective marketing available.
- **Professional development:** Technology and workflows change rapidly. Practitioners who stop learning become unemployable in this sector faster than in most.
- **Reputation:** The industry is small and highly networked. Behaviour on one production is known before your next interview.

## WHS in Screen Production

Creative work is still work, and screen production carries genuine physical risk:

| Hazard | Context |
|---|---|
| **Fatigue** | Long shooting days; night shoots; inadequate turnaround. Fatigue is a leading contributor to on-set incidents and road accidents travelling home. |
| **Electrical** | Lighting rigs, generators, cabling in wet or outdoor conditions |
| **Manual handling** | Camera, grip, and lighting equipment is heavy and awkward |
| **Working at height** | Rigging, elevated camera positions, scaffolds |
| **Vehicles and traffic** | Location shooting near roads; picture vehicles |
| **Water, heights, fire, stunts, firearms** | Specialist areas requiring qualified supervision and specific permits |
| **Psychosocial** | Bullying and harassment have been significant documented issues in the screen sector; long hours and hierarchical structures increase risk |

**Turnaround** — the minimum rest period between the end of one shift and the start of the next — is a WHS control, not just an industrial entitlement. Breaching it systematically is a genuine safety issue.

**You retain the right to cease unsafe work** in screen production exactly as in any other industry. The pressure of a shooting schedule does not override WHS law.

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You have been offered your first paid freelance role as a camera assistant on a three-day commercial shoot. The production company sends you a one-page agreement that states: a flat fee of $1,500 for the three days; that you will supply your own equipment; that "all intellectual property in the work vests in the Producer"; that "the Crew Member consents to all acts or omissions that would otherwise infringe their moral rights"; and that hours will be "as required to complete the schedule."*

*Identify what you should consider before signing, what you might reasonably query, and what you should not be concerned about.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

**What is reasonable and standard here:**

**The IP assignment clause** is standard in commercial production and I would not expect to negotiate it away. The production company is paying for the work and needs unencumbered ownership to deliver it to the client. As a camera assistant I am not the author of copyright material in any case — my contribution does not generate separate copyright the way a screenplay or a musical score does. This clause is normal.

**The moral rights consent** is also standard. Commercial production involves editing, cutting to time, re-versioning for different platforms, and often omitting crew credits entirely on a 30-second TVC. Without consent, each of those could technically infringe the right of integrity or attribution. Broad consent clauses are industry norm.

However, I would note that moral rights consent should ideally be specific rather than a blanket "all acts or omissions." A more precise clause identifying the categories of use is better practice, though a blanket clause is very common and I would not expect a first-time freelancer to successfully renegotiate it on a small commercial.

---

**What I should query:**

**1. "Hours as required to complete the schedule" — the most significant issue.**

A flat fee of $1,500 for three days is $500 per day. That is a reasonable-sounding day rate — but only if a "day" has a defined length. With unlimited hours, $1,500 could be three ten-hour days ($50/hr) or three eighteen-hour days ($27.78/hr).

I would query: **What is the expected shoot day length, and what happens if it overruns?** Standard practice is a defined day (commonly 10 hours including or excluding meal breaks, depending on the agreement) with overtime payable beyond it. I would ask for the day length to be specified and for overtime provisions.

I would also ask about **turnaround** — the minimum rest between wrap and the next call. This is a genuine safety issue, not just a pay issue, and it matters most on multi-day shoots.

**2. Superannuation.**

Even though I am engaged as a contractor with an ABN, I am being engaged **principally for my labour**. Under the Superannuation Guarantee legislation, this may well create a super entitlement notwithstanding the contractor framing. I would ask whether super is payable in addition to the fee, or whether the $1,500 is inclusive of super.

This is a legitimate and professional question and does not mark me as difficult.

**3. Equipment supply.**

"You will supply your own equipment" needs definition. As a camera assistant, providing my own basic kit (tape, tools, filters, follow focus) is normal and expected. Being expected to supply camera bodies, lenses, or matte boxes would not be — that is a rental item and should be a separate equipment hire fee, not absorbed into a $500 day rate.

I would ask for **a specific list of what I am expected to supply.**

**4. Insurance and workers compensation.**

As a contractor, I am likely not covered by the production's workers compensation. I would ask whether I am covered under their policy, and if not, I need to consider personal accident/income protection insurance. Camera assisting involves manual handling, working around electrical and grip equipment, and long hours — the injury risk is real.

**5. Payment terms.**

The agreement states the fee but not **when it is paid.** I would ask for payment terms in writing — 14, 30, or 45 days from invoice. Late payment is one of the most common problems freelancers in this sector face, and an agreed term gives me something to point to.

---

**What I should not be concerned about:**

- The IP assignment (standard and appropriate)
- The moral rights consent in principle (standard, though its breadth is worth noting)
- The fact that it is only one page — short agreements are common on small commercial jobs; length is not a proxy for fairness

---

**How I would raise these:**

By email, before signing, framed as clarification rather than challenge:

> "Thanks very much for this — really pleased to be on board. Just a few things to confirm before I sign so we are both clear:
> 1. What is the expected length of the shoot day, and how is overtime handled if we run over?
> 2. Is the $1,500 inclusive or exclusive of superannuation?
> 3. Could you confirm exactly what equipment you are expecting me to supply?
> 4. What are the payment terms?
>
> Happy to sign once those are confirmed."

That is professional, brief, and does not read as difficult. A production company that responds badly to those four questions is telling me something useful about how the shoot will run.

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly distinguishes standard industry terms from genuinely problematic ones — a distinction many new entrants get wrong in both directions, either accepting everything or objecting to normal clauses. The identification of "hours as required" as the central issue is exactly right, and the arithmetic demonstrating why is persuasive. The superannuation question shows knowledge that many working freelancers lack. The equipment, insurance, and payment terms queries are all legitimate. The suggested email is professionally pitched. This demonstrates genuine industry literacy, not just recall. Strong Competent.
