## What This Unit Is About

Photography is the foundation of all screen work. Every principle that governs a still image — exposure, focus, composition, light — governs a moving one. A camera operator who does not understand exposure is guessing.

CUADIG303 asks you to capture technically competent images to a brief, process them appropriately, and deliver them in the correct format for their intended use.

## The Exposure Triangle

Three settings control how much light reaches the sensor. Changing any one requires compensating with another.

### Aperture

The size of the lens opening, expressed as an **f-number** (f/1.4, f/2.8, f/5.6, f/11, f/16).

| | Wide aperture (f/1.4–f/2.8) | Narrow aperture (f/11–f/22) |
|---|---|---|
| **Light** | More light | Less light |
| **Depth of field** | Shallow — background blurred | Deep — most of frame sharp |
| **Typical use** | Portraits, low light, subject separation | Landscape, group shots, product |

**Counterintuitive but essential:** a *smaller* f-number is a *larger* opening. f/2.8 lets in far more light than f/16.

**Depth of field** — the zone of acceptable sharpness — is controlled primarily by aperture, but also by focal length and subject distance. Longer lenses and closer subjects both reduce depth of field.

### Shutter Speed

How long the sensor is exposed, in seconds or fractions (1/1000, 1/250, 1/60, 1", 30").

| Fast (1/500+) | Slow (1/30 and below) |
|---|---|
| Freezes motion | Motion blur |
| Requires more light | Gathers more light |
| Sport, action | Long exposure, low light, deliberate blur |

**Camera shake rule of thumb:** to hand-hold without visible shake, use a shutter speed of at least 1/focal length. A 50mm lens needs roughly 1/50s or faster; a 200mm lens needs about 1/200s. Image stabilisation extends this, but does not stop subject movement.

### ISO

Sensor sensitivity to light. Low ISO (100–400) gives the cleanest image. High ISO (3200+) enables shooting in low light at the cost of **noise** — visible grain and colour speckle — plus reduced dynamic range and colour fidelity.

**Working principle:** use the lowest ISO the situation allows. Raise ISO only after aperture and shutter speed have been set to what the shot requires.

### Reciprocity — the trade-off in practice

Each of these is measured in **stops**. One stop = double or half the light.

If you open the aperture one stop (f/5.6 → f/4), you must halve the shutter duration (1/125 → 1/250) to keep the same exposure. The exposure is identical; the *look* is not — you have traded depth of field for motion-freezing capability.

**This is the central skill:** deciding which setting matters most for the shot, setting that first, and compensating with the others.

## Metering and Exposure Judgement

### Metering Modes

| Mode | Behaviour | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Evaluative / Matrix** | Assesses the whole frame | General purpose default |
| **Centre-weighted** | Prioritises the centre | Portraits, centred subjects |
| **Spot** | Measures a small area only | High-contrast scenes; metering a face against a bright background |

**Why meters get it wrong:** camera meters assume the scene averages to middle grey. A snow scene will be underexposed (the meter tries to make white into grey); a dark subject filling the frame will be overexposed. Both require **exposure compensation** — deliberately overriding the meter.

### The Histogram

The histogram graphs tonal distribution from black (left) to white (right). It is far more reliable than the rear screen, which changes appearance with ambient light and screen brightness.

- **Bunched at left, touching the edge** — underexposed; shadows are clipped and contain no recoverable detail
- **Bunched at right, touching the edge** — overexposed; highlights are blown and unrecoverable
- **Spread across the range** — generally well exposed

**Clipped highlights cannot be recovered.** Once a pixel is pure white, there is no data. Shadows retain some recoverable information, particularly in RAW. Where you must choose, protect the highlights.

### Dynamic Range

The range from darkest to brightest detail a sensor can capture. When a scene exceeds it — a person indoors against a bright window — something must be sacrificed. Options: add light to the subject, use a reflector, reposition, use a graduated filter, or bracket exposures and blend.

## Focus

| Mode | Behaviour | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-shot AF** (AF-S / One Shot) | Locks focus, then holds | Static subjects |
| **Continuous AF** (AF-C / AI Servo) | Tracks continuously | Moving subjects |
| **Manual focus** | You control it | Low contrast, low light, macro, video, precise control |

**Focus point selection matters more than mode.** Leaving the camera to choose typically focuses on whatever is closest and highest-contrast — often not your subject.

**For portraits, focus on the eye nearest the camera.** At shallow depth of field, eyes sharp and nose soft reads as correct; nose sharp and eyes soft reads as a mistake.

## Composition

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **Rule of thirds** | Place key elements on the lines or intersections of a 3×3 grid rather than dead centre |
| **Leading lines** | Lines in the scene that draw the eye toward the subject |
| **Framing** | Using foreground elements to enclose the subject |
| **Headroom** | Space above the head — too much makes the subject sink; too little feels cramped |
| **Looking room / lead room** | Space in the direction the subject faces or moves; without it the frame feels blocked |
| **Fill the frame** | Getting closer usually improves the image more than cropping later |
| **Background awareness** | Check behind the subject before shooting — poles, signs, and clutter appearing to grow from heads |
| **Symmetry and pattern** | Deliberate symmetry can be powerful; accidental near-symmetry usually looks like an error |
| **Simplify** | Remove what does not contribute |

**Rules describe what commonly works, not law.** Centre framing, extreme headroom, and cramped framing are all valid deliberate choices. The distinction between a choice and a mistake is whether you can articulate why.

## Light

### Qualities of Light

| Quality | Description | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard** | Small or distant source (direct sun, bare flash) | Sharp-edged shadows, high contrast, texture emphasised |
| **Soft** | Large or diffused source (overcast, softbox, window) | Gradual shadows, flattering on faces |
| **Direction** | Front, side, back, top, under | Determines where shadows fall and how form is revealed |

- **Front light** — flat, minimal shadow, low drama
- **Side light** — reveals texture and form; the standard for character
- **Backlight** — separates subject from background; produces rim light or silhouette
- **Top light** — midday sun; unflattering on faces (eye sockets and under-nose shadows)
- **Under light** — unnatural; conventionally reads as sinister

### Colour Temperature and White Balance

Measured in **Kelvin (K)**:

| Source | Approx. Temperature | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Candlelight | 1800K | Very warm/orange |
| Tungsten / household | 2800–3200K | Warm |
| Sunrise / sunset | 3000–4000K | Warm |
| Daylight (midday) | 5000–5600K | Neutral |
| Overcast | 6000–7500K | Cool/blue |
| Shade | 7000–8000K+ | Very cool/blue |

**White balance** tells the camera what to treat as neutral white. Set incorrectly, images have a colour cast — tungsten light on daylight balance looks orange; daylight on tungsten balance looks blue.

**Mixed lighting is the practical problem.** A room with tungsten lamps and daylight windows cannot be corrected with a single white balance. Solutions: gel the lights to match, turn off one source, or accept and manage the mix.

**Shooting RAW allows white balance to be changed after the fact without quality loss** — one of the strongest arguments for RAW.

### The Golden and Blue Hours

- **Golden hour** — shortly after sunrise and before sunset. Low, warm, soft light; long shadows. The most flattering natural light for most subjects
- **Blue hour** — the period after sunset before full dark. Even, cool ambient light that balances well with artificial lights

## File Formats and Colour

### RAW vs JPEG

| | RAW | JPEG |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Minimally processed sensor data | Processed and compressed in-camera |
| **Bit depth** | Typically 12–14 bit | 8 bit |
| **Editing latitude** | Large — recover shadows, change white balance freely | Limited — heavy edits degrade quickly |
| **File size** | Large | Small |
| **Ready to use** | Requires processing | Immediately usable |
| **Compression** | Lossless or near | Lossy — quality lost each re-save |

**Shoot RAW when** the images matter, conditions are difficult, or grading is expected.
**Shoot JPEG when** speed and volume matter and conditions are controlled — high-volume event or real estate work with immediate delivery.
**Shoot RAW+JPEG when** you need immediate files but want the safety net.

### Other Formats

| Format | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **TIFF** | Lossless, large, wide support | Print masters, archival |
| **PNG** | Lossless, supports transparency | Graphics, logos, screen use |
| **WebP / AVIF** | Efficient modern web formats | Web delivery |
| **DNG** | Adobe's open RAW format | Archival RAW standardisation |

### Colour Spaces

| Space | Gamut | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **sRGB** | Smallest | Web, social, general screen — the safe default |
| **Adobe RGB** | Wider | Print workflows |
| **ProPhoto RGB** | Widest | Editing headroom; never for delivery |

**Delivering Adobe RGB files for web use is a common and visible error** — colours appear dull and desaturated in browsers that ignore the profile. **Convert to sRGB for anything going online.**

### Resolution and Sizing

- **PPI/DPI is meaningless without physical dimensions.** An image is a pixel count; PPI only matters at print
- **Print** — approximately 300 PPI at final size. A 200×300mm print needs roughly 2400×3600 pixels
- **Screen** — size in pixels for the intended display
- **Never upscale beyond modest amounts.** Interpolation invents data; it does not restore detail

## Processing Workflow

A repeatable order prevents rework:

1. **Ingest and back up** — copy from card to at least two locations *before* formatting the card
2. **Cull** — reject technical failures; select the keepers
3. **Global adjustments** — white balance, exposure, contrast, highlights and shadows
4. **Local adjustments** — dodging, burning, masks, gradients
5. **Colour** — saturation, vibrance, colour grading
6. **Detail** — sharpening and noise reduction, checked at 100%
7. **Crop and straighten**
8. **Retouch** — blemish and distraction removal
9. **Export** — correct format, colour space, and size for the destination
10. **Archive** — RAW originals plus edit settings, backed up

**Non-destructive editing** (Lightroom, Capture One, RAW processors) stores adjustments as instructions rather than altering the original — you can always revert.

### Backup — the 3-2-1 rule

**3** copies, on **2** different media types, with **1** off-site.

Card corruption, drive failure, theft, and fire all happen. A single copy is not a backup, and a client shoot lost to drive failure is a business-ending professional failure.

## Legal and Ethical Considerations

### Copyright

In Australia, copyright in a photograph generally vests in the **photographer** — with important exceptions:
- Photographs taken by an **employee in the course of employment** are generally owned by the employer
- Commissioned photographs for **private or domestic purposes** (weddings, family portraits) may vest in the commissioner
- Contracts commonly assign copyright — **read them**

**[VERIFY: Copyright ownership rules for commissioned photographs under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) contain specific provisions and exceptions. Confirm current position before relying on it commercially.]**

**Moral rights** — attribution, against false attribution, and integrity — remain with the photographer and cannot be assigned.

### Model and Property Releases

- **Model release** — permission to use a recognisable person's image, particularly for commercial purposes. Required for advertising and stock; obtain before use, not after
- **Property release** — for recognisable private property, artworks, or distinctive buildings used commercially
- **Minors** — parent or guardian consent required
- **Editorial vs commercial** — editorial and news use has broader latitude; commercial use promoting a product or service requires releases

### Photographing in Public

In Australia there is generally no law preventing photography of people in public places. However:
- Private property owners may set conditions — shopping centres, stadiums, transport hubs
- Some sites require permits, particularly for commercial shoots
- Restrictions apply around defence, some government, and some court facilities
- **Photographing children requires particular care** — it is legal in public but sensitivity, and often permission, is appropriate
- Surveillance devices legislation restricts recording in circumstances of reasonable privacy expectation

**[VERIFY: Photography restrictions vary by state and by site. Check the relevant state legislation and any site-specific permit requirements.]**

### Manipulation and Honesty

Retouching is normal and expected in commercial and portrait work. It becomes an ethical problem when it **misrepresents fact**:
- News and documentary photography: adding, removing, or moving elements is generally prohibited by professional codes. Global tonal adjustment is acceptable; content alteration is not
- Advertising: misleading representation of a product may breach the Australian Consumer Law
- Real estate: digitally altering a property to misrepresent it is misleading conduct

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You have been asked to photograph a chef in a working commercial kitchen for a restaurant's website and social media. Conditions: the kitchen is lit by overhead fluorescent panels, there is a large window at one end letting in daylight, and stainless steel surfaces everywhere. The chef will be working — plating dishes — and cannot stop for long. You have one camera, a 24–70mm lens, and a small reflector.*

*Describe your technical approach, the problems you anticipate, and how you would solve them.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

This scenario contains three genuine technical problems: mixed colour temperature, low and unflattering light, and a moving subject I cannot direct for long. I would plan for each before shooting.

---

**Problem 1 — Mixed lighting (the hardest one)**

The kitchen has fluorescent overheads and a daylight window. These are very different colour temperatures — fluorescent commonly around 4000K and often with a green cast, daylight around 5600K. **No single white balance corrects both.** If I balance for the fluorescent, the window light goes blue; if I balance for daylight, the fluorescent-lit areas go green.

**Solutions, in order of preference:**

1. **Position the chef so one source dominates.** If I place him near the window and let daylight be the main light on his face, the fluorescent becomes a weak fill and the mismatch is much less visible. This is the cheapest and best fix and costs nothing but positioning.
2. **Turn off the overheads** if the kitchen can operate safely without them — often not possible mid-service.
3. **Shoot RAW** so I can correct globally afterwards and apply local white balance adjustments to different areas of the frame if needed.
4. **Accept and manage it** — a slight warm/cool separation between subject and background can read as natural rather than as an error, provided the *face* is correct.

**My decision:** position near the window, shoot RAW, prioritise correct skin tone above all else. Viewers forgive a slightly cool background; they do not forgive a green face.

---

**Problem 2 — Light quality and level**

Overhead fluorescent panels are **top light** — the least flattering direction for a face. It puts shadow in the eye sockets and under the nose and adds nothing to form.

**Solutions:**
- **Window as key light.** Window light is large and diffused — soft, directional, and flattering. Placing the chef with the window at roughly 45 degrees to his face gives me proper modelling instead of flat top light.
- **Reflector as fill.** Positioned opposite the window, the reflector bounces light back into the shadow side, opening up the eye sockets and reducing contrast. This is exactly what the reflector is for and it costs nothing.
- **Use the stainless steel.** Steel surfaces are large reflectors already in the room. They will bounce light around and can be used deliberately — though they also cause the next problem.

---

**Problem 3 — Stainless steel reflections**

Steel is highly reflective, producing specular hotspots that can blow out to pure white, and it reflects *me* and the camera.

**Solutions:**
- Change my shooting angle so reflections fall away from the lens — small movements make large differences with specular surfaces
- Watch the histogram for clipped highlights on the steel; expose to protect them, since blown highlights are unrecoverable
- Check the frame edges and background for my own reflection before shooting
- A polarising filter would help if I had one; I do not, so angle control is my tool

---

**Problem 4 — Moving subject, limited time**

The chef is working and cannot pose repeatedly. This is actually an advantage for authenticity — genuine working action beats a posed shot for hospitality content — but it constrains me technically.

**Settings approach:**

I would set for the constraint that matters most first, then compensate.

- **Shutter speed** is my primary constraint. Plating involves hand movement. I want at least **1/125s**, preferably 1/250s, to freeze hands cleanly. This is non-negotiable — motion-blurred hands look like a mistake, not a choice.
- **Aperture** — I want some subject separation from a cluttered kitchen background, so around **f/2.8–f/4**. Wide open at f/2.8 on a 70mm gives lovely separation but very shallow depth of field on a moving subject; f/4 is a safer working compromise that still separates.
- **ISO** — whatever these two require. In a fluorescent-lit kitchen with window light, I would expect to land somewhere around **ISO 800–1600**. Modern sensors handle this well, and mild noise is far less damaging than motion blur or missed focus.

**Focus:** **Continuous AF (AF-C)** with the focus point placed deliberately on the chef's face or hands, not left to automatic selection. Automatic selection in a kitchen will grab the nearest saucepan.

**Framing:** I would shoot a range deliberately, because the client needs multiple formats:
- Wide establishing shots showing the kitchen environment
- Mid shots of the chef working
- Tight detail shots of hands and food
- **Both horizontal and vertical versions** — the website needs 16:9 or similar, social needs 9:16 and 1:1. Shooting only horizontal and cropping later loses too much resolution and composition

---

**Before I shoot — practical preparation:**

- **Confirm the deliverables.** What sizes, what platforms, how many images, by when? This determines resolution and format decisions
- **Check I have permission** — the chef's consent to being photographed, and a model release if the images will be used in paid advertising rather than just the restaurant's own site and social
- **WHS awareness.** A working commercial kitchen has hot surfaces, knives, wet floors, and people carrying dangerous things at speed. I need to stay out of the working path, wear enclosed shoes, and not put a light stand or my own body where someone carrying a hot pan needs to walk. If I am in the way, I am a hazard
- **Food safety.** I should not lean over exposed food or touch anything on the pass

---

**Delivery:**

- Process from RAW, prioritising accurate skin tone and controlled highlights on the steel
- Export **sRGB** for web and social — not Adobe RGB, which will look flat and desaturated in browsers
- Size appropriately for each destination rather than delivering full-resolution files for web use
- Deliver both horizontal and vertical crops
- Back up per 3-2-1 before I leave and before I format anything

**Examiner Annotation:** Outstanding. The student correctly identifies mixed colour temperature as the central problem and — importantly — solves it by *positioning* rather than by reaching for post-production, which is the professional answer. The priority stated ("viewers forgive a cool background, not a green face") demonstrates genuine judgement about what matters. The settings reasoning is exemplary: the student identifies shutter speed as the binding constraint, sets it first, and lets ISO absorb the consequence — which is exactly the correct order of thinking and the opposite of the common student error of protecting ISO at the expense of sharpness. Recognition that automatic focus point selection will fail in a cluttered kitchen is practical and correct. Shooting for multi-format delivery rather than cropping afterwards shows current industry awareness. The WHS and food safety paragraph is unprompted and demonstrates that the student understands they are a visitor in someone else's hazardous workplace. The sRGB export note catches the single most common delivery error in this field. Strong Competent.
