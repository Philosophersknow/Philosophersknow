## Example 1 — CPC30220 Carpentry: Rectifying Another Trade's Error

**Units: CPCCCM2001, CPCCCM1012, CPCCCA3003**

### The Prompt

*You are framing internal walls in a new two-storey house. Working from the architectural floor plan, you set out and frame the upper floor walls.*

*The following morning the plumber arrives and tells you the bathroom wall you framed yesterday is 400mm out from where the plans he was given show it. He produces a hydraulic plan showing the wall in a different position, with the drainage stack already roughed in below the floor to suit his plan.*

*You check your set-out against the architectural plan and it is correct. His plan and your plan disagree.*

*The site supervisor is off site until tomorrow.*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**I stop work on that section and I do not move the wall.** The conflict is between two sets of documents, and resolving that is not my decision to make unilaterally — even though my set-out is correct.

**First — confirm the facts before anything else.**

- **Re-check my own set-out.** I believe it is right, but I check again before asserting it, from the same datum, against the same drawing. Being wrong after insisting I was right is a bad position
- **Check the revision numbers and dates on both drawings.** This is the single most likely explanation. If the hydraulic plan is Rev B and the architectural is Rev D, the wall was moved in a later revision and the plumber has been working from a superseded document. If the reverse, I may be the one working from a superseded plan
- **Check whether I have the current architectural drawing.** Superseded drawings on site are extremely common
- **Look for a structural drawing** — if that wall is load-bearing, the structural drawing may resolve it, and it generally takes precedence on structural matters

**Revision checking resolves a large proportion of these conflicts in about five minutes**, and it should happen before anyone starts talking about who is at fault.

**Second — assess what is actually at stake.**

This is not a cosmetic discrepancy. A 400mm error in a bathroom wall position affects:

- **The drainage stack**, which is already installed below the floor to the plumber's position
- **Waterproofing and floor falls** — a shower or floor waste located to suit one plan and a wall built to the other creates a genuine waterproofing problem
- **Fixture positions** — pan connector, vanity, shower
- **Possibly structural elements** if that wall carries load from above
- **Compliance** — bathroom layouts have clearance requirements, and drainage has gradient and access requirements

**The plumber's work is already in the ground.** That means whichever plan is correct, someone has rework, and the cost difference between moving a stud wall and re-doing an in-slab or under-floor drainage run is very large.

**Third — do not proceed on either plan.**

I would not move the wall to suit the plumber, because:
- My set-out matches the architectural drawing
- Moving it may put it in the wrong place if the plumber's plan is the superseded one
- If it is load-bearing, moving it may be a structural issue
- I would then own the error

I would also not tell the plumber to move his drainage, for the same reasons in reverse.

**Fourth — escalate today, not tomorrow.**

The supervisor is off site, which is not the same as unreachable.

- **Phone or message the supervisor now**, with photographs of both drawings showing the revision numbers and dates, and photographs of the framed wall and the roughed-in stack
- If the supervisor is genuinely unreachable, contact the **builder or project manager**
- The **architect and hydraulic consultant** may need to resolve it between them — this is a documentation coordination failure and it belongs with the people who produced the documents

**"The supervisor is back tomorrow" is not a reason to wait.** Every hour of framing that continues past this point may need to come out, and a phone call takes two minutes.

**Fifth — work elsewhere in the meantime.**

I would move to another section of the job that is not affected. **Stopping work on the disputed area is correct; standing around is not.** There is almost always something else on the schedule.

**Sixth — document it.**

- Time and date I identified the conflict
- Both drawing references, revisions and dates
- Photographs of the drawings, the framing and the roughed-in services
- Who I contacted and when
- What instruction I received and from whom

**A drawing conflict is a variation.** It will have cost and time implications, and whoever bears them will want to know what happened and when. My notes are what protect me if that becomes contentious.

**Seventh — the conversation with the plumber.**

I would keep it collaborative. He is not the problem and neither am I — **we have both correctly built to the drawings we were given.** That is a coordination failure upstream of both of us.

*"Mate, my set-out matches the architectural — let's compare revision numbers on both and photograph them, and I'll get the supervisor on the phone now. Neither of us should touch anything until someone tells us which one's current."*

That is a much better position than an argument about who is wrong, and it produces a resolution faster.

**What I would not do:**

- Move the wall to keep the peace
- Keep framing and leave the problem for later
- Wait until tomorrow
- Assume I am right because my drawing is the architectural one — architectural drawings are not automatically current

---

### Insufficient Response

I would check my measurements against the plan again to make sure I was right. Since my set-out matches the architectural drawing, I would tell the plumber that his plan must be wrong and that he will need to move his drainage. I would continue framing the rest of the walls and let the supervisor know tomorrow when he is back on site.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The student verifies their own work, which is correct, and then makes three consequential errors.

- **"His plan must be wrong"** — an unverified conclusion. The student has not checked revision numbers or dates, which is the most likely explanation and takes minutes. The architectural drawing being architectural does not make it current
- **Directing another trade to rework in-ground services** is well outside a carpenter's authority and could commit the builder to a very expensive and possibly unnecessary variation
- **"Continue framing"** — if the resolution goes the other way, the additional framing also comes out. Continuing past a known documentation conflict increases the rectification cost with every wall
- **"Let the supervisor know tomorrow"** — the most serious failure. A conflict affecting installed services, waterproofing and possibly structure is escalated immediately, not at the next convenient moment. The supervisor being off site does not make them uncontactable
- **No documentation** of the conflict, the drawings, or the timeline
- **No consideration of waterproofing, floor falls, fixture positions or load-bearing status** — the student has treated it as a dimensional disagreement rather than a compliance and cost problem

The student's set-out is correct and their conclusion may well turn out to be correct. **Being right about the measurement does not confer authority to resolve a documentation conflict**, and that is what the scenario is assessing.

## Example 2 — SIT30821 Commercial Cookery: Time and Temperature Under Pressure

**Units: SITXFSA006, SITHCCC043, SITXFSA005**

### The Prompt

*You are working a function for 150 guests. The plated main is a chicken breast dish. The kitchen has produced all 150 portions of chicken, which have been cooked and held in bain-maries on the pass ready for plating.*

*Service was scheduled for 7:00pm. Speeches have run over and the function coordinator now tells you service will not start until 8:30pm.*

*You check the bain-marie at 7:45pm and the chicken at the edge of the tray is reading 54°C.*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

I raise this with the head chef immediately, and I start checking properly rather than acting on one reading.

**What the reading means:**

**60°C is the minimum hot holding temperature.** At 54°C the chicken is in the **Temperature Danger Zone** (5°C–60°C), where bacteria multiply rapidly.

Chicken is a high-risk food — high protein, high moisture, low acid — and *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter* are both strongly associated with poultry.

**But one reading at the edge of a tray is not enough information to make a decision on.**

**First — establish the actual situation, in about ninety seconds:**

1. **Probe multiple points** across all trays — edges, centres, top and bottom of the mass. Edge portions are typically the coolest, so the true picture may be better than 54°C suggests. It may also be worse in another tray
2. **Check the bain-marie itself** — is it set correctly, is it operating, is the water level right, has a unit failed? A failed unit is a different problem from a marginal one
3. **Establish when the chicken came off the heat** and when it went into the bain-marie. This determines how long it has been below 60°C, which is the number that actually matters
4. **Check the temperature log** — if hot holding temperatures have been recorded, I can see when it was last compliant

**Second — report to the head chef immediately.** This is not my decision. 150 covers and a food safety issue is a head chef decision, and they need the information now, not at 8:25pm.

**Third — apply the rule correctly.**

The critical question is **how long the chicken has been below 60°C**, not what it reads right now.

Applying the **2-hour/4-hour rule** to time in the danger zone:
- **Under 2 hours** — can be used, or reheated to 60°C+ and returned to correct hot holding
- **2 to 4 hours** — must be used immediately, cannot be returned to holding
- **Over 4 hours** — **discard**

**If it has only recently dropped below 60°C**, the correct action is to **reheat rapidly to 60°C or above** and restore proper hot holding. Reheating must be rapid — through the danger zone quickly — not a slow drift back up in a struggling bain-marie.

**If it has been below 60°C for an extended period**, or if I cannot establish how long, it is discarded. **Where the time cannot be established, the safe assumption is the unsafe one.**

**Fourth — the real problem is that service is 45 minutes away and the food is already made.**

Even if the chicken is currently salvageable, **holding it for another 45 minutes in a unit that is already failing to maintain temperature is not a plan.**

Options I would raise with the head chef:
- **Rapidly reheat and hold in a properly functioning unit** — a combi oven at correct temperature holds far better than a marginal bain-marie
- **Rapidly chill and re-fire to order** — if there is capacity and time, chilling correctly (60°C→21°C within 2 hours, then to 5°C) and reheating at service is safer than extended holding. Whether this is achievable in 45 minutes with 150 portions depends on chilling capacity
- **Split the batch** — hold a first wave properly and re-fire the rest
- **Discard and re-cook** — expensive and possibly not feasible in 45 minutes for 150 covers, but it is on the table

**Fifth — the pressure, and why it does not change the answer.**

150 guests, a function coordinator waiting, speeches running over, and a large amount of food already produced. Discarding is a significant cost and a service crisis.

**None of that alters the microbiology.** A function of 150 people is likely to include older guests, pregnant women, and people who are immunocompromised. **A food-borne illness outbreak across 150 people would mean serious illness, notification to the health authority, likely closure, and potential prosecution.** Against that, the cost of the chicken is not a consideration.

**Reheating will not fix a long hold.** It kills vegetative bacteria but does not destroy **heat-stable toxins** — *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Bacillus cereus* both produce toxins that survive cooking temperatures. And the food will look, smell and taste completely normal.

**Sixth — document it.**

- Time I identified the issue and the readings taken, at which points
- Bain-marie status
- Established time below 60°C
- What was reported, to whom, and when
- The action taken and by whose decision

If this is a HACCP-based food safety program, a hot holding breach is a **critical limit breach** with a documented corrective action.

**Seventh — afterwards, the systemic question.**

The underlying failure is not the bain-marie. It is that **150 portions were fully produced before the service time was confirmed.** Speeches running over is entirely normal at functions and should be planned for.

Worth raising with the head chef after service:
- Should the protein be fired in waves rather than all at once?
- Is there a communication protocol with the function coordinator for timing changes?
- Are hot holding temperatures being logged at a frequency that would have caught this earlier?

**Someone should have known at 7:00pm that service was delayed.** That is a communication failure, and it is more fixable than the bain-marie.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would turn the bain-marie up to bring the temperature back over 60°C and keep an eye on it until service starts. The chicken has been cooked properly so any bacteria would have been killed during cooking, and it hasn't been sitting that long. I would let the chef know if it dropped again.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The response contains the specific misconception this scenario exists to test.

- **"Cooked properly so any bacteria would have been killed"** — this is the central error. Cooking kills bacteria present at the time of cooking. It does not prevent **recontamination or growth afterwards**, which is exactly what happens during a hold below 60°C. Cooked food in the danger zone is a growth medium, not a sterile product
- **No consideration of heat-stable toxins.** Turning the unit up will not destroy toxin already produced
- **"It hasn't been sitting that long"** — asserted, not established. The student has not checked when it came off the heat, has not consulted the log, and has not applied the 2/4-hour rule. **Time in the danger zone is the whole question and it has not been asked**
- **One reading treated as the whole picture.** No probing of multiple points or trays, no check of whether the unit has failed
- **Turning the bain-marie up is a slow reheat** through the danger zone, which is precisely the wrong method — and if the unit is failing, it will not achieve temperature at all
- **The chef is not told now.** "I would let the chef know if it dropped again" defers a food safety decision affecting 150 people to a junior judgement
- **The 45-minute continued hold is not addressed at all** — even on the student's own reasoning, the food would need to survive another 45 minutes in a unit that has already failed once
- **No documentation, no corrective action record**

The response reflects a genuine and widespread belief in kitchens — that cooking is a permanent safety step. It is not, and this scenario is designed to surface exactly that assumption.

## Example 3 — AUR30620 Light Vehicle: Diagnosis vs Parts Replacement

**Units: AURTTA003, AURETR124, AURAFA003**

### The Prompt

*A customer's vehicle has had its battery replaced twice in eight months by two different workshops. It is now presenting with the same complaint: the vehicle will not start after being parked for two or three days, but starts normally if driven daily.*

*The customer is frustrated and says "just put a decent battery in it this time."*

*The current battery is four months old. Charging system output tests normal at 14.2V.*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

I do not fit a third battery. **Two batteries in eight months with an identical recurring symptom means the battery is not the fault** — it is the casualty.

**What the pattern tells me:**

- **Fails after 2–3 days standing; fine when driven daily.** The battery is being drained while the vehicle is off, and daily driving recharges it before the drain becomes critical
- **Charging system normal at 14.2V.** The alternator is replenishing correctly, so this is not a charging fault
- **Two batteries have already failed the same way.** A repeatedly flattened lead-acid battery sulphates and loses capacity permanently — so both previous batteries were probably damaged by the underlying fault, not defective on arrival

**Diagnosis: parasitic drain.** Something is drawing current while the vehicle is switched off and the modules should be asleep.

**A third battery would fail the same way**, and the customer would have paid three times for the same non-repair.

---

**What I say to the customer:**

*"I can fit another battery, but I don't think the battery's your problem — you've had two fail the same way in eight months. That pattern says something's draining it while the car's parked. If we just fit another one, you'll be back here in a few months with the same fault and you'll have paid for three batteries. I'd like to test for a parasitic drain first — that's the thing that will actually fix it."*

**Being straight about this is both the right advice and the commercially better one.** A customer who has paid three times for the same fault does not come back; a customer whose recurring fault is finally diagnosed does.

I would be honest that diagnostic time costs money, and give an estimate before starting.

---

**The test procedure — parasitic drain:**

**1. Prepare the vehicle.**
- Battery fully charged and tested good — I must confirm the current battery is actually serviceable, or I am chasing a drain on a failed battery
- All accessories off, key out, doors closed
- **Ensure the modules go to sleep.** This is the step most commonly rushed. Modern vehicles keep modules awake for a considerable period after the doors are closed — commonly 20 to 45 minutes, sometimes longer. **Measuring before sleep gives a high reading that is completely normal and sends the diagnosis in the wrong direction**
- Use a magnet or tape on door latches so I can access the interior without waking the vehicle

**2. Measure the drain.**

- **Inductive current clamp** across the battery cable is the practical method — it does not break the circuit, so the modules do not wake
- Alternatively an ammeter in series, but disconnecting to insert it wakes the modules and the sleep period must be repeated

**Typical acceptable parasitic draw** for a modern vehicle after the modules have slept is in the order of **20–50mA**.

**[VERIFY: Acceptable parasitic draw varies substantially by vehicle and equipment level. Use the manufacturer's specification.]**

**Rough arithmetic:** a 60Ah battery with a 200mA drain loses roughly 4.8Ah per day. Over three days that is around 14Ah — enough to take a partially charged battery below cranking capability, which matches the symptom exactly.

**3. Isolate the circuit.**

With an excessive drain confirmed, pull fuses one at a time and watch for the current to drop.

- Note that **pulling a fuse can wake the modules**, so allow settling time between fuses
- Once the circuit is identified, work through the components on it
- **Consult the wiring diagram** — the fuse identifies a circuit, not a component

**4. Common causes, worth considering as I go:**

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Aftermarket accessory** | Dashcam, alarm, stereo, GPS tracker wired to a permanent live rather than a switched one. **Very common, and a poorly installed accessory is the leading cause of this fault** |
| **Module not sleeping** | A control module staying awake due to a fault or a bad software state |
| **Glovebox, boot or vanity light** | A switch not opening — easy to miss and easy to check |
| **Faulty relay** | Stuck closed |
| **Door or bonnet switch** | Keeping the body module awake |
| **Corroded or chafed wiring** | Leakage to earth |

**I would ask the customer whether anything was fitted to the vehicle around eight to twelve months ago** — a dashcam, a tracker, an alarm, a tow bar wiring kit. That question alone frequently identifies the cause, and the timing fits the onset.

**5. Confirm and repair.**

- Identify the specific component
- Repair or rewire correctly — an accessory on a permanent live is corrected to a switched live or given a proper timer/relay arrangement
- **Test the current battery's actual condition.** After multiple deep discharges it may be permanently degraded and genuinely need replacing — but I would replace it because it has been damaged, having fixed the cause, not instead of fixing the cause

**6. Verify.**

- Re-measure the parasitic draw after repair, with proper sleep time — it should now be within specification
- **Confirm with the customer over the following week** that it starts after standing. This is a fault that only proves itself over days
- Document the complaint, cause and correction

---

**What I would not do:**

- Fit a third battery and hand back the keys
- Disconnect the aftermarket accessory without telling the customer — they paid for it and may want it rewired properly rather than removed
- Charge for diagnostic time without agreeing it first
- Tell the customer the previous workshops were incompetent. It is unprofessional, and I do not know what they were asked to do — they may have been instructed to fit a battery, exactly as I have been

---

### Insufficient Response

I would test the battery and the charging system. Since the charging system tests fine at 14.2V, the battery must not be holding charge properly, so I would fit a good quality battery as the customer requested and advise them to drive the car regularly. If the problem happens again I would investigate further.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The student reaches the conclusion the customer asked for and ignores the evidence that contradicts it.

- **"The battery must not be holding charge"** — this ignores the central fact of the scenario. **Two batteries have already failed identically.** Two independent batteries do not both fail the same way in eight months by coincidence; that pattern is the diagnosis
- **The symptom pattern is not analysed at all.** Fails after 2–3 days standing, fine when driven daily, is a textbook parasitic drain signature and it is not mentioned
- **"Advise them to drive the car regularly"** — advising the customer to change their behaviour to accommodate an undiagnosed fault. The vehicle should start after being parked for three days
- **"If the problem happens again I would investigate further"** — it has already happened twice. Waiting for a third occurrence, at the customer's expense, is not a diagnostic strategy
- **No parasitic drain test**, no mention of module sleep time, no current measurement, no fuse isolation
- **Doing what the customer asked rather than what the evidence indicates.** The customer is not qualified to diagnose their own vehicle; that is what they are paying for. Complying with "just put a decent battery in it" without advising against it is a failure of professional obligation
- **No consideration of an aftermarket accessory**, which is the single most likely cause given the timing

This is the parts-replacement failure mode in its clearest form: a plausible-sounding action that will not fix the fault and will cost the customer a third time.

## Example 4 — RII30820 Civil Construction: Changed Ground Conditions

**Units: RIIRIS301E, RIIWHS201E, RIICCM201D**

### The Prompt

*Your crew is excavating a 2.2m deep trench for a water main along a rural road verge. The SWMS specifies battering the walls at the angle determined for the expected ground — firm clay.*

*By mid-morning the excavation has encountered a band of loose sand and gravel at about 1.4m depth, which was not identified in the geotechnical information. Water is seeping into the trench from this layer. The battered walls above the sand band are holding, but material is running from the sand layer and undercutting the clay above it.*

*The pipe crew is due on site after lunch and the leading hand wants to keep going and "get it laid before it gets worse."*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**Nobody enters that trench.** The conditions have changed from what the SWMS assessed, and the specific change is the one that causes fatal collapses.

**What is actually happening:**

This is not a minor variation. Three things are occurring together:

1. **Loose sand and gravel** has a much shallower angle of repose than firm clay. The batter angle specified for clay is **not safe** in this material
2. **Water seepage** is the most dangerous element. Water reduces the shear strength of the material, and flowing water actively transports material out of the face
3. **Undercutting** — material running from the sand band is removing support from beneath the clay above it

**That third point is the critical one.** An undercut clay face can appear completely stable right up until it fails, because the clay retains its cohesion and holds together while its support is progressively removed underneath. **It then fails as a block, without warning.**

This is precisely the failure mode that kills people in trenches. Workers see a wall that looks solid and has been standing all morning and take that as evidence of stability. It is not.

**A collapse at 2.2m is a fatality risk.** Around a cubic metre of soil weighs 1.5–2 tonnes; burial to chest height causes crush asphyxiation and workers do not self-rescue.

---

**What I do — immediately:**

**1. Stop work and clear the trench.** Everyone out and away from the edge. No exceptions, no "just finishing this bit."

**2. Establish an exclusion zone** around the excavation — set back from the edge, because ground can fail behind the visible face. Barricade it so nobody wanders in.

**3. Move plant back from the edge.** An excavator tracking near the edge surcharges the wall and adds vibration — both make collapse more likely.

**4. Check the spoil setback.** Spoil should be at least 1m back from the edge. If it is not, that is additional surcharge load contributing to the failure, and it should be moved back — from outside the zone, using plant, not by hand at the edge.

**5. Notify the supervisor immediately.** Not the leading hand — the site supervisor and, if the leading hand is the most senior person present, above them.

---

**Why "get it laid before it gets worse" is exactly wrong:**

I would say this plainly, because the leading hand is proposing to put people in the trench.

- **"Before it gets worse" acknowledges that it is deteriorating.** That is an argument for stopping, not for hurrying. If the face is degrading, every minute increases the risk to whoever is in there
- **Water seepage does not stabilise.** It continues and it continues removing material
- **The undercut is progressive.** It gets deeper as more sand runs out
- **Speed does not reduce exposure meaningfully** — laying pipe in a 2.2m trench takes time, and the collapse does not care that the crew intended to be quick
- **The pipe crew arriving after lunch is a schedule fact, not a safety input**

---

**What has to happen before anyone re-enters:**

**The SWMS must be reviewed and revised.** The ground conditions differ materially from those it was based on. A SWMS is required to be reviewed when the work conditions change, and these have changed fundamentally.

**A competent person must assess the actual ground conditions** and specify the support method. My view is that this now requires **shoring or a trench shield** rather than battering, because:
- Re-battering to a safe angle for loose saturated sand would require a very wide excavation, which may not fit in a road verge
- Battering does not address the water
- A shield protects workers regardless of whether the face fails

**Additional matters that need addressing:**
- **Dewatering** — the water needs managing, and dewatering has its own considerations including where the water is discharged and environmental approvals
- **Whether the geotechnical information needs revisiting** for the rest of the run. If unexpected material was found here, it may be present elsewhere along the alignment
- **The trench may need partial backfilling** to stabilise it while the method is reassessed

**[VERIFY: Excavation support requirements, competent person definitions and depth triggers are set by state WHS regulations and codes of practice. Confirm the requirements in your jurisdiction.]**

---

**If the leading hand directs the crew in anyway:**

I exercise the **right to cease unsafe work**, and I would say so to the crew as well, not just to the leading hand — because the person most at risk is whoever gets in the trench, and they are entitled to know.

*"I'm not going in and I don't think anyone should. That sand's running and it's undercutting the clay — that face can come away as a block with no warning. We need the supervisor and a proper look at it before anyone's down there."*

The right to cease unsafe work is legally protected, and I cannot be lawfully disadvantaged for exercising it. I would notify the supervisor, escalate to the principal contractor if needed, and document the direction and my response.

---

**Documentation:**

- Time the changed conditions were identified and what was observed — depth of the sand band, water ingress, undercutting
- Photographs, taken from a safe distance
- That work was stopped and the trench cleared
- Who was notified and when
- Any direction I was given and my response

**And the productive part:** this is a **variation**. Changed ground conditions requiring a different support method have cost and programme implications, and the contractor will need the record. Documenting it properly serves the commercial case as well as the safety one.

---

**What I would do with the rest of the day:**

Stopping the trench is not stopping work. There will be other tasks — preparing bedding material, checking the pipe delivery, working on an unaffected section of the alignment. **I would raise that with the leading hand as part of the conversation**, because "stop" is easier to accept when it comes with "and here's what we do instead."

---

### Insufficient Response

I would tell the leading hand that I'm concerned about the sand layer and suggest we batter the walls back further to make it safer. If we can get the walls stable I think we could continue, since the pipe crew is coming and we don't want to hold up the job. I would keep an eye on the trench while the crew is working in it and call out if I see anything moving.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The student identifies the hazard, raises it, and then proposes to continue working in the trench — which is the outcome the scenario is testing.

- **"Batter the walls back further"** — a plausible-sounding measure that the student is not authorised to determine. The batter angle for saturated loose sand is a competent person's assessment, not a site adjustment, and in a road verge there may not be room to achieve it
- **The water is not addressed at all.** Seepage is the element driving the instability and the response does not mention it
- **The undercutting is not understood.** The student does not identify that a clay face undercut from beneath fails as a block without warning — which is the specific mechanism making this situation lethal rather than merely poor
- **"Keep an eye on the trench and call out if I see anything moving"** — this is the most dangerous element of the response. **It is not a control.** A trench collapse occurs in a fraction of a second. There is no meaningful warning period in which a spotter can shout and a worker can climb out of a 2.2m trench. Proposing observation as the safeguard indicates the student does not understand how fast the failure is
- **The SWMS is never mentioned.** Conditions have changed materially from those assessed, which mandates review. This is a legal requirement and it is the correct procedural answer
- **"We don't want to hold up the job"** — the schedule is allowed to determine the safety decision
- **No exclusion zone, no plant setback, no spoil check, no escalation to the supervisor, no documentation**

The student's instinct — raise the concern, propose a mitigation — looks responsible. The failure is that the mitigation is inadequate, unauthorised, and ends with people back in the trench.
