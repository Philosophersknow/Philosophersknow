## What This Is

Additional worked assessment examples, beyond those already contained in the unit guides.

Each unit guide contains one worked example. This bank provides **further practice scenarios per qualification**, so students who have worked through a guide have more material to test themselves against, and RTOs have a broader pool of scenario material.

---

## How Examples Are Structured

Every example follows the same four-part structure:

| Part | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **The prompt** | A realistic assessment-style scenario or question |
| **Exemplary response** | What a strong Competent answer looks like in full |
| **Insufficient response** | Where included — a plausible but inadequate answer |
| **Examiner annotation** | Why one works and the other does not |

**The insufficient responses are deliberately plausible.** They are not strawmen. They are the answers students actually write — reasonable-sounding, superficially complete, and missing the thing the assessor is looking for. Showing a student a bad answer they would never have written teaches nothing.

## How to Use It

### Students

1. **Read the prompt only.** Cover the response.
2. **Write your own answer** before reading further. Reading a model answer and thinking "yes, I'd have said that" is not the same as having said it.
3. **Compare** against the exemplary response — not for wording, but for what it covers that yours did not.
4. **Read the insufficient response.** If your answer resembles it more than the exemplary one, that is the most useful information in the document.
5. **Read the annotation** to understand the distinction.

### RTOs and Trainers

- Scenario material for classroom discussion and formative assessment
- Benchmark responses for assessor calibration and moderation
- Evidence of supporting resources for compliance and scope purposes
- A basis for developing your own contextualised assessment tools

**These are teaching and practice resources. They are not assessment tools for a specific RTO's validated assessment system**, and they should not be submitted as a student's own work. See the note on academic integrity below.

## A Note on Academic Integrity

These resources exist to help students **understand what a competent response looks like** so they can produce their own.

**They are not to be copied into an assessment submission.** RTOs use similarity detection, assessors recognise material that does not match a student's demonstrated ability, and — most importantly — a student who submits someone else's answer arrives in a workplace without the competency the qualification claims they have.

In community services, construction, food safety and automotive work, that gap is not academic. It is the gap between a worker who recognises a deteriorating client, an unbraced roof, an over-proved dough or an under-thickness brake disc, and one who does not.

**Use these to learn the reasoning. Then write your own answer.**

## What Makes a Response Competent

Across every qualification in this library, the same patterns separate Competent from Not Yet Competent responses:

**Competent responses:**
- **Apply** the concept to the specific situation rather than describing it in general
- **Cite** the relevant legislation, standard or specification by name
- **Show the reasoning**, not just the conclusion
- **Work the calculation** where one is involved, and sense-check the answer
- **Identify what they would do**, in order, with reasons
- **Recognise the limits of their role** and escalate appropriately
- **Document** — because undocumented work is unverifiable
- **Take a clear position** on ethical questions rather than presenting both sides and stopping

**Insufficient responses typically:**
- Describe the concept correctly but never apply it to the scenario
- Say "I would follow the correct procedure" without stating what it is
- Identify the problem but not the response
- Reach the right conclusion with no reasoning shown
- Miss the escalation or documentation step entirely
- Equivocate on something the law does not permit equivocation about

**The most common single cause of a Not Yet Competent outcome is answering the general question instead of the specific one.**
