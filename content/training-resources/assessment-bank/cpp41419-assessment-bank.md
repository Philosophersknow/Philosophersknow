## Example 1 — Material Fact Disclosure

**Units: CPPREP4002, CPPREP4003, CPPREP4104**

### The Prompt

*You are selling a property for a vendor, Mr Voss. During the listing process he mentions in passing that the house "had a bit of water through it a few years back" during a storm, but that it was "all fixed up properly."*

*You later notice that the neighbouring properties on the low side of the street have raised garden beds and that one has a flood barrier stored beside the garage.*

*At the second open inspection, a young couple who are clearly very interested ask you directly: "Has this place ever had any water issues? We're first home buyers and we can't afford a disaster."*

*Mr Voss has told you he "doesn't want the flooding thing brought up because it'll kill the price."*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

I answer the question honestly. I do not have the option of concealing this, and Mr Voss's instruction does not change that.

**Why this is a material fact:**

A **material fact** is information that would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to purchase or the price they would pay. Previous flooding or water inundation is squarely within that category — it affects insurability, insurance premiums, structural condition, resale, and the buyer's basic decision about whether they want the property.

The buyers have also **asked me directly**. Even if there were any argument that the duty to volunteer it were unclear, there is none at all once a direct question is asked.

**The legal position:**

- Failing to disclose a material fact, or answering a direct question misleadingly, is **misleading or deceptive conduct** under the **Australian Consumer Law**. Silence in response to a direct question can itself be misleading conduct
- **Omission that creates a false impression** is treated the same as a false statement
- State property legislation imposes disclosure obligations on agents and vendors, and material facts regimes exist in several jurisdictions
- The consequences include rescission of the contract, damages, disciplinary action against my licence, and penalties against the agency

**[VERIFY: Specific material fact disclosure requirements vary by state — some jurisdictions prescribe material facts by regulation. Confirm the requirements in your state.]**

**What I say to the buyers:**

*"Thanks for asking directly — that's exactly the right question to ask. The vendor has told me the property had water through it during a storm some years ago, and that remediation work was done. I don't have the details of the extent or the repairs, but I'll get you what documentation exists. I'd also strongly suggest you get a building inspection and check with an insurer about flood cover for this address before you commit."*

**Why that is the right answer:**
- It discloses the fact
- It is accurate about what I know and what I do not — I am not speculating about severity or quality of repair
- It points them to the two things that will actually protect them: a building inspection and an insurance check
- It does not editorialise or talk them out of the property

**The conversation with Mr Voss:**

I would speak to him before or immediately after, and be direct.

*"Mr Voss, I have to tell you that I've been asked directly about water and I've disclosed what you told me. I can't withhold that — it's a material fact and not disclosing it would be misleading conduct under consumer law. It would expose you as well as me and the agency, and it could see the contract rescinded after settlement, which is a far worse outcome than a price adjustment now."*

**Framing the risk to him is important** — his instruction comes from a fear about price, and the honest answer is that non-disclosure creates a much larger financial risk than disclosure does. A buyer who discovers undisclosed flooding after settlement has grounds to act, and the vendor is exposed.

**What I would then do properly:**

1. **Get the facts.** Ask Mr Voss for the specifics — when, how much water, what areas, what remediation, by whom, is there documentation, was an insurance claim made?
2. **Obtain documentation** — repair invoices, insurance records, any engineer's or builder's report
3. **Check the flood mapping.** Most councils and state agencies publish flood overlay information. The neighbouring properties' raised beds and flood barrier are a strong indicator that this is a known flood-affected area, not a one-off event
4. **Ensure it is disclosed in the marketing and to all prospective buyers**, not just the ones who happen to ask
5. **Ensure the vendor's solicitor is aware** so that disclosure obligations in the contract are met
6. **Document everything** — what Mr Voss told me, what I disclosed, to whom, and when

**If Mr Voss instructs me to conceal it:**

I cannot follow that instruction. My duty to my client is to follow **lawful** instructions, and this one is not lawful.

If he insists, I would decline to act, and consult my licensee in charge about terminating the agency agreement. Continuing to market a property while concealing a known material fact would expose me to disciplinary action and potentially to personal liability.

**The commercial reality worth stating:** disclosure will likely affect the price, and that is genuinely difficult for the vendor. But **the price a property achieves when the buyer knows the facts is the price the property is actually worth.** A higher price obtained by concealment is not a better outcome; it is a liability with a delay on it.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would tell the buyers that I'm not aware of any water issues and suggest they get a building inspection, which would pick up anything like that. That way I'm not lying to them and they've got the opportunity to find out for themselves. I'd respect the vendor's wishes since he's my client and I have a duty to act in his interests.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC**, and this one matters because it is the answer many students genuinely believe is clever.

- **"I'm not aware of any water issues" is a lie.** The vendor told the agent directly. This is a false statement to a buyer, made knowingly, in response to a direct question. It is misleading conduct under the Australian Consumer Law and it is the exact conduct that ends real estate careers
- **Recommending a building inspection does not cure it.** Directing a buyer to discover for themselves a fact you already know and have concealed does not discharge the disclosure obligation — it compounds the deception by creating a false appearance of helpfulness
- **"I'm not lying to them"** — the student appears to believe that steering someone toward the truth while denying knowledge of it is honest. It is not, and the ACL does not recognise the distinction
- **The fiduciary duty is misapplied.** The duty to the vendor extends to **lawful** instructions only. It has never extended to committing misleading conduct against a third party on the client's behalf, and a student who thinks otherwise has misunderstood agency law at a fundamental level
- **The obligation to buyers is missed entirely.** The agent acts for the vendor but owes buyers honesty and freedom from misleading conduct
- **First home buyers who said they cannot afford a disaster** — the response acknowledges the stakes and proceeds anyway

The student has constructed a rationalisation rather than an answer. The consistent pattern in real estate ethics assessment is that the plausible-sounding workaround is the wrong answer, and the plain honest answer is the right one.

## Example 2 — Trust Account Breach

**Units: CPPREP4001, CPPREP4003, CPPREP4002**

### The Prompt

*You are a newly licensed property manager at a small agency. The principal, who is also the licensee in charge, asks you to process a payment: transfer $8,000 from the trust account to the agency's general operating account, describing it as "commission owing."*

*You know from the ledgers that the agency's earned commission for the period is approximately $3,000. The principal says "we're just a bit tight this month, it'll go back in when the sales settle next week."*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

I do not process that transfer. What is being described is a **trust account defalcation** — the misappropriation of client money — and it is among the most serious offences in the industry.

**What the trust account is:**

A trust account holds **money belonging to other people** — rent collected on behalf of landlords, tenant bonds before lodgement, sales deposits held pending settlement. It is not agency money. The agency's only entitlement is to withdraw commission and fees **that have actually been earned and are due**, in accordance with the agency agreement.

**What is being proposed:**

$8,000 is being taken against approximately $3,000 of earned commission. **Approximately $5,000 of that is client money.**

The label "commission owing" is a **false accounting entry** designed to disguise the withdrawal. That is not a technicality — the falsification is itself an offence, and it converts what might otherwise be argued as a bookkeeping error into evident intent.

**"It'll go back in next week" is not a defence.** Trust money cannot be borrowed. The offence is complete at the moment the money leaves; restoration afterwards is mitigation at best. Every defalcation begins as a short-term borrowing that the person genuinely intends to repay.

**The legal consequences:**

Trust account offences under state property agents legislation carry:
- **Licence cancellation** — for the principal and potentially for anyone involved
- **Substantial financial penalties**
- **Criminal prosecution**, including fraud and false accounting charges
- **Personal liability**
- Claims against the state compensation or fidelity fund, with recovery pursued against those responsible

**My own exposure is the point I need to be clear about.** If I process this transfer, I am not a bystander. I executed it. "The principal told me to" is not a defence to participating in the misappropriation of trust money — and as a licensed person I am expected to know that.

**[VERIFY: Trust account provisions, offences and penalties are set by state property agents legislation and vary by jurisdiction. Confirm the requirements and reporting obligations in your state.]**

**What I do:**

**1. Decline, clearly and without ambiguity.**

*"I can't process that. Only about $3,000 is earned commission — the rest is client money, and taking it out isn't something I can do regardless of whether it goes back. I'm sorry, I know the cash flow is a problem, but this isn't a way to solve it."*

**Firm, not accusatory.** He may be a person under genuine financial pressure making a very bad decision rather than a career fraudster. The answer is the same either way, but the tone can leave room for him to step back.

**2. Suggest the legitimate alternatives.**

The underlying problem is agency cash flow, and there are lawful ways to address it: an overdraft or business finance facility, deferring a discretionary payment, an owner's contribution, or speaking to creditors about terms. **I would raise these**, because presenting the refusal alongside an alternative is more likely to result in the right outcome than a bare no.

**3. Document immediately.**

- Date, time, exactly what was requested, the amount, the description proposed
- My response
- Anything said after that

**Contemporaneous notes are my protection.** If this comes to light later — through an audit, an inspection, or a complaint — I need a record showing I identified it and refused.

**4. Do not conceal it.**

If the principal proceeds without me, or instructs someone else to, I have a serious problem that I cannot resolve internally, because **the person committing it is the licensee in charge.** There is no one above him in the agency.

At that point my options are:
- Seek advice from the **state licensing authority** or the industry institute — most have a professional advice line, and I can seek guidance on my obligations
- Obtain independent legal advice
- Consider whether I have a **reporting obligation** to the licensing authority. In several jurisdictions there are notification duties in relation to trust account irregularities, and auditors have specific obligations
- Recognise that continuing to work at an agency where trust money is being misappropriated puts my licence at risk regardless of my own conduct

**5. Understand that my licence is the asset at stake.**

I have just qualified. My licence is the thing that makes me employable in this industry. **Participating in a trust breach, or knowingly remaining silent about one, can end that permanently** — and no employer is worth that.

**The judgement:**

The pressure here is real: I am new, he is the principal, the agency's cash flow is genuinely difficult, and refusing a direct instruction from the licensee in charge is not a comfortable thing to do in your first months.

**But trust accounting is the one area of real estate where there is no grey.** The money is not ours. The rule is absolute precisely because the temptation is common and the harm to clients — landlords, tenants, buyers — is severe.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would raise my concerns with the principal and suggest that we only transfer the $3,000 that has actually been earned. If he insisted on the full amount, I would process it since he is the licensee in charge and it is his trust account and his responsibility, but I would make a note that I had raised concerns and that the money was to be returned the following week.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The student identifies the problem correctly and then participates in the offence anyway, which is worse than not identifying it.

- **"I would process it"** — this is the failure. Executing the transfer makes the student a participant in the misappropriation of client money. Raising a concern first does not change that
- **"It is his trust account"** — factually wrong, and the misunderstanding is fundamental. **A trust account does not belong to the agency or the licensee.** It holds money belonging to landlords, tenants and buyers. The licensee is a trustee, not an owner
- **"His responsibility"** — responsibility is not exclusive. A licensed person who executes an unlawful transfer bears their own liability. Following instructions is not a defence to participating in fraud
- **"Make a note that I had raised concerns"** — a file note does not convert an unlawful act into a lawful one. It documents that the student knew it was wrong and did it anyway, which is evidentially worse than not having noticed
- **"The money was to be returned"** — the offence is complete on withdrawal. Intended restitution is not a defence, and this is the reasoning by which every trust defalcation begins
- **No consideration of external advice, reporting obligations, or the student's own licence exposure**

The proposal to transfer only the earned $3,000 is correct and shows the student understands the entitlement rule. They then abandon it under authority pressure — which is precisely the scenario the assessment is constructed to test.

## Example 3 — Underquoting and Vendor Pressure

**Units: CPPREP4101, CPPREP4102, CPPREP4103, CPPREP4002**

### The Prompt

*You have appraised a property at $780,000–$820,000 based on four comparable sales, and you have documented that estimate.*

*The vendor, Mrs Halliday, has told you she will not accept less than $850,000 and has instructed you to advertise the property with a price guide of "offers over $750,000" because, in her words, "that's what the other agents do — get them in the door and then work them up."*

*A colleague tells you this is "just how the market works" and that if you don't do it, the vendor will list with the agency down the road that will.*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

I decline to advertise a guide of $750,000. What is being described is **underquoting**, it is unlawful, and I would be the one who did it.

**What underquoting is:**

Advertising or communicating to buyers a price that is **below the agent's genuine estimate of the likely selling price, or below the price the vendor has said they will accept.**

Here, both limbs are breached at once:
- My documented estimate is **$780,000–$820,000**. A guide of $750,000 is below the bottom of my own appraisal
- Mrs Halliday has stated she **will not accept less than $850,000**. Quoting $750,000 to buyers is quoting a figure the vendor has expressly refused

**This is not a marginal judgement.** A $100,000 gap between the advertised guide and the vendor's stated minimum is not a price range; it is bait.

**Why it is prohibited:**

- **Misleading or deceptive conduct** under the **Australian Consumer Law**
- **Specific underquoting provisions** in state property legislation — several states have introduced targeted regimes with significant penalties, and NSW and Victoria in particular have active enforcement
- Requirements to record an **estimated selling price** in the agency agreement and to ensure advertised prices are consistent with it
- Requirements to provide buyers with a **Statement of Information** or equivalent containing comparable sales and an indicative selling price range

**[VERIFY: Underquoting provisions, Statement of Information requirements and penalties vary significantly by state and have been amended repeatedly. Confirm the current requirements and enforcement position in your state.]**

**The harm it causes — and who it actually harms:**

Underquoting is often defended as a harmless marketing tactic. It is not.

- **Buyers waste money.** Someone who believes a property is achievable at $750,000 pays for a building and pest inspection, pays a conveyancer to review the contract, takes time off work, and arranges finance — and then discovers they were never in contention. That is real money lost by people who were misled
- **It distorts the market.** Advertised prices that do not reflect reality corrupt the information buyers use to understand what things are worth
- **It wastes the vendor's campaign** on buyers who cannot transact at her price
- **It damages the profession.** Underquoting is the single largest source of public distrust of real estate agents in Australia

**Answering the arguments put to me:**

**"That's what the other agents do."** Some do, and some of them have been fined, named publicly, and had licences affected. The prevalence of a practice does not make it lawful, and "everyone does it" is not a defence available to me at a disciplinary hearing.

**"Get them in the door and work them up."** This is an accurate description of the intent, and it is the intent that makes it an offence. It is an admission that the advertised figure is not a genuine estimate.

**"She'll list with the agency down the road."** She might. **That is a commercial cost of compliance and I accept it.** An agency that wins listings by breaking the law has an unsustainable business and takes its clients' exposure with it. I am not prepared to trade my licence for one listing.

**What I would actually say to Mrs Halliday:**

I would not simply refuse. I would show her why the honest approach serves her better.

*"Mrs Halliday, I can't advertise at $750,000 — my appraisal is $780,000 to $820,000, and quoting below that is underquoting, which carries real penalties for both of us. But I also don't think it would work for you. Buyers who come at a $750,000 guide are $750,000 buyers. They can't stretch to $850,000, so all we do is fill the open homes with people who can't buy it, and the buyers who genuinely could afford $850,000 filter us out of their search because we look like a cheaper property than we are.*

*If your position is firmly $850,000, let me show you the evidence I've based my appraisal on and let's have a proper conversation about where the market is — and then let's market it at a number that brings the right buyers through the door."*

**Two things that does:**
1. It states the legal position without lecturing
2. It gives her the **commercial** argument, which is often more persuasive than the legal one — underquoting genuinely does attract the wrong buyers

**The harder conversation underneath this:**

There is a second issue: Mrs Halliday wants $850,000 and my evidence supports $780,000–$820,000. That gap is the real problem and the price guide is a symptom of it.

**My professional obligation is to give her my honest evidence-based assessment**, even though it is not what she wants to hear. I would take her through the four comparable sales, explain the adjustments, and be honest that on current evidence $850,000 is above what the market is likely to deliver.

She is entitled to test the market at her price. What she is not entitled to do is have me advertise a figure I do not believe and she will not accept.

**If she insists on the underquoted guide:**

I decline the listing, and I document why — the appraisal, the evidence supporting it, her stated minimum, her instruction, and my refusal. If she lists elsewhere and that agency underquotes, my file shows I identified it and refused to participate.

I would also raise it with my licensee in charge, both for support and because the colleague's advice indicates a cultural issue in the agency that will produce the same problem again.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would explain to the vendor that underquoting is illegal and that I can't advertise below my appraisal. I would suggest we advertise at $780,000 since that's the bottom of my range, which is technically within my appraisal, and then negotiate up from there. This keeps us compliant while still attracting plenty of interest.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC**, and this is the more instructive of the two failure modes because the student clearly knows the rule and then engineers around it.

- **The student has correctly identified underquoting and correctly refused $750,000.** Credit for that. The failure is what comes next
- **"$780,000 — technically within my appraisal"** — the vendor has stated she will not accept below **$850,000**. Advertising $780,000 quotes buyers a figure $70,000 below the vendor's known minimum. **That is still underquoting**, because the offence is not only about the agent's appraisal — it is also about advertising below the price the vendor has said they will take
- **"Technically" is the word that gives it away.** A response that reaches for a technical compliance argument while preserving the substance of the prohibited conduct has not understood the obligation. Consumer protection provisions are construed by effect on the buyer, not by whether a defensible-sounding number was selected
- **"Negotiate up from there"** — this is a description of conditioning buyers, which is the mischief the legislation targets. The student has restated the vendor's original plan with a different number
- **The appraisal gap is never addressed.** The vendor wants $850,000; the evidence supports $780,000–$820,000. That conversation is the agent's actual professional obligation and it is absent
- **No Statement of Information consideration, no documentation, no escalation**

The distinction between this and the exemplary response is the distinction between complying with the letter of a rule and understanding what the rule exists to prevent. In consumer protection law, that distinction decides cases.
