## Example 1 — Dignity of Risk

**Units: CHCCCS031, CHCCCS038, CHCLEG001**

### The Prompt

*Bettina is 79 and lives alone in her own home with a home care package. She has moderate osteoarthritis and had a fall six months ago. She walks with a four-wheel frame indoors but refuses to use it outside because she says it makes her "look like an old woman."*

*Bettina walks to the shops twice a week — about 600 metres each way — without the frame, carrying her shopping home in bags. Her daughter has called your organisation and demanded that the walks stop, saying "she's going to fall and break a hip and it'll be your fault."*

*Bettina is cognitively intact and has clearly stated that the walk is the most important part of her week.*

*How do you respond?*

---

### Exemplary Response

This situation is a direct tension between duty of care and dignity of risk, and it has to be resolved in Bettina's favour unless there is a reason it cannot be.

**Bettina has capacity.** She is cognitively intact and has clearly articulated what the walk means to her. That means the decision about whether to take that walk is **hers**, not her daughter's and not the organisation's.

**Dignity of risk** is the principle that people have the right to make their own choices, including choices that carry risk, and that removing all risk from a person's life also removes autonomy, purpose, and meaning. An older person prevented from doing the one thing that gives their week structure and value has been made safer in one narrow sense and significantly worse off in every other.

**Duty of care does not mean preventing all risk.** It means taking reasonable steps to identify and reduce risk. Those two are commonly confused, and the confusion usually resolves in favour of restricting the person because that feels like the safe option for the organisation.

**What I would actually do:**

**1. Talk to Bettina first.** Not to talk her out of it — to understand it and to explore whether the risk can be reduced without removing the activity.

I would explore:
- What is it about the frame outdoors that bothers her? "Looking like an old woman" is about identity and how she is seen, and that is a legitimate concern, not vanity to be dismissed
- Would she consider an alternative — a walking stick, a shopping trolley that provides some stability, a lighter or more discreet mobility aid?
- Could the shopping be split into smaller loads, or delivered, so she still makes the walk but is not carrying weight?
- Would she accept a physiotherapy or occupational therapy review to work on balance and strength, which may reduce the actual fall risk?
- What footwear is she wearing? Are her glasses current?

**Several of these reduce risk without touching the walk itself.** That is what good practice looks like here.

**2. Document the conversation** — Bettina's stated preferences, her reasons, her capacity, what was discussed, and what she agreed and declined.

**3. Report to my supervisor and the care coordinator.** This needs to be recorded formally, and a risk assessment should be documented showing that the risk was identified, discussed with Bettina, and mitigated as far as she consented to.

**A documented, discussed, mitigated risk that the person has chosen to accept is a very different position for the organisation than an undocumented one.**

**4. The daughter.** Her concern is genuine and comes from love, and I would not treat it dismissively. But:

- I cannot discuss Bettina's care with her without Bettina's consent — that is a privacy obligation under the Privacy Act and the organisation's confidentiality policy
- Even with consent, the daughter does not have decision-making authority over a mother who has capacity. She is not a substitute decision-maker, and there is no basis for her to become one while Bettina retains capacity

The response to the daughter — from the care coordinator, not from me directly — should acknowledge her concern, explain that Bettina has capacity and the right to make her own decisions, and describe (with consent) what is being done to reduce the risk. She may accept that, or she may not, but the answer does not change.

**5. What I would not do:**

- Tell Bettina she has to stop
- Quietly arrange for the shopping to be delivered so she has no reason to go, which is coercion dressed as helpfulness
- Agree with the daughter to keep the peace
- Ignore the risk entirely — dignity of risk is not an excuse for doing nothing

**The outcome I am aiming for:** Bettina keeps her walk, with whatever risk reduction she is willing to accept, and the organisation has a documented record showing the risk was properly identified, discussed and managed.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would explain to Bettina that walking without her frame is dangerous and that she has already had one fall. I would tell her that our duty of care means we can't let her put herself at risk, and I would arrange for her shopping to be delivered so she doesn't need to make the trip. I would reassure the daughter that we have addressed the problem.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** This response inverts the relationship between duty of care and the person's autonomy, which is the single most important concept in the unit.

Specific problems:

- **"We can't let her"** — Bettina has capacity. The organisation has no authority to prevent a competent adult from walking to the shops. This is not a difficult legal question
- **Arranging delivery so she "doesn't need to" walk** is the most concerning element. It removes the reason for the activity while appearing helpful. That is coercion, and it is more insidious than a direct instruction because it is harder for Bettina to identify and object to
- **Dignity of risk is not mentioned at all** — the concept the scenario exists to assess
- **The daughter is treated as the client.** She is not. Bettina is
- **No exploration of risk mitigation** — the response jumps from "there is risk" to "remove the activity" with nothing in between
- **No documentation, no supervisor, no risk assessment**
- **No recognition of the privacy obligation** in discussing Bettina's care with a family member

The exemplary response and this one reach opposite conclusions from the same facts. The difference is whose decision it is.

## Example 2 — Recognising and Reporting Abuse

**Units: CHCLEG001, CHCCCS031, CHCCOM005**

### The Prompt

*You have supported Raymond, 84, in his home for eight months. He has mild cognitive impairment. His nephew Damien moved in four months ago "to help out."*

*Over recent visits you have noticed:*
*- Raymond has become quieter and more withdrawn, particularly when Damien is home*
*- There is noticeably less food in the house than there used to be, and Raymond has lost weight*
*- Raymond mentioned last month that he "had to move some money around" for Damien's car*
*- Today Raymond has bruising on his upper arm in a pattern that looks like finger marks. When you asked, he said "I bumped it" and changed the subject*
*- Damien has begun staying in the room during your visits, which he did not previously do*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

Individually, several of these could have innocent explanations. **Together they form a pattern consistent with elder abuse**, and I have an obligation to report it.

**What the indicators suggest:**

| Observation | Possible significance |
|---|---|
| Withdrawn, especially when Damien present | Fear; psychological abuse |
| Less food, weight loss | Neglect, or financial abuse leaving insufficient money for food |
| "Moved money around" for the nephew's car | **Financial abuse** — a very common form |
| Grip-pattern bruising, implausible explanation | **Physical abuse** |
| Damien now present during visits | **Isolation** — preventing Raymond from speaking privately, and monitoring what he says. This is a recognised pattern in abusive situations, not a coincidence |

**The last one matters more than it first appears.** A change in behaviour where the person of concern begins supervising all interactions is a documented control tactic. It also means Raymond currently has no safe opportunity to disclose.

**What I do:**

**1. Do not confront Damien.** Absolutely not. Confronting a suspected abuser:
- Places Raymond at risk of retaliation after I leave
- Alerts the person to the concern and gives them opportunity to influence Raymond or remove evidence
- Is outside my role entirely

**2. Do not press Raymond, particularly not with Damien present.** I asked once about the bruise and he deflected. Pushing further in front of Damien could endanger him. If I could create a private moment naturally — assisting with personal care, or a task in another room — I would give him an opening: *"Raymond, is everything alright? You know you can talk to me."* I would not interrogate, and I would accept whatever he chose to say or not say.

**3. Document precisely, factually, and today.**

- **Exact observations**, not conclusions: "Bruising approximately 4cm on the outer aspect of the left upper arm, four discrete oval marks consistent in size and spacing" — not "bruise that looks like someone grabbed him"
- **Raymond's exact words** in quotation marks: he said *"I bumped it"* and changed the subject
- The **timeline** — when I first noticed changes, and what has changed since
- Weight loss and reduced food, with dates
- The financial comment, with the approximate date and his exact words as I recall them
- Damien's presence during visits and when that started

**Photographs only if the organisation's policy permits and Raymond consents.** I would not photograph without both.

**4. Report immediately — the same day, before I finish my shift.**

To my supervisor and the care coordinator, verbally first for speed and then in writing. This is not something that waits for the next scheduled report.

**5. Understand the reporting obligations.**

Reporting requirements vary depending on the setting and the funding stream — a Commonwealth-funded aged care provider has obligations under the **Serious Incident Response Scheme (SIRS)**, and there may be additional state-based requirements. My organisation will have a documented policy on elder abuse reporting, and my role is to report internally and promptly so that the people with the reporting obligation can meet it.

**[VERIFY: SIRS applicability and reporting timeframes depend on the service type and funding stream, and the scheme has been extended over time. Confirm the obligations applying to your specific service.]**

**6. What happens next is not mine to determine** — the organisation may involve the aged care regulator, an elder abuse service, the Public Advocate or Public Trustee regarding the financial matter, police if there is a criminal offence, or an Aged Care Assessment for Raymond's needs.

My job is to observe accurately, report promptly, and not to investigate.

**7. Continue to support Raymond normally.** Changing my manner would alert Damien and could increase the risk. Consistent, warm, ordinary support is both what Raymond needs and what is safest.

**Why I report despite Raymond's non-disclosure:**

Raymond has not alleged anything, and he has given an explanation for the bruise. That does not remove my obligation.

**People experiencing abuse frequently do not disclose** — from shame, from fear of retaliation, from fear of being moved into residential care, from loyalty to family, or because they depend on the person harming them. Requiring a disclosure before acting means most abuse is never reported.

**I am not deciding that abuse has occurred.** I am reporting a pattern of observations that warrants assessment by people qualified to make that determination. Those are different things, and the second is squarely my responsibility.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would ask Raymond privately if Damien is hurting him. If he says yes, I would report it to my supervisor. If he says no, I would keep an eye on the situation and note it in my progress notes, and report if I see anything more definite.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The response makes reporting conditional on disclosure, which is the central error this scenario tests.

- **"If he says no, I would keep an eye on it"** — this is the failure. Non-disclosure is the norm in elder abuse, for well-documented reasons. A worker who reports only on disclosure will rarely report at all
- **"Anything more definite"** — the observations are already a coherent pattern across four abuse types. Waiting for something more definite means waiting for a more serious injury
- **Asking directly whether Damien is hurting him** is well-intentioned but risky if there is any chance of being overheard, and the response does not consider that Damien is now present during visits
- **No documentation detail.** "Note it in my progress notes" without specifying factual, objective recording of observations and exact words
- **No urgency.** No same-day reporting, no verbal escalation
- **No awareness of reporting obligations** such as SIRS
- **No recognition of the isolation pattern** — Damien's new presence at every visit is treated as background rather than as an indicator

The student has correctly identified that something might be wrong. They have not understood that identifying it is the point at which the obligation activates, not the point at which they start waiting for confirmation.

## Example 3 — Person-Centred Practice Under Pressure

**Units: CHCCCS031, CHCCCS040, HLTWHS002**

### The Prompt

*You are running late. Your previous client needed extra support and you are 25 minutes behind.*

*Your next client is Joyce, 88, who has Parkinson's disease. Joyce takes a long time to do things but is determined to do as much as possible herself. Your scheduled tasks are a shower, dressing, breakfast, and medication prompting — allocated 45 minutes.*

*When you arrive, Joyce says she would like to wear the blue dress today because her granddaughter is visiting, and she wants to choose her own clothes and do her own buttons, which takes her about ten minutes.*

*You have 20 minutes before your next client, who has a medical appointment you must not make her late for.*

*What do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

This is the everyday version of the conflict this qualification is really about: the schedule says one thing and person-centred practice says another, and there is no version where I get to do both perfectly.

**What I will not do is take Joyce's autonomy away to fix a scheduling problem that is not hers.**

Joyce doing her own buttons is not an inefficiency in my visit. It is **maintenance of function** — for someone with Parkinson's, fine motor tasks performed daily are how capability is retained. It is also her choosing what she wears on a day her granddaughter is visiting, which is about identity and dignity and matters to her.

**Taking over to save ten minutes** would:
- Remove a functional activity that has therapeutic value
- Communicate that her preferences are negotiable when I am busy
- Create dependency — the person who is done *for* stops doing
- Be a decision I made for my convenience and framed as necessity

**What I actually do:**

**1. Be honest with Joyce immediately.** Not vague, not apologetic, and not pretending everything is fine while visibly rushing.

*"Joyce, I need to be straight with you — I'm running behind this morning and I've got about 20 minutes before I need to be at my next visit. The blue dress is no problem at all. Can we work out together what matters most to you this morning, so we get the important things done properly?"*

**This treats her as an adult with a stake in the decision**, rather than as a task list I am falling behind on. It also frequently produces a better outcome than I would have chosen, because she knows what matters to her.

**2. Let her set the priority.** She may say the shower matters most, or she may say she would rather skip the shower today and be dressed properly for her granddaughter. **That is a legitimate choice and it is hers to make.**

I would not assume the shower is the priority because it is on my task list.

**3. Do not compromise on the non-negotiable.** **Medication prompting is not something I trim for time.** Missing or delaying Parkinson's medication has direct clinical consequences — Parkinson's medications are highly time-critical, and a missed or late dose can significantly affect mobility, function and safety for the rest of the day.

If something has to give, it is not the medication.

**4. Find time where it does not cost her anything.** Some tasks can be done efficiently without reducing her involvement — I can be laying out breakfast while she does her buttons, rather than standing watching her. That is real time saved at no cost to her autonomy.

**5. Do not rush her physically.** Hurrying a person with Parkinson's is counterproductive as well as undignified — **stress and pressure worsen tremor and freezing**, so rushing Joyce will genuinely make her slower, not faster. This is a case where the person-centred approach and the efficient approach are the same approach.

**6. Report the scheduling problem.** This is the part that actually fixes it.

I would notify the care coordinator that the visit ran over and why, and — more importantly — flag that **45 minutes may be inadequate for Joyce's needs**. A person who takes longer because of a progressive neurological condition and who is appropriately supported to maintain independence needs a schedule that reflects that.

**If I absorb this every visit by rushing her, nobody ever learns the allocation is wrong.** The schedule keeps being set to a time that only works if her autonomy is quietly removed.

**7. The next client.** If I am going to make her late for a medical appointment, I ring the office **now**, not when I arrive late. That gives the coordinator options — reallocating the visit, or ringing ahead about the appointment. Late notice removes all of them.

**What I would document:**

- Tasks completed and any not completed, with the reason
- That Joyce chose the priorities
- Medication prompted and time
- The scheduling concern, for follow-up

**The underlying point:**

The pressure is real and it is not Joyce's fault. **The correct response to inadequate time is to be honest, prioritise with the person, protect what is clinically critical, and report the systemic problem** — not to solve it quietly by taking things away from the person receiving support, who has no idea that is what is happening.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would explain to Joyce that I'm running late today, so I'll help her get dressed quickly to save time, and we can do the buttons together. I'd make sure she gets her shower and medication, and I'd apologise and say we can take more time next visit. It's important to stay on schedule so other clients aren't affected.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC**, though closer than it appears — which is why it is a useful example.

The response is well-intentioned, polite, and gets the medication right. It fails on the central principle.

- **"Help her get dressed quickly to save time"** — this removes the functional activity. The response has identified the person-centred issue and then overridden it for the schedule, which is precisely the decision the scenario is testing
- **The clothing choice is not addressed at all.** Joyce specifically asked for the blue dress because her granddaughter is visiting. The response does not mention it. That omission is telling — the thing that mattered most to Joyce has dropped out of the answer entirely
- **Joyce is not given the choice.** She is informed of the plan. Person-centred practice means she participates in the decision about her own morning
- **No recognition that rushing worsens Parkinson's symptoms** — so the approach is also practically ineffective
- **"We can take more time next visit"** is a promise that does not address today, and the schedule will be the same next visit
- **No report of the scheduling problem.** This is the omission that guarantees recurrence. By absorbing the shortfall invisibly, the worker ensures the allocation is never reviewed
- **"It's important to stay on schedule so other clients aren't affected"** — reasonable on its face, but it frames Joyce's autonomy as the flexible element and the roster as fixed. It is the wrong way round

A student writing this answer is not careless — they are prioritising in the way an inexperienced worker under pressure genuinely would. The distinction between this and the exemplary response is the difference the unit exists to teach.
