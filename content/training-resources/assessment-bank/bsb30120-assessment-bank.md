## Example 1 — Written Workplace Communication

**Units: BSBXCM301, BSBCRT311**

### The Prompt

*You work in the administration team at a mid-sized engineering firm. Your manager, Priya, asks you to email all 40 staff about a change to the leave application process. From 1 August, leave requests must be submitted through the new HR portal rather than by email to their supervisor, and requests need 14 days' notice instead of the current 7 days.*

*Priya has given you these notes:*

> *"new system going live 1 aug — everyone has to use the portal now, no more emails to supervisors. also we're moving to 14 days notice not 7. IT will send login details. tell them there's a training session but attendance optional. any dramas come to me."*

*Write the email.*

---

### Exemplary Response

**Subject: Action required: Leave applications move to HR Portal from 1 August**

Hi everyone,

From **Monday 1 August**, the way you apply for leave is changing. There are two changes and one thing you need to do before then.

**What's changing**

1. **Leave applications go through the new HR Portal.** Emails to your supervisor will no longer be processed after 1 August.
2. **Notice period increases from 7 days to 14 days.** Applications submitted with less than 14 days' notice may not be approved in time.

**What you need to do**

- **Watch for your login details.** IT will email these to you before 1 August. If you haven't received them by **Friday 29 July**, contact IT.
- **Submit any leave you've already planned.** If you have leave coming up in the first half of August, please submit it through the portal as soon as you have your login, so the new notice period doesn't catch you out.

**Training**

An optional training session on the portal will be held — details to follow. If you're comfortable with online systems you'll likely find it straightforward, but the session is there if you'd like it.

**Questions**

Any questions about the change, please contact Priya Raman directly.

Thanks,
[Name]
Administration Team

---

**Why the email is structured this way:**

**Subject line** states the action and the deadline. "Leave process update" would be ignored; "Action required" with a date will not be.

**The two changes are numbered and separated.** Priya's notes ran them together. The notice period change is the one most likely to affect someone badly — a person who plans leave on the old 7-day assumption and is refused is the predictable failure of this rollout, so it needs to be unmissable.

**"What you need to do" is a separate section.** The most common failure in this kind of email is that the reader finishes it knowing something has changed but not what is required of them.

**The transitional problem is addressed.** Priya's notes did not mention it, but anyone with leave planned for early August is caught between the old and new notice periods. Flagging it prevents the complaints that would otherwise arrive in the first week. **This is the value the writer adds beyond transcribing the notes.**

**Tone is plain and adult.** No "we are excited to announce," no unnecessary apology, and the optional training is described honestly as optional.

**The escalation point is named** — Priya said "any dramas come to me," so the email says exactly that, with her name.

**What I would check with Priya before sending:**

- **Is the training session date confirmed?** "Details to follow" is weak; a date is better. If it is not set, I would ask whether to wait
- **What happens to leave already submitted by email before 1 August?** Is it still valid? This will be asked within an hour of sending, so it is better answered in the email
- **Are there exceptions for emergency or personal leave?** A 14-day notice period cannot apply to sick leave or an emergency, and staff will assume the worst if it is not clarified
- **Should this go to all staff or via managers first?** Managers being blindsided by a process change announced to their team is a common and avoidable problem

**These are worth raising before sending, not after.** They take two minutes to ask and they prevent a second corrective email, which undermines the credibility of the first.

---

### Insufficient Response

**Subject: Leave**

Hi all,

Just letting you know that we are moving to a new system for leave. From August you will need to use the HR portal instead of emailing your supervisor. Also the notice period is changing to 14 days.

IT will send out login details. There is a training session which is optional if you want to attend.

Any questions let me know.

Thanks

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** Every fact from Priya's notes is present. That is not the same as a competent workplace communication.

- **Subject line "Leave" is useless.** It conveys no urgency, no action and no date. In a 40-person inbox it will be skimmed or skipped
- **"From August"** is imprecise. The date is 1 August and should be stated
- **The notice period change is buried** in a subordinate clause beginning "Also." It is the change most likely to disadvantage someone and it is treated as an afterthought
- **No action section.** The reader is not told what to do or by when. There is no deadline for chasing missing login details
- **The transitional issue is not addressed.** Staff with leave planned in early August are not warned
- **"Any questions let me know"** contradicts the instruction. Priya said questions come to her; the writer has redirected them to themselves and has not named anyone
- **No structure.** Four short paragraphs with no headings, numbering or emphasis. For a two-change announcement to 40 people, structure is what makes it scannable
- **No clarifying questions raised.** The writer has transcribed the notes without noticing the gaps a reader will immediately hit

The response demonstrates the most common workplace communication failure: treating "did I include the information" as the standard, rather than "will the reader know what to do."

## Example 2 — Responding to a Customer Complaint

**Units: BSBXCM301, BSBCRT311, BSBTWK301**

### The Prompt

*You work on the service desk of a company that supplies office furniture to businesses.*

*A customer, Mr Okafor, calls. He ordered 12 desks six weeks ago for a new office. Eight were delivered on time. Four were back-ordered and he was told they would arrive within two weeks. It has now been four weeks with no contact from your company. He has staff sitting on the floor. He is audibly angry and says the last two people he spoke to promised to call back and neither did.*

*You check the system and find the four desks are still on back-order with no confirmed delivery date, and there are two internal notes from colleagues saying "customer chasing — will follow up" with no follow-up recorded.*

*What do you say and what do you do?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**What I say first — before anything else:**

*"Mr Okafor, I'm sorry. You've been waiting four weeks for something you were told would take two, and you've been promised call-backs twice that didn't happen. That's not good enough and I can see in the system exactly what you're describing. Let me tell you what I can find out, and I will call you back today either way — even if I don't have good news."*

**Why that opening:**

- **Apologise for the specific failure**, not a generic "sorry for any inconvenience." He has named two things — the delay and the broken call-back promises — and both are acknowledged
- **Confirm I can see it.** He has been dealing with people who apparently could not or did not check. Telling him the record confirms his account tells him he is being believed
- **Do not defend the company.** Explaining supply chain difficulties before acknowledging the failure reads as excuse-making and escalates the call
- **Commit to a call-back with a deadline, and commit to calling even with bad news.** The broken promise is a bigger trust problem than the delay, so the first thing to repair is the reliability of what I say

**What I do NOT say:**
- "Unfortunately that's our supplier's fault" — he bought from us
- "I'll try to find out" — vague, and it is what the last two people said
- "There's nothing I can do" — there is
- Any promise about a delivery date I have not verified

---

**What I actually do:**

**1. Get the facts before I call back.**

- Contact the supplier or purchasing for a **genuine** date, not an optimistic one
- Establish why it slipped and whether it will slip again
- Check whether the four desks can be sourced from another supplier, another branch, or a different but comparable model

**2. Look for something that solves his actual problem.**

His problem is not "four desks are late." His problem is **four staff sitting on the floor**.

Options worth exploring before I call back:
- Can we supply four loan or ex-display desks in the interim?
- Can we substitute a different model available now, at the same price or with the difference absorbed?
- Can we deliver partial — even two now?

**A workaround that gets his staff off the floor this week is worth more to him than an accurate delivery date in three weeks.** This is the difference between processing a complaint and resolving it.

**3. Consider what is owed.**

I would raise with my supervisor whether some remedy is appropriate — freight credit, a discount on the outstanding items, or a credit against a future order. I would not promise this on the call before I have authority.

There is also a consumer law dimension worth flagging internally: under the **Australian Consumer Law**, goods must be supplied within a reasonable time, and there are consumer guarantees that apply to a business purchase of this kind. **A four-week overrun on a two-week commitment, with no communication, is the sort of failure that can give rise to remedies.** That is a reason to resolve it properly rather than a reason to be defensive.

**[VERIFY: ACL consumer guarantee application to business-to-business purchases depends on the value and nature of the goods. Confirm the position before relying on it with a customer.]**

**4. Call him back today, as promised — including if I have nothing good.**

If the answer is still "no confirmed date," I ring anyway and say so. **Calling with bad news on time rebuilds more trust than calling with good news late.**

**5. Fix the internal failure, not just this call.**

The system shows two "will follow up" notes with no follow-up. That is a process failure that will happen to the next customer.

I would raise with my supervisor:
- Are back-ordered items being actively monitored, or only when a customer chases?
- Is there a mechanism that flags a promised call-back that has not happened?
- Should customers be notified proactively when a back-order date slips, rather than discovering it themselves?

**Two identical failures on one account is not bad luck; it is the process working as currently designed.**

**6. Document properly.**

- What Mr Okafor reported, in his terms
- What I found in the system
- What I committed to and by when
- What I actually did and the outcome

So that if he calls again, the next person is not the third to say "will follow up."

---

**Handling the anger:**

He is angry and he is entitled to be. **I would not take it personally and I would not become defensive.** He is not angry at me; he is angry at a company that has let him down twice and I am the person answering.

If he raised his voice I would let him finish rather than interrupting. People generally de-escalate once they are satisfied they have been heard and believed. The fastest way to prolong an angry call is to start explaining before acknowledging.

If it became abusive rather than angry, that is different — I would remain calm and, if necessary, advise that I want to help and will call back shortly. But anger at a genuine failure is not abuse and should not be treated as such.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would apologise for the inconvenience and explain that the desks are on back-order with our supplier and unfortunately these things are outside our control. I would tell Mr Okafor that I will chase it up and get back to him as soon as I have information. I would ask him to be patient and assure him we are doing everything we can. I would put a note on the system that I have spoken to him.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The response is polite and does nothing.

- **"Apologise for the inconvenience"** — generic, and it does not acknowledge either specific failure. He was promised call-backs that did not happen; that is not "inconvenience," it is the company breaking its word twice
- **"Outside our control"** — deflection. He contracted with this company. Blaming the supplier in the first minute of a complaint call is the single most reliable way to escalate it
- **"I will get back to him as soon as I have information"** — this is **verbatim what the previous two people said**, and it is what produced the current call. Repeating it, with no deadline and no commitment to call regardless of outcome, guarantees a fourth call
- **"Ask him to be patient"** — he has been patient for four weeks. This will be received as an instruction to stop complaining
- **No attempt to solve the actual problem.** No interim desks, no substitution, no partial delivery. The staff are still on the floor
- **No remedy considered**, no consumer law awareness
- **"Put a note on the system"** — the system already has two such notes. The response reproduces the exact failure it is responding to
- **No identification of the process failure.** The next customer gets the same experience

The response would feel adequate to write and would fail completely on contact with the customer. That gap is what the assessment is testing.

## Example 3 — Sustainability Within Scope

**Units: BSBSUS211, BSBCRT311, BSBWHS311**

### The Prompt

*You work in the office of a courier and logistics company with 25 office staff and a fleet of 18 vans. Your manager has asked each team member to identify sustainability improvements and present one proposal at the next team meeting.*

*A colleague plans to propose replacing the entire van fleet with electric vehicles.*

*What would you propose, and what would you say about your colleague's proposal?*

---

### Exemplary Response

**My proposal: a paper and print reduction programme in the office.**

I would choose something **within scope, measurable, and achievable without capital approval** — because a proposal that cannot be implemented is not a proposal, it is a wish.

**The problem, quantified:**

Before proposing, I would establish the baseline. I would check:
- Print volumes from the printer's usage counters — most networked printers report page counts
- Paper purchasing volume from the last three orders
- What is actually being printed — consignment notes, delivery dockets, invoices, internal reports

**Without a baseline, I cannot demonstrate improvement**, and "we should print less" is not a proposal.

**What I would propose:**

| Action | Effect | Cost | Authority needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Set all printers to **double-sided by default** | Immediate ~40% paper reduction on multi-page documents | Nil | IT to apply |
| Set printers to **black and white by default** | Reduces colour toner cost significantly | Nil | IT |
| Enable **printer sleep/power-down** outside business hours | Energy reduction | Nil | IT |
| Move internal reports to **screen-based distribution** | Removes a recurring print volume | Nil | Team agreement |
| Introduce **paper recycling bins** at each desk cluster | Diverts paper from landfill | Minimal | Manager approval, supplier |
| Review whether **customer dockets** can be digital | Potentially the largest single volume | Requires assessment | Management |

**Why this proposal works:**

- **Most of it costs nothing.** Default print settings are a configuration change
- **It is measurable.** Printer counters give a before and after, so I can report actual reduction rather than assert it
- **It is within my scope** or requires only low-level approval
- **It saves money as well as paper** — this matters, because sustainability proposals that also reduce cost get implemented and ones that only cost money get deferred
- **It applies the waste hierarchy correctly** — avoid and reduce first (don't print), then recycle. A proposal that jumps straight to recycling bins has skipped the two most effective steps

**How I would present it:** with the baseline figure, the proposed actions, the expected reduction, and the cost. A one-page proposal with a number on it is far more likely to be adopted than a general suggestion.

---

**On my colleague's proposal:**

**I would support the intent and be honest about the practicalities**, and I would say so to them before the meeting rather than critiquing it in front of everyone.

**The intent is sound.** Fleet emissions are almost certainly this company's largest environmental impact by a wide margin — 18 vans doing courier work will dwarf anything happening in the office, including my proposal. **On impact, they are looking at the right thing and I am not.** That is worth saying plainly.

**The practical problems:**

- **It is a major capital decision.** Replacing 18 vans is a very significant investment and sits with the directors, not with a team meeting
- **It is outside the scope of the exercise.** The manager asked for improvements team members could identify and propose — this is a business strategy decision
- **Operational feasibility is unaddressed.** Courier work involves daily range, load, and charging turnaround. Whether current vehicles meet the operational requirement, what charging infrastructure would be needed, and what the depot's electrical capacity is are all real questions
- **Timing and fleet cycle.** Replacing vehicles before the end of their useful life has its own environmental cost — the embodied emissions in manufacturing a new vehicle are substantial

**How I would suggest they reframe it:**

*"The fleet's obviously where the real impact is — that's a much bigger lever than anything happening in the office. But replacing all 18 at once is a board-level capital decision, and I don't think it'll get traction as a team meeting proposal. What if you pitched it as: when vans come up for replacement on the normal cycle, evaluate EV or hybrid options against operational requirements — and propose a trial with one or two vehicles first to get real data on range and charging?"*

**That reframing:**
- Keeps the substance
- Makes it achievable — a trial and a procurement policy change, rather than a fleet replacement
- Generates the operational data that would be needed to justify the larger decision later
- Is something a manager can actually take upward

**I would say this to them privately before the meeting.** Publicly picking apart a colleague's proposal is not collaborative and it is not what I would want done to mine.

---

### Insufficient Response

I would propose that the company switches to renewable energy for the office and installs solar panels on the roof, which would significantly reduce our carbon footprint. I would also support my colleague's electric vehicle proposal because reducing fleet emissions is the most important thing we can do. Both proposals would show that the company is serious about sustainability.

---

### Examiner Annotation

**NYC.** The response identifies large-impact ideas and demonstrates no understanding of scope, which is the specific thing this unit assesses.

- **Solar installation and energy contracts are capital and procurement decisions.** A Certificate III business administration employee does not propose or implement either. The unit explicitly distinguishes what a worker at this level can do from what requires management authority, and the response does not engage with that distinction at all
- **Nothing is quantified.** No baseline, no expected saving, no cost, no measurement method. "Significantly reduce our carbon footprint" is an assertion
- **No implementation detail.** Who does it, by when, with what approval
- **The waste hierarchy and resource-efficiency concepts are absent**
- **Supporting the EV proposal without qualification** repeats the same scope error rather than identifying it
- **"Would show that the company is serious"** — the measure offered is appearance rather than outcome

The student has confused *ambition* with *proposal quality*. The exemplary response argues explicitly that the colleague's larger-impact idea is aimed at the right target — so the failure here is not lack of ambition, it is the absence of any bridge between the idea and something that can actually be done.
