## What This Unit Is About

Critical thinking is one of the most in-demand skills in the modern workplace — and one of the least well understood. This unit is not asking you to be negative, argumentative, or contrary. It is asking you to think carefully, systematically, and with an open mind when facing a workplace problem or decision.

In a team environment, critical thinking means contributing to group problem-solving in a way that improves the quality of the team's decisions — by questioning assumptions, considering alternatives, evaluating evidence, and challenging your own and others' biases constructively.

---

## What Critical Thinking Actually Is

Critical thinking is the ability to:
- **Analyse** information rather than accepting it at face value
- **Evaluate** the quality of evidence and reasoning
- **Identify assumptions** — your own and others' — that shape how a problem is understood
- **Consider alternatives** — other ways of framing or solving a problem
- **Draw conclusions** that are supported by evidence and reasoning
- **Reflect** on your own thinking process and recognise its limitations

Critical thinking is NOT:
- Criticising other people's ideas
- Always disagreeing
- Overthinking simple decisions
- Being contrarian for its own sake

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Identify the problem or issue

You need to:
- Clearly define what the problem actually is (not just the symptoms)
- Identify the scope of the problem — who is affected, to what extent, when does it occur
- Gather relevant information from appropriate sources
- Distinguish between facts and assumptions in how the problem is currently understood

**Key skill: Root cause vs. symptom**

Many workplace "problems" are actually symptoms of a deeper issue. A team that consistently misses deadlines might have a time management problem — or they might have an unclear brief problem, or a resourcing problem, or an expectation-setting problem. Critical thinking means identifying the real cause, not just treating the symptom.

---

### Element 2: Evaluate options to address the problem

You need to:
- Generate multiple possible solutions (not just the first or most obvious one)
- Evaluate each option against relevant criteria (effectiveness, cost, feasibility, risk, alignment with organisational values)
- Identify the likely consequences of each option — positive and negative
- Consider the perspectives of people who would be affected by each option
- Avoid cognitive biases that could distort your evaluation (see below)

---

### Element 3: Apply critical thinking in a team environment

You need to:
- Contribute constructively to team problem-solving discussions
- Listen actively to other team members' perspectives
- Question assumptions (your own and others') respectfully and constructively
- Build on others' ideas rather than dismissing them
- Help the team reach a decision that is better than any individual could reach alone

---

### Element 4: Communicate the outcomes of critical thinking

You need to:
- Present your analysis and reasoning clearly
- Support conclusions with evidence and logical reasoning
- Acknowledge limitations and uncertainties in your analysis
- Present findings in a format appropriate to the audience (verbal summary, written report, recommendation)

## Cognitive Biases — Know These for Your Assessment

A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern of thinking that can lead to poor decisions. Assessors will check whether you can identify biases in scenarios and explain how they affect decision-making.

| Bias | What It Means | Workplace Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | Only noticing evidence that confirms what you already believe | A manager who believes a team member is underperforming only notices their mistakes |
| **Anchoring** | Over-relying on the first piece of information you receive | Assuming a project will cost $10k because that was the first estimate, even when evidence suggests otherwise |
| **Groupthink** | Team members conforming to the group consensus to avoid conflict, even when they disagree | Nobody raises concerns about a flawed plan because everyone else seems to agree |
| **Availability bias** | Overweighting information that comes easily to mind | Thinking a risk is more likely because a recent incident brought it to attention |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | Continuing with a bad decision because of what has already been invested | Continuing to spend on a failing project because you don't want to "waste" what has already been spent |
| **Halo effect** | Letting one positive quality cause you to view someone positively overall | Assuming a well-dressed candidate is more competent than they may be |
| **Framing effect** | How information is presented affects how it is evaluated | "90% success rate" vs. "10% failure rate" — same information, very different feel |

## The Critical Thinking Process — A Practical Framework

Use this as a structure for any problem-solving scenario in your assessment:

**Step 1 — Define**
What exactly is the problem? Who is affected? What are the boundaries of the problem? What do we know and what are we assuming?

**Step 2 — Gather**
What information do we need? Where will we get it? What sources are credible and relevant?

**Step 3 — Analyse**
What does the information tell us? Are there patterns? What are the possible causes? What assumptions are embedded in how the problem is framed?

**Step 4 — Generate options**
What are at least three different ways this problem could be addressed? (Aim for genuine alternatives, not variations of the same idea.)

**Step 5 — Evaluate**
What are the pros and cons of each option? What are the risks? What do the people affected prefer?

**Step 6 — Decide and justify**
Which option is best, and why? What evidence and reasoning support this choice?

**Step 7 — Reflect**
After implementation: what worked? What didn't? What would we do differently?

## Common Mistakes

**1. Describing one solution without evaluating alternatives**

Critical thinking requires generating and evaluating multiple options. An assessment response that goes straight to "the solution" without considering alternatives demonstrates the opposite of critical thinking. Always show your process: what options did you consider, and why did you choose the one you did?

---

**2. Presenting opinions as facts**

"Everyone knows that customers prefer [X]" is not evidence. "Customer survey data from Q1 2026 shows 68% of customers prefer [X]" is evidence. Practice distinguishing between what you think, what you assume, and what you can demonstrate.

---

**3. Ignoring the team element**

BSBCRT311 is specifically about critical thinking *in a team environment*. Responses that only describe individual thinking, without addressing how you contribute to team decision-making, are incomplete. Assessors want to see: how do you handle it when the team is heading in a direction you think is wrong? How do you challenge without damaging the team dynamic?

---

**4. Shallow reflection**

Reflection is not just "I learned a lot." Genuine reflection requires: What happened? What did I think at the time? What was I assuming? What evidence did I overlook? What would I do differently? Use this structure.

## Worked Example — Critical Thinking Case Study

**Prompt:**

*Your team at a small marketing firm has been tasked with deciding whether to switch to a new project management software. The current software (ProjectFlow) is old and has limited features. Three team members have tried a new tool called TeamHub and love it. One team member (the longest-serving staff member) is resistant to the change and says, "We've always used ProjectFlow — it works fine." Your manager wants the team to make a recommendation by Friday.*

*Using a critical thinking approach, describe how the team should approach this decision.*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

The first step is to define the problem clearly. The problem is not "which software is better" — it is "does the team need to change software, and if so, what criteria should guide the choice?" Starting with this framing avoids rushing to a solution before the problem is properly understood.

The team should identify what they actually need from project management software: current pain points with ProjectFlow, features required for upcoming projects, integration with other tools, cost, training requirements, and user adoption risk. This information should be gathered from all team members — including the resistant staff member, whose concerns deserve a hearing.

The team should then evaluate at least three options: (1) continue with ProjectFlow with potential upgrades or workarounds; (2) switch to TeamHub; (3) evaluate an additional alternative tool before deciding. Evaluating only two options (current vs. proposed) is a common decision-making error — it forces a binary choice when better alternatives may exist.

The enthusiasm of the three team members for TeamHub should be taken seriously, but the team should also identify potential biases: they may be experiencing the "novelty effect" — new tools feel better partly because they are new. The manager should ask whether there are objective performance metrics: does TeamHub demonstrably improve project completion rates, reduce miscommunication, or save time?

The longest-serving staff member's resistance should also be examined critically. Is their concern based on evidence — for example, a previous failed software transition that created months of disruption? Or is it a status quo bias — preferring the familiar regardless of its merits? Both are worth understanding. The team should not dismiss the concern; they should understand it.

A structured decision could use criteria weighting: list the 5–7 most important criteria, weight each by importance to the team, and score each option against the criteria. This makes the reasoning transparent and reduces the influence of individual biases.

The team should present its recommendation to the manager with a clear rationale — not just "we chose TeamHub" but why, what alternatives were considered, what risks were identified, and how those risks will be managed.

**Examiner Annotation:** This response demonstrates genuine critical thinking throughout: problem definition (not jumping to the solution), multiple options, evidence evaluation, bias identification (novelty effect, status quo bias), team dynamics management, and structured decision-making. The student understands that the goal is a better decision, not a comfortable one. Strong Competent.
