## What This Unit Is About

Every business, regardless of size or industry, has WHS obligations. As an office or business services worker, you are not a WHS officer — but you are expected to recognise hazards, follow safe work procedures, contribute to hazard reporting, and participate in workplace safety processes. BSBWHS311 asks you to demonstrate that you can do all of these things consistently.

---

## The Legislative Framework

### Model WHS Act
The Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Cth) is the model legislation adopted in most Australian states and territories (WA uses the Occupational Safety and Health Act 1984; VIC uses the Occupational Health and Safety Act 2004 — [VERIFY: current status in each state]).

**Key concepts:**

**PCBU (Person Conducting a Business or Undertaking):** The employer. Has the primary duty to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health, safety, and welfare of workers and others.

**Workers:** Must take reasonable care for their own health and safety; must take reasonable care not to adversely affect others; must comply with reasonable WHS instructions.

**"So far as is reasonably practicable":** WHS obligations are not absolute. They require weighing the likelihood and severity of harm against the costs and difficulty of eliminating or minimising the risk.

**Health and Safety Representatives (HSRs):** Workers elected to represent their work group in WHS matters; have statutory powers to inspect the workplace, issue provisional improvement notices, and direct work to cease in urgent situations.

**Health and Safety Committees:** Bodies including both management and worker representatives that consult on WHS issues. Many larger organisations are required to establish them.

## Elements of Competency

### Element 1: Contribute to workplace safety processes

You need to:
- Participate in WHS consultation processes
- Understand your role in WHS management relative to your level and responsibilities
- Access and follow WHS policies and procedures relevant to your role
- Contribute to WHS inspections and audits when asked

---

### Element 2: Identify and report hazards

You need to:
- Proactively identify hazards in your work area
- Report hazards using your organisation's system (hazard report form, electronic system)
- Not walk past a hazard and leave it for someone else to deal with
- Follow up to ensure reported hazards are addressed

---

### Element 3: Implement risk controls

You need to:
- Understand the hierarchy of controls (see below)
- Apply risk controls as instructed by your organisation
- Not introduce new hazards when implementing controls
- Report when controls are not working or are not being followed

---

### Element 4: Participate in WHS training and consultation

You need to:
- Attend and actively participate in WHS training and induction
- Contribute your observations and concerns in WHS consultation
- Understand that consultation is a legal requirement — employers must consult workers on WHS matters

## The Hierarchy of Controls

When a hazard is identified, controls are applied in order from most to least effective:

| Level | Control Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Elimination** | Remove the hazard completely | Remove a damaged cable entirely rather than taping it |
| 2 | **Substitution** | Replace with something less hazardous | Replace a heavy filing cabinet with a digital document system |
| 3 | **Isolation** | Separate the hazard from people | Fence off a slippery area; store chemicals in a locked cabinet |
| 4 | **Engineering controls** | Physical modifications to reduce the risk | Install ergonomic workstations; add guarding to machinery |
| 5 | **Administrative controls** | Procedures and training to manage the risk | Safe work procedures; hazard warning signs; job rotation |
| 6 | **PPE (Personal Protective Equipment)** | Equipment worn by the worker to reduce harm | Safety glasses, hearing protection, gloves |

**Key principle:** PPE is the last resort, not the first response. It protects the worker but does not remove or reduce the hazard.

## Common Hazards in a Business Services Setting

Unlike a construction site or care facility, a business services workplace may seem low-risk. But hazards exist in every workplace:

| Hazard Type | Business Services Examples |
|---|---|
| **Ergonomic** | Poor workstation setup, repetitive keyboard work, sustained sitting, awkward screen position |
| **Psychological / psychosocial** | Work overload, harassment, poor management, long hours, lack of control |
| **Slip/trip/fall** | Cables across walkways, wet floors, poor lighting, cluttered aisles |
| **Fire and emergency** | Blocked exit routes, faulty electrical equipment, overloaded power boards |
| **Hazardous substances** | Cleaning products, printer toners, solvents |
| **Violence and aggression** | Customer-facing roles; lone working |

## Incident Reporting

An **incident** is an event that:
- Has caused injury or illness to a person, OR
- Has caused damage to property, OR
- **Could have** caused injury, illness, or damage (a near miss)

Near misses must be reported — they are early warnings that a control has failed or a hazard was not managed.

**What to do when an incident occurs:**
1. Make the area safe if it is within your capability to do so — without creating further risk
2. Call emergency services if anyone is injured
3. Notify your supervisor immediately
4. Do not disturb the scene (where relevant for investigation)
5. Complete an incident report as soon as practicable
6. Cooperate with any investigation

**Notifiable incidents** (those that must be reported to the WHS regulator) include serious injuries (requiring hospitalisation), death, or dangerous incidents. Your supervisor or WHS officer manages the regulator notification — but you must still report to your supervisor immediately.

## Emergency Procedures

Every business must have emergency procedures covering at least:
- Emergency evacuation
- Fire response
- First aid

**Your responsibilities:**
- Know the location of evacuation exits, assembly point, and fire extinguishers
- Know who your first aider is and where the first aid kit is located
- Participate in evacuation drills
- Follow instructions from emergency wardens
- Do not re-enter a building during an evacuation until given clearance

## Worked Example

**Prompt:**

*You work in the administration area of a small accounting firm with 12 staff. You notice that a power board under the reception desk has 8 items plugged into it, and one side of it is charred and smells slightly of burning plastic. The receptionist says "yeah, it's been like that for a while, it's fine." What do you do?*

---

**Exemplary Response:**

The charred power board is an immediate fire and electrical hazard. "It's been like that for a while" is not reassurance — it suggests the hazard has been present and unaddressed, which makes it more serious, not less.

**Immediate actions:**
I would not use the power board and would unplug devices from it if I could do so safely (without touching the charred area). I would advise the receptionist that I am going to report it and ask that no additional items be plugged in.

I would report the hazard to my supervisor immediately — verbally for speed, followed by a written hazard report as required by the organisation's procedures.

I would ask that the power board be taken out of service and replaced before the relevant workstations are used again. Depending on my organisation's procedures, this may involve calling an electrician to inspect the area.

**Why the hierarchy of controls applies here:**
- **Elimination:** Remove and dispose of the faulty power board
- **Substitution:** Replace with a properly rated power board with surge protection
- **Administrative control:** Remind staff not to overload power boards; add to the next WHS inspection checklist
- PPE is not relevant here — the hazard needs to be removed, not worked around

**Why "it's fine" is not an acceptable response:**
A charred power board indicates an electrical fault. Electrical fires typically start inside walls or wiring before any visible flame appears. This is a potentially life-threatening hazard. The appropriate response is removal from service, reporting, and inspection — not continued use.

**Examiner Annotation:** This response demonstrates correct hazard recognition, correct application of the hierarchy of controls (removing the hazard, not adding PPE or a warning sign), appropriate urgency, and correct reporting process. The student also correctly challenges the assumption that duration of a hazard means it is safe. Strong Competent.
