# 1. The Problem With the Draft As Supplied

Your draft contains this:

> *"The authors do not accept or undertake any duty of care relating to this material. The authors shall not be liable in any respect to any recipient of this material for: any loss of profit… or any consequential, special, indirect, exemplary, or punitive damages, of any nature…"*

**Three issues, in order of seriousness.**

## 1.1 It likely conflicts with the Australian Consumer Law — and saying so may itself be a breach

Under the **Australian Consumer Law** (Schedule 2, *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth)):

- Goods and services supplied to a **consumer** carry statutory **consumer guarantees** — including that services are rendered with due care and skill, and that goods and services are fit for purpose
- **Section 64 makes void any term that purports to exclude, restrict or modify those guarantees**
- Section 64A permits liability to be limited — but only for services **not of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption**, and only to specified remedies such as resupply

**Why this bites here:** a student buying a $149 assessment support pack is very likely a **consumer** under the ACL. The "$100,000 threshold or ordinarily acquired for personal use" test is comfortably met.

**And the second-order risk is the real one.** The ACCC has taken enforcement action against businesses whose terms **misrepresented consumer rights** — a blanket "we accept no liability whatsoever" can constitute **misleading conduct under s18** independently of whether the clause is enforceable. So a clause that is void may *also* be a contravention.

**The fix is standard and it is not onerous:** a carve-out stating that nothing in the disclaimer excludes the consumer guarantees, followed by the limitation expressed as applying only to the extent permitted by law. Every competent Australian commercial disclaimer has one. **[VERIFY with your solicitor: whether s64A limitation is available for your product type, and the correct form of the carve-out.]**

## 1.2 "Do not accept or undertake any duty of care" does not do what it looks like

A duty of care arises **in law**, from the relationship and the circumstances. You cannot unilaterally decline one by announcement.

What you *can* do is **limit liability contractually**, subject to the ACL. The distinction matters because a clause purporting to disclaim a duty may be read as ineffective in its entirety, whereas a properly drafted limitation clause is given effect to the extent the law allows.

## 1.3 "May be subject to copyright" understates your position

The phrasing reads as though it was adapted from an **educational institution copying notice** — the kind of warning a university attaches to material copied under a statutory licence, where "may be subject to copyright" is accurate because the institution does not own it.

**That is the opposite of your position.** You own this material. The notice should **assert** copyright, not hedge about it — particularly if you are licensing it to RTOs, where the strength of the assertion is part of what you are selling.

# 6. The Image Licensing Risk — Read Before Buying Any Stock Image

This bears directly on the co-branding model and is the most commonly missed IP trap in resource publishing.

**Standard stock image licences almost universally prohibit:**

- **Sub-licensing** — passing rights to a third party
- Use in material that is **redistributed or resold**
- Use in **templates or products intended for further distribution**

**Your co-branding model does exactly those things.** You supply material to an RTO, the RTO puts its logo on it and distributes it to its students. Depending on the licence, that can be:

1. A sub-licence you are not permitted to grant, and
2. Redistribution the licence does not cover

**The consequences are real** — stock agencies pursue this actively, and the exposure is per-image, per-use.

**The safe positions, in order of preference:**

| Approach | Risk | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Originally created diagrams and infographics** | None — you own them outright | Time |
| **Commissioned work with full assignment of copyright** | None, if the assignment is written and complete | Money |
| **Extended / enterprise licence explicitly permitting sub-licensing and redistribution** | Low, if the terms genuinely cover it | Higher |
| **Standard stock licence** | **High — likely breach in a co-branded product** | Low |
| **Web-sourced or AI-generated images of uncertain provenance** | **Unacceptable** | — |

**Recommendation: originate everything.** For technical training material, original diagrams are also *better* — a purpose-drawn hierarchy of controls or trench cross-section teaches more than a stock photograph of a person in a hard hat, and it carries no licensing exposure.
