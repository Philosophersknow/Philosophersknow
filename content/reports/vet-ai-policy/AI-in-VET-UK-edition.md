# AI in Further Education and Skills: International Comparisons and What UK Providers Need to Do Now

**A briefing for FE college leaders, training providers, EdTech advisers, and policy professionals**

---

*This report analyses how artificial intelligence is being regulated and integrated in vocational education and training (VET/FE) across twelve jurisdictions — the UK, Ireland, Finland, Canada, New Zealand, Singapore, China, Japan, the European Union, Indonesia, Australia, and the United States — and draws practical conclusions for UK further education providers, regulators, and policy bodies.*

## 1. Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is transforming vocational education and training worldwide. For UK further education colleges and independent training providers, the question is no longer whether to engage with AI — it is how to do so responsibly, confidently, and competitively.

This report compares the UK FE sector's current approach to AI against international best practice across twelve jurisdictions. The central finding is this: **the UK has strong principles but uneven implementation**. The Department for Education has issued guidance. Jisc has published a strategic framework for colleges. Ofsted has studied early adopters. Skills England is integrating AI into apprenticeship units. Yet adoption across the FE sector remains fragmented, staff confidence is the primary barrier rather than technology, and a regulatory gap exists between the expectations placed on higher education providers (via the Office for Students) and those placed on FE colleges and independent training providers (via Ofsted and the Education and Skills Funding Agency).

International comparators offer clear lessons. Ireland has moved to sector-wide AI principles with implementation timelines. Finland is training every educator in AI literacy. Singapore has embedded AI into core qualifications and built digital infrastructure across all providers. Australia's higher education regulator (TEQSA) has required every university to submit a formal AI action plan. None of these steps are beyond the reach of UK policy and practice.

The report makes two sets of practical recommendations: one for **FE college and training provider leaders** seeking to act now, and one for **UK regulatory and policy bodies** (DfE, Ofsted, Skills England, Jisc, and OfS) seeking to close the systemic gap.

The core message: **the biggest risk is doing nothing**, a conclusion Ofsted itself reached in its 2025 early-adopter study. The UK FE sector has the frameworks, the research, and the international evidence. What is needed now is confident, coordinated action.

## 2. The UK FE Context: Why AI Urgency Is Now

The UK further education and skills sector faces a convergence of pressures that make the AI question both unavoidable and time-sensitive.

**Skills England and the national skills mission.** The creation of Skills England in 2024 signals the most significant structural shift in workforce development policy in a generation. Its mandate — to align training with the UK's industrial strategy and close critical skills gaps — depends on FE providers adapting rapidly to the needs of an AI-intensive economy. In April 2026, Skills England launched updated AI leadership apprenticeship units, replacing the original Developing AI Strategy unit with three more granular components following employer feedback. The implication is direct: the qualifications infrastructure is changing to reflect AI, and FE providers must keep pace.

**T-Levels and digital readiness.** The rollout of T-Levels — including Digital Production, Design and Development — has brought new demands for up-to-date digital and AI literacy into core provision. Students studying technical routes increasingly encounter AI in workplace placements and expect their learning to reflect the tools they will use on day one of employment.

**Apprenticeship funding and employer expectations.** Employers funding apprenticeships through the Apprenticeship Levy are asking whether their providers have the AI capability to deliver relevant, work-ready training. The 2025-26 funding rules increasingly reflect the expectation that providers maintain current digital and technical expertise. Providers that cannot demonstrate AI competence risk losing employer partnerships to better-prepared competitors.

**Ofsted's positioning.** Ofsted's 2025 early-adopter study, *"The biggest risk is doing nothing"*, examined how 21 schools, colleges, and multi-academy trusts were using AI in practice. The study found that AI is already saving time on lesson planning, resource creation, assessment support, and administrative tasks — and that the providers making the most progress share one characteristic: a designated AI champion or leader who supports staff confidence and translates potential into practice. Critically, Ofsted confirmed it does not inspect AI use as a standalone category, but inspectors will note how AI is being used and whether providers are responding appropriately to a changing environment.

**The staff confidence gap.** Jisc's research makes clear that in UK colleges, the primary barrier to AI adoption is not technology — it is staff confidence. Colleges with coherent, trusted AI toolkits are seeing benefits across teaching, learning, and operations. Those without clear guidance are seeing individual staff either avoiding AI entirely or adopting it without governance, creating both missed opportunities and integrity risks.

**Academic integrity pressure.** As AI writing and reasoning tools become more capable and more accessible to students, assessment authenticity is under strain across FE provision — particularly in written components of qualifications. BTEC portfolios, written reflections in health and social care, and theory components in construction and engineering courses are all affected. Unlike higher education, where university registrars and TEQSA (in Australia) have moved quickly to update policies, many FE providers are still operating academic integrity frameworks that predate generative AI.

## 3. How the UK FE Sector Currently Stands

### What Is Working

The UK FE sector has a more developed AI landscape than is often recognised. Several elements of genuine strength exist:

**DfE Generative AI Guidance (2023).** The Department for Education issued guidance for schools and FE colleges on generative AI in 2023, establishing clear principles: protect personal data, do not feed sensitive information into public AI tools, comply with data protection and copyright law, and treat undisclosed AI-generated work as plagiarism. This guidance created a baseline that serious FE providers have built on.

**Jisc's "Leading AI in Colleges" Strategic Framework.** Jisc's framework, updated through 2025, organises AI adoption across three pillars: *skills, knowledge and culture*; *technology*; and *governance*. It is one of the most practical sector-specific resources available to FE leaders and includes maturity assessments, checklists, and practical examples. The Jisc principles for AI in FE colleges complement the framework, providing an ethical baseline for providers.

**The Ofsted Early-Adopter Study.** Ofsted's 2025 study provides sector-specific evidence of what responsible AI adoption looks like in practice. Its finding — that AI is most effectively embedded when there is a designated leader, clear governance, and staff development — gives FE SLTs a clear organisational model to follow.

**Russell Group AI Principles.** At the higher education level, the 24 Russell Group universities' five principles for generative AI (AI literacy; staff support; ethical and equitable access; academic integrity; sharing best practice) provide a benchmark that FE quality bodies and providers can adapt.

**Skills England AI Apprenticeship Units.** The availability of modular AI leadership apprenticeship units from April 2026 provides FE providers with a direct opportunity to offer relevant AI upskilling to employer partners, creating a new commercial and delivery pathway.

### What Is Missing

Despite these strengths, three gaps stand out:

**No sector-wide formal requirement.** Unlike Australia's TEQSA, which required every higher education provider to submit a formal AI action plan by July 2024, no equivalent directive has been issued to FE colleges or independent training providers. DfE guidance is advisory. Ofsted's approach is to observe rather than require. The result is that provider responses vary widely — some colleges have robust AI governance frameworks, many have none.

**No FE-specific assessment integrity standard.** The updated qualifications and standards for FE — including the current revision of apprenticeship assessment plans — do not yet systematically address how authenticity should be verified in an AI-capable environment. Assessment frameworks that were designed for a pre-generative-AI world are being applied to students who have ready access to tools that can produce plausible written, analytical, and even practical planning content.

**No unified AI maturity baseline.** There is no systematic means by which the sector, regulators, or the DfE can assess how prepared FE providers are to use AI responsibly or where the greatest capacity gaps lie. Jisc's AI maturity toolkit is available but voluntary. Without baseline data, it is difficult to target support where it is most needed or to demonstrate sector-wide progress.

## 4. International Best Practices in AI and VET Regulation

### United Kingdom (Higher Education Sector)

The UK's higher education sector has been more proactive than FE in establishing formal AI governance. The 24 Russell Group universities published five collective principles for generative AI in 2023, committing to: AI literacy for students and staff; equipping staff to guide ethical use; adapting teaching and assessment; upholding academic integrity; and sharing best practice (Russell Group, 2023).

The Quality Assurance Agency and Jisc have both issued guidance for the sector. Universities have updated academic conduct policies, convened internal AI taskforces, and moved toward varied assessment designs that reduce AI substitutability. The principle-led, pro-innovation approach — encouraging adoption under ethical guidelines rather than imposing bans — is widely regarded as a best-practice model (Weale, 2023).

The lesson for FE: the principle-based approach works when it is backed by institutional governance. Principles without governance structures remain aspirational. FE providers that have adopted the Jisc framework alongside designated AI leadership are replicating the conditions that have made the HE model effective.

### Ireland

Ireland's VET system operates through Education and Training Boards (ETBs) with over 250,000 learners in further education and training. The Irish National AI Strategy and the qualifications body QQI have both engaged with AI in education. Ireland's approach emphasises developing an *AI-literate population* and addresses the ethical, social, and economic implications of AI at a national level.

For education specifically, Ireland has promoted sector-wide AI principles that address academic integrity, equal access, and the development of AI skills as a core outcome of vocational training. Ireland's advisory bodies have stated that the importance and urgency of responding to AI in education "cannot be overstated" and have encouraged institutions to treat their AI policies as live documents — updated iteratively as practice develops, rather than set once and forgotten.

**Lesson for UK FE:** Ireland's emphasis on live, iterative policy (rather than static guidance) is a model worth following. AI capabilities and risks are changing faster than annual policy review cycles can accommodate.

### Finland

Finland is notable for its systematic approach to AI literacy at a national level. Finland's AI strategy includes a national programme — *Elements of AI* — that has reached hundreds of thousands of citizens, including educators, and teaches the fundamentals of AI and its societal implications. For vocational education, Finland has embedded digital and AI competence into national qualifications frameworks and educator training.

The Finnish approach addresses a problem the UK FE sector faces acutely: uneven staff confidence. By treating AI literacy as a systemic national priority rather than an individual college responsibility, Finland has achieved broad baseline capability across its education workforce. Local colleges are building on a shared foundation rather than starting from zero.

**Lesson for UK FE:** The Elements of AI programme (freely available online and adaptable to UK contexts) is a practical, low-cost resource for FE staff development. The Jisc National Centre for AI already supports some similar provision, but a more systematic national FE staff AI literacy programme would accelerate progress.

### Canada

Canada's VET and community college sector has addressed AI through a combination of federal AI strategy (notably the Pan-Canadian Artificial Intelligence Strategy) and institutional governance at individual colleges. The U15 Canada group of research universities published principles for AI in teaching and learning in 2024. Many community colleges have developed internal AI policies focusing on academic integrity, fair access, and assessment redesign.

Canada's challenge — a federated system with significant provincial variation — mirrors the UK's devolved governance across England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Canada's experience suggests that national principles and resources are most effective when they are accompanied by provincial-level implementation support and sector body guidance (similar to how Jisc supports UK colleges), rather than left entirely to individual institutions.

**Lesson for UK FE:** The federated/devolved governance challenge is solvable with strong sector body coordination. Jisc's role in the UK FE sector is analogous to what effective provincial education bodies do in Canada — the question is whether Jisc's frameworks are resourced and mandated at a level that ensures uptake, not just availability.

### New Zealand

New Zealand's Tertiary Education Commission (TEC) and the New Zealand Qualifications Authority (NZQA) have both engaged with AI in education. NZQA updated its assessment integrity guidance in response to generative AI, explicitly addressing AI-assisted cheating and clarifying that academic work submitted for NZQA qualifications must represent the student's own learning. New Zealand's approach reflects its small, integrated tertiary sector — the same regulator oversees vocational and academic qualifications — which allows more consistent policy application than in jurisdictions with separate VET and HE regulators.

**Lesson for UK FE:** New Zealand's integrated regulatory approach (one qualifications authority spanning VET and HE) reduces the gap between sectors. The UK's divided landscape — with Ofsted, OfS, DfE, and Skills England each covering parts of the system — requires deliberate coordination to achieve the same consistency.

### Singapore

Singapore represents arguably the most deliberate and infrastructure-backed approach to AI in education anywhere in the world. The Ministry of Education's AI strategy explicitly covers vocational and technical education through the Institute of Technical Education (ITE) and the polytechnics. AI competencies have been embedded into national curricula. Teachers receive structured professional development in AI. The SkillsFuture initiative provides a national framework for lifelong learning that explicitly addresses AI upskilling for the existing workforce as well as initial qualification.

Singapore's approach is notable for addressing equity directly: AI tools and digital infrastructure are provided systematically across all educational institutions, not left to individual providers to source, meaning the digital divide between well-resourced and under-resourced colleges does not widen under AI adoption.

**Lesson for UK FE:** The equity question — whether smaller and less well-resourced FE providers can access the same AI infrastructure as well-funded colleges — is a significant risk in the UK system. A national approach to AI tool procurement or licensing, coordinated through bodies like Jisc or the ESFA, would reduce this gap.

### China

China has pursued a state-directed approach to AI in education, mandating AI and computational thinking across school curricula and integrating AI content into vocational and technical qualifications at the national level. China's vocational education system, serving tens of millions of learners, has been required to update curricula to include AI literacy as a standard component.

China's approach is notable for its speed and scale of implementation, though this comes with significant top-down control over content and limited space for educator autonomy or student-led inquiry. For Western education systems, the content of China's AI curricula is less transferable than the *institutional model* of treating AI literacy as a core outcome of vocational qualification, not an optional add-on.

**Lesson for UK FE:** AI literacy should be treated as a core component of vocational outcomes — embedded in qualifications standards — not as enrichment activity dependent on individual college enthusiasm.

### Japan

Japan has developed AI guidelines for education through the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT). Guidelines released from 2023 onward address the use of generative AI in schools and tertiary education, with a focus on appropriate use, verification of student work, and the development of students' own thinking and reasoning — not mere reliance on AI outputs.

Japan's distinctive contribution to the international debate is the emphasis on **cognitive development**: the concern that excessive reliance on AI tools, particularly for written and analytical tasks, may impair students' development of independent thinking, reasoning, and communication skills. Japan's guidelines therefore encourage educators to design learning experiences where AI augments but does not replace the cognitive work of students.

**Lesson for UK FE:** This is a live debate in UK FE, particularly in programmes where written reflection is central to demonstrating competence (health and social care, education and childcare, professional service occupations). Japan's framework offers a principled response: design for AI-augmented, not AI-substituted, learning.

### European Union

The EU AI Act, in force from 2024, is the world's first legally binding AI regulation and has direct implications for any AI tool used in UK educational settings — including AI-powered learning platforms, AI detection tools, assessment software, and student monitoring systems — where those tools are supplied by EU-based companies or process data of EU citizens.

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems used in education in ways that affect both compliance requirements and data rights. AI systems that determine access to educational programmes or assess student competency are classified as high-risk, requiring conformity assessments, transparency documentation, and human oversight. AI systems that could manipulate student behaviour or exploit vulnerabilities (including psychological profiling without consent) are prohibited.

For UK FE providers using AI tools from EU-based EdTech suppliers, or planning to use AI in assessment-related functions, the EU framework creates practical compliance requirements regardless of whether the UK adopts equivalent domestic regulation.

The EU's Digital Education Action Plan (2021-2027) also directly addresses vocational and further education, with specific work on AI in assessment, digital infrastructure for education, and AI literacy for educators.

**Lesson for UK FE:** EdTech procurement decisions need to include AI Act compliance as a criterion. Providers relying on EU-supplied platforms for learning management, assessment, or student analytics should be requesting supplier documentation of their AI Act conformity.

### Indonesia

Indonesia's approach to AI in education is shaped by the UNESCO-KOMINFO AI Readiness Assessment, which identified AI literacy as a national development priority. Indonesian vocational education institutions (SMK) have been included in national AI awareness programmes. Indonesia's experience is instructive as a rapidly developing system navigating the tension between rapid AI adoption — driven by economic opportunity — and the infrastructure and governance constraints that make responsible adoption difficult.

**Lesson for UK FE:** The Indonesian case illustrates the cost of under-resourcing AI governance in education. Providers that adopt AI tools without adequate governance frameworks face compounding risks: integrity breaches, data exposure, and loss of trust from employers and learners.

### Australia

Australia provides the closest parallel to the UK's challenge because it faces the same structural issue: a divided regulatory landscape between vocational education (regulated by the Australian Skills Quality Authority, ASQA) and higher education (regulated by the Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency, TEQSA), creating uneven standards and uneven provider responses.

TEQSA's approach has been the more proactive. By June 2024, TEQSA required every registered higher education provider to submit a formal AI action plan to a defined timeline. Universities were required to develop institutional governance responses, update academic integrity policies, and demonstrate how they were addressing AI's impact on assessment validity. TEQSA supported this directive with practical resources, including good-practice guides on assessment reform and an AI resource hub.

ASQA's response has been slower and more advisory: newsletter guidance on academic integrity risks, recommendations to redesign assessments, and — from April 2025 — a broader acknowledgement of AI's positive potential for VET delivery. But as of mid-2026, ASQA has not issued a comparable formal directive to registered training organisations.

The result is a two-tier sector: universities with formal AI governance frameworks and RTOs with widely varying responses. Australia's experience demonstrates that principles and advisory guidance, without formal requirements, produce inconsistent outcomes across a diverse provider landscape.

**Lesson for UK FE:** The UK FE sector risks the same two-tier outcome. Without a formal regulatory signal from DfE, Ofsted, or Skills England requiring FE providers to develop AI governance frameworks, adoption will remain uneven and the gap between the best-prepared and least-prepared providers will widen.

## 5. The UK FE Regulatory Gap: Ofsted, DfE, Skills England, and OfS

Understanding the current UK regulatory landscape for AI in FE requires examining four separate bodies and their roles.

### Department for Education

The DfE issued generative AI guidance for schools and FE colleges in 2023, covering data privacy, intellectual property, academic integrity, and ethical use. In 2025-26, the DfE has continued to develop its AI in education programme, including the AI in Education content store and the commissioned Ofsted early-adopter study. The DfE's AI roadmap for education signals intent but, as of mid-2026, has not translated into formal requirements on FE providers.

The gap: DfE guidance is advisory. There is no mechanism by which the DfE requires FE providers to implement AI governance, demonstrate AI readiness, or update assessment integrity frameworks to address generative AI. Providers are expected to respond professionally to guidance, but there is no accountability mechanism.

### Ofsted

Ofsted does not inspect AI as a standalone domain and does not require providers to be using AI tools. However, Ofsted's 2025 early-adopter study makes clear that inspectors will note how AI is being used and whether providers are responding appropriately to the changing environment. The study's finding — "the biggest risk is doing nothing" — is an implicit signal that passive inaction will be read negatively in the quality framework, even if no explicit AI standard exists.

Ofsted's current approach places the burden of leadership on individual providers. Colleges with strong AI governance may benefit indirectly from inspection evidence of innovation in teaching and learning; those with no AI response may find their quality of education judgements affected by outdated assessment design and limited staff development.

The gap: There is no clear accountability signal from Ofsted about AI readiness. A provider that has done nothing to address AI since 2023 is not in formal regulatory jeopardy under current inspection frameworks. This reduces the urgency signal to SLTs.

### Skills England

Skills England's role is growing rapidly, with significant implications for FE. The publication of updated AI leadership apprenticeship units in April 2026 — following employer feedback and Skills England-led engagement — demonstrates that the qualifications infrastructure is actively adapting to AI. Skills England's Assessment of Priority Skills to 2030 explicitly includes AI-related competencies in the national skills picture.

However, Skills England's mandate is primarily curriculum and qualification design, not provider quality assurance. It shapes what qualifications require; it does not inspect whether colleges are delivering those qualifications with appropriate AI competence.

The gap: Skills England is changing what needs to be taught without a corresponding mechanism to ensure FE providers are equipped to teach it. A qualification that includes AI competencies is only meaningful if the educators delivering it are AI-literate themselves.

### Office for Students (OfS)

The OfS regulates English higher education providers and has taken AI seriously in the context of academic integrity, access and participation, and quality assurance. OfS conditions of registration require providers to demonstrate effective academic integrity frameworks, and AI-facilitated academic misconduct has become a live enforcement risk for universities.

The gap: OfS oversight does not extend to FE colleges or independent training providers. There is no FE equivalent of OfS's conditions of registration. This means the regulatory accountability that drives HE providers to maintain robust academic integrity frameworks simply does not apply in the same way to Level 3-5 provision delivered outside universities.

### The Systemic Gap

Taken together, the four bodies that shape UK FE quality — DfE, Ofsted, Skills England, and OfS — have each made some contribution to AI governance in education, but none has created the accountability mechanism that would compel consistent provider-level action. The closest equivalent to Australia's TEQSA directive, which required every provider to submit a formal AI action plan, does not exist in UK FE.

This gap matters not because providers are acting irresponsibly, but because **voluntary responses produce voluntary distribution**. The providers that engage most seriously with Jisc's frameworks, DfE guidance, and the Ofsted early-adopter study are disproportionately the larger, better-resourced colleges with designated quality teams. Smaller general FE colleges, specialist providers, and many independent training providers are working without adequate governance frameworks because there is no formal requirement to develop one.

## 6. Recommendations for UK FE Providers

These recommendations are for FE college senior leadership teams, curriculum and quality managers, and independent training provider directors.

### 6.1 Appoint an AI Lead

The single strongest predictor of effective AI adoption in the Ofsted early-adopter study was the presence of a designated staff member leading AI integration — someone who supports others, builds enthusiasm, and translates potential into practice. This does not require a new hire. It requires identifying an AI-curious, digitally confident staff member with sufficient seniority and protected time to lead the work.

### 6.2 Develop a Formal AI Governance Framework

Use the Jisc "Leading AI in Colleges" strategic framework as the foundation. Structure your framework across the three pillars: *skills, knowledge and culture* (what your staff and students know and can do with AI); *technology* (what AI tools you use, how they are governed, how vendor compliance with the EU AI Act is verified); and *governance* (policy, accountability, risk management, and review cycles).

A formal framework does not need to be long. A ten-page document covering scope, principles, approved tools, academic integrity standards, staff development expectations, and a review cycle is sufficient for most providers and demonstrates to Ofsted, employers, and awarding bodies that AI is being addressed thoughtfully.

### 6.3 Update Academic Integrity Policies Immediately

Your current academic integrity policy almost certainly predates generative AI. Update it to: explicitly define the use of AI-generated content without disclosure as academic misconduct; clarify the permitted uses of AI in different contexts (research, drafting, editing, translation); set out how authenticity will be verified; and establish a consistent set of consequences for misuse.

Communicate the updated policy to all staff and students at the start of each academic year. Ensure that assessors receive specific guidance on how to respond if they suspect AI-generated submission in competency-based evidence portfolios.

### 6.4 Redesign High-Risk Assessments

Identify the assessments in your curriculum most vulnerable to AI substitution — typically written reflections, case studies, reports, and analytical essays without a supervised component. Develop a planned programme of assessment redesign, prioritising the highest-risk areas first. Redesign options include: increasing the proportion of observed, practical, and oral components; adding a short structured discussion or verification interview alongside written submissions; introducing staged submission processes that make wholesale AI generation less feasible; and contextualising written tasks with learner-specific information (workplace details, specific placement experiences) that AI tools cannot reliably fabricate.

### 6.5 Invest in Staff AI Literacy

Staff confidence, not technology, is the primary barrier. Create a structured staff development programme using freely available resources (including the Elements of AI course, Jisc's National Centre for AI resources, and the DfE AI in Education content store) supplemented by practical workshops tailored to your curriculum areas.

Set a minimum expectation for all teaching and assessment staff: by a defined date, every member of the teaching staff should be able to describe how generative AI works at a basic level, identify its primary academic integrity risks, and know what the college's AI policy requires of them. This is a reasonable, achievable baseline — not an advanced AI qualification.

### 6.6 Build AI Competence Into Employer Engagement

Many employers funding apprenticeships and work-based learning are themselves uncertain about AI governance and want support from their provider partners. Position your college's AI readiness as a commercial differentiator. Offer employers briefing on how your programmes address AI literacy; discuss how AI tools being used in their workplaces can be incorporated into training delivery; and use AI leadership apprenticeship units as a vehicle for direct employer engagement on workforce AI upskilling.

### 6.7 Participate in Sector Networks

Connect with Jisc's National Centre for AI, the Association of Colleges working groups on digital and AI, and Ufi VocTech Trust programmes. The intelligence and resources available through these networks reduce the cost of developing internal expertise and ensure your governance frameworks reflect current best practice.

## 7. Recommendations for UK Regulators and Policy Bodies

These recommendations are for DfE, Ofsted, Skills England, Jisc, and OfS.

### 7.1 DfE: Introduce a Formal AI Readiness Requirement

Issue a formal requirement — modelled on TEQSA's 2024 Request for Information — that all FE providers registered with the Education and Skills Funding Agency must demonstrate, by a defined date, that they have an AI governance framework in place. This framework should cover academic integrity, staff AI literacy, approved tools, and assessment integrity.

The requirement does not need to be prescriptive. A short self-declaration, against a published standard, would be sufficient as a first step. It would create the accountability signal that advisory guidance cannot.

### 7.2 Ofsted: Include AI Governance in Quality Framework Signals

Develop clear published signals within the FE and Skills inspection framework about what inspectors expect to see in relation to AI governance. This does not require a new inspection category. It can be addressed within existing quality of education, leadership and management, and professional development judgements. Publishing a clear set of indicators — equivalent to the Jisc maturity framework but translated into inspection language — would give providers a concrete target and give inspectors consistent criteria.

### 7.3 Skills England: Mandate AI Literacy Baseline for Educators

Include a minimum AI literacy standard in the educator training and development frameworks that Skills England is developing as part of its workforce development mandate. Any educator delivering AI-related apprenticeship units should meet a defined minimum AI literacy threshold. This should be underpinned by a national professional development offer, coordinated through Jisc and available to all FE staff.

### 7.4 Jisc: Move from Framework to Verified Implementation

Jisc's strategic frameworks for AI in colleges are excellent resources. The next step is to move from resource provision to verified implementation support. Introduce an AI maturity baseline survey — mandatory for all FE members — that enables Jisc, sector bodies, and DfE to understand where the greatest capacity gaps lie and to target support accordingly. Publish aggregated findings annually so the sector can track its own progress.

### 7.5 OfS and DfE: Coordinate Across the HE/FE Boundary

As dual-qualification programmes, degree apprenticeships, and Level 4-5 technical qualifications grow, the boundary between HE and FE provision is increasingly porous. Develop a joint DfE-OfS protocol for AI governance in borderline provision, ensuring that learners in the same qualification framework are protected by equivalent integrity standards regardless of whether their provider is an FE college or a university.

## 8. Future Directions and Conclusion

AI will not stabilise. The tools available to FE learners and educators in 2027 will be more capable, more accessible, and more contextually integrated than those available today. Policy and governance frameworks built for a single-generation AI landscape will require continuous revision.

The international evidence is clear about what works. Countries and systems that are managing AI in VET effectively share three characteristics: they treat AI governance as an institutional responsibility requiring active leadership, not just policy; they invest in educator capability as the foundational enabler; and they ensure regulatory accountability creates consistent expectations across provider types and sizes.

The UK FE sector has the raw ingredients. The Jisc frameworks, the DfE guidance, the Ofsted early-adopter evidence, the Skills England qualification infrastructure — these are serious, substantive contributions. The gap is not in resources or intelligence. It is in the conversion of available guidance into consistent, accountable practice across 200 general FE colleges and thousands of independent training providers.

Closing that gap is achievable. Other jurisdictions have done it. Australia's TEQSA showed that a formal directive, with practical support, can move an entire sector from reactive awareness to active governance within a single academic year. Ireland showed that principle-led frameworks with implementation timelines produce faster and more consistent uptake than indefinitely advisory guidance. Finland showed that investing in educator AI literacy as a systemic national priority — not an individual college responsibility — produces sector-wide capability rather than pockets of excellence.

For UK FE college leaders, the practical message is this: do not wait for a formal regulatory requirement before developing your AI governance framework. The providers that will be best positioned for Ofsted inspection, employer competition, and learner expectation in 2027 are the ones building that capability now. The biggest risk is doing nothing. The second biggest risk is starting too late.

For UK policy bodies, the message is complementary: voluntary frameworks, however excellent, produce voluntary distribution. The UK FE sector is too large and too diverse to rely on professional enthusiasm alone. A formal accountability signal — proportionate, practical, and backed by real support — will move the whole sector, not just the already-engaged.

The international comparisons in this report are not cautionary tales about other systems. They are working models. The UK has every reason, and every resource, to adapt them.
