# AI Policy in VET and Regulatory Oversight: Global Comparisons and ASQA/TEQSA Review

## Regulating AI in Vocational Education: International Comparisons and Recommendations for Australia

# Executive Summary

The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) in education and training is forcing regulators worldwide to respond urgently. This report compares how Australia's vocational education regulator, the Australian Skills Quality Authority (ASQA), is addressing AI in the Vocational Education and Training (VET) sector against international best practices. It finds that while other jurisdictions, including the UK, Ireland, Finland, Canada, New Zealand, Singapore, China, Japan, the European Union, and Indonesia, have initiated proactive, comprehensive strategies to integrate AI ethically and effectively into education, Australia's response has lagged. ASQA's efforts to date have focused mainly on the risks of AI-facilitated cheating, with emerging guidance on incorporating AI in assessments, but lacks the breadth of many overseas approaches. By contrast, the Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA), which regulates higher education, has been more active, for example, requiring universities to develop action plans for managing generative AI. This discrepancy suggests a need for stronger collaboration between ASQA and TEQSA. The report recommends a coordinated national approach that leverages international lessons: develops clear guidelines and principles for AI use in VET, invests in AI literacy and training for educators, ensure equity and data privacy and adapt quality standards to uphold integrity without stifling innovation. Urgent action is needed for Australia to catch up to global best practice so that AI benefits in improved learning personalization, administrative efficiency and workforce readiness, can be realised in the VET sector while safeguarding educational quality and public trust.

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence is transforming education and training worldwide, bringing both immense opportunities and significant challenges. In the vocational education sector, AI tools can personalize learning, automate administrative tasks, and help prepare students for an AI-driven workforce. At the same time, AI raises concerns about academic integrity (e.g. students using AI to cheat on assessments), data privacy and the potential to exacerbate inequalities. Many countries have recognized that regulating and guiding the use of AI in education is now an urgent policy priority. Australia, however, has been comparatively slow in formulating a clear regulatory response. Australia's national VET regulator, ASQA, is only beginning to address AI issues, primarily in the context of academic cheating and there is a growing sense that Australia risks falling behind international best practice. This report provides an updated analysis of how ASQA's approach compares with leading international examples and outlines steps Australia can take to strengthen its regulatory framework for AI in the VET sector including coordination between ASQA and the higher education regulator TEQSA. The report is addressed to government policymakers, regulators, compliance consultants and professional associations, with a focus on solutions that balance innovation with integrity.

## International Best Practices in AI and Education Regulation

Global regulators and policymakers have approached the rise of AI in education with proactive strategies. This section critically compares how key jurisdictions are responding, highlighting best practices that could inform Australia's approach.

# United Kingdom

## Introduction

The UK's VET sector, known as Further Education (FE) and Skills, is broad and diverse, comprising both public colleges and a significant number of private training providers. The system serves over 2 million learners annually, with a focus on qualifications such as BTECs, NVQs, T-Levels, and Apprenticeships. While the UK has moved away from strict competency-based frameworks like Australia's, practical skills assessment and employer input remain central, particularly in apprenticeships. Governance is devolved, with England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland each operating their own systems.

## AI Policy and Regulation Approach 

The UK has adopted a principle-based, collaborative approach to integrating AI in education. Rather than banning AI tools, UK authorities emphasize **safe and responsible use** guided by clear principles. In the schools and further education (FE) sector, the Department for Education (DfE) issued guidance on generative AI in 2023, focusing on data privacy, intellectual property and student safety. For example, schools and colleges are advised that any personal data should be protected and generally not input into AI tools and that AI use must comply with data protection and copyright laws (DfE, 2023). The guidance also makes clear that passing off AI-generated work as one's own will be treated as plagiarism or cheating and urges schools to educate students on the ethical use of AI.

At the tertiary level, all 24 Russell Group universities (the UK's leading research universities) collectively developed **five guiding principles** for generative AI in education in mid-2023. These principles commit universities to:

(1) support students and staff to become AI-literate

(2) equip staff to help students use AI tools effectively and appropriately

(3) adapt teaching and assessment to incorporate the ethical use of AI while ensuring **equal access** to these tools

(4) uphold academic integrity and rigor and

(5) share best practices as the technology evolves (Russell Group, 2023).

In line with these principles, UK universities have updated academic conduct policies to clarify where AI use is inappropriate and how it should be acknowledged when permitted. There is a strong focus on **faculty training**, ensuring staff can guide students in using AI and redesign assessments to minimize cheating risks, while not reverting solely to "AI-proof" traditional exams at the expense of authentic skill evaluation. Overall, the UK's approach is characterized by **pro-innovation regulation**: encouraging adoption of AI for educational benefit (enhancing teaching and learning) under robust ethical guidelines, rather than heavy-handed restrictions. This collaborative, principle-led strategy, involving government, universities and quality bodies (e.g. the Quality Assurance Agency and Jisc), is widely seen as a best practice model combining innovation with integrity (Sally Weale, 2023).

# Ireland

## Introduction

Ireland's VET system is delivered through Education and Training Boards (ETBs), independent private colleges and apprenticeship programs. The sector has grown significantly, with over 250,000 learners in further education and training (FET). Ireland integrates competency-based elements in its National Framework of Qualifications but allows more holistic assessment practices. Trainers are usually dual-qualified and industry-experienced. Curriculum is centrally developed through QQI, with strong consultation processes involving industry.

## AI Regulatory Approach

Ireland has recently formulated a comprehensive strategy to manage AI in education, reflecting a **thoughtful and measured approach**. In early 2025, Ireland's AI Advisory Council, a government-appointed expert body, released guidelines for generative AI in education (Irish AI Advisory Council, 2025). These guidelines explicitly acknowledge the **rapid pace of AI development and its disruptive impact** on traditional teaching and assessment, urging that "we cannot stop these technology developments...instead we should ensure AI is used properly, ethically and only when benefits outweigh costs" (AI Advisory Council, 2025). The Irish guidance rejects futile bans and instead focuses on managing AI's integration across primary, secondary and tertiary education. A key concern identified is the "full spectrum" of inconsistent approaches in the absence of top-down policy, with some institutions banning AI and others innovating, which creates confusion for educators and students. To address this, the Council calls for leadership and coherent policy.

**Core principles** endorsed by Ireland's AI Advisory Council include:

(1) AI tools used by students should be **secure, private, and free** for all to ensure equitable access;

(2) data generated from AI use in education should not be used to train AI models (preventing data leaks and protecting privacy); and

(3) AI tools should be **inclusive by design**, accessible to all learners and instructors (AI Advisory Council, 2025)

These principles mirror global trends emphasizing equity and data protection. The Council's report also makes **pragmatic recommendations**: develop coordinated, consistent AI guidelines for all education levels that remain "live" documents updated as technology evolves; they provide AI literacy training for educators across all sectors; ensure equitable access by addressing cost and language barriers (notably Ireland's concern for Irish-language support); and convene a national conversation among stakeholders once guidelines are in place to maintain a stable, adaptive approach (AI Advisory Council, 2025). Significantly, Ireland highlights that detection-based responses to AI-assisted cheating "do not and will not work," and thus shifts focus to **assessment redesign and critical thinking** for teaching students how to use AI constructively and evaluate its outputs, rather than relying on trying to catch misuse. Ireland's approach stands out for its strong emphasis on **ethical use, educator upskilling and inclusivity** and for explicitly planning mechanisms to keep policies updated in the face of fast-changing AI capabilities. This measured yet urgent strategy provides a valuable example of balancing innovation with caution.

# Finland

## Introduction

Finland's vocational education system is deeply integrated with its secondary education and lifelong learning strategy. Around 45% of upper secondary students choose VET pathways. The system is highly personalized and competency-based, with flexible curricula tailored to learner needs. Trainers are required to have both pedagogical and industry qualifications. Industry plays a formal role in curriculum development through tripartite bodies.

## Regulatory Approach

Finland has taken a proactive, holistic stance on AI in education, aligning with its broader national commitment to digital innovation. In 2025, the Finnish National Agency for Education, together with the Ministry of Education and Culture, announced it will publish **comprehensive guidelines for AI use** across all educational sectors -- from early childhood and schools to vocational education and training (VET) and adult education (Finnish National Agency for Education, 2025). The aim is to support educators and training providers in **using AI as part of teaching and learning**, underpinned by Finland's values of transparency, human-centric design, and equity. These forthcoming guidelines are being developed collaboratively with experts, researchers, and stakeholders, ensuring buy-in from the education community.

Early indications of Finland's approach show a focus on **ethical AI integration**. For example, Finland's national AI program (AuroraAI) emphasizes that AI systems in public services (including education) must have transparency, accountability, and inclusivity built in (Moraitis, 2025). In educational settings, this translates to expectations that AI-driven tools explain their recommendations in understandable language and that if AI is used for tasks like grading, processes are in place (such as human oversight and published fairness reports) to maintain accountability (Moraitis, 2025). Finnish universities and institutions are already adopting policies to ensure AI "enhances, not replaces, critical thinking", for instance, teachers using generative AI like ChatGPT must undergo ethical review processes to confirm the tool is used to supplement (not substitute) student learning (University of Oulu policy, cited in Moraitis, 2025). Finland also strongly prioritizes **teacher training and curriculum innovation** to leverage AI. There is an understanding that educators need support to implement AI in pedagogy and that curriculum reforms should embed AI literacy and skills across subjects. Finland appears to be setting a global benchmark through **comprehensive guidelines that marry innovation with ethics**, ensuring AI adoption in education is systematically supported by training, robust governance and a commitment to equality of access (Finnish National Agency for Education, 2025). This anticipatory approach positions Finland as a pioneer in harnessing AI for learning while safeguarding educational values.

# Canada

## Introduction

Canada's VET system, often referred to as "career and technical education," is governed at the provincial and territorial level, resulting in diverse systems across the country. Colleges, polytechnics, and CEGEPs (in Québec) provide vocational training to over 700,000 learners annually. While elements of competency-based learning exist (particularly in apprenticeships), there is less national standardisation than in Australia. Qualifications for instructors vary by province but typically include a relevant post-secondary degree and industry experience. Industry has strong advisory influence, particularly in applied programs and apprenticeships.

## Regulatory Approach

In Canada, education is primarily a provincial responsibility, but there has been a notable pan-Canadian dialogue on AI in education in the past two years. In June 2024, Canada's provincial and territorial ministers of education jointly discussed AI's potential and risks at the 112th Council of Ministers of Education, Canada (CMEC) meeting. They shared actions taken in each province and agreed to **exchange expertise through CMEC** to ensure that rapid advances in AI are harnessed for better learning outcomes (CMEC, 2024). This high-level commitment reflects a recognition that *cooperative efforts* are needed so that AI can be integrated to improve teaching and learning while addressing challenges consistently across jurisdictions. Ministers particularly emphasized the need to teach **digital citizenship and critical thinking** in the era of AI, ensuring students can responsibly evaluate information (including AI-generated content) and understand ethical issues. This indicates that Canadian policymakers view strengthening students' critical digital skills as a key part of the AI response.

At the post-secondary level, Canadian higher education institutions have begun taking their own initiatives. A leading example is the collective action by the U15 Group of Canadian Research Universities. In September 2024, U15 released guidance on the use of generative AI in teaching and learning, developed by provosts across the universities. The U15 paper outlines a set of **values, principles and best practices** to help faculty and students use AI tools responsibly and effectively, while protecting academic integrity (U15, 2024). It affirms the opportunities AI presents (such as new ways to enhance learning) but also the need for safeguards and transparency in usage. The guidance is to be regularly reviewed and updated as best practices evolve, underscoring an understanding that policy must keep pace with technology. Many Canadian universities, following this lead, have updated their academic integrity policies in 2023--2024 to explicitly address AI, often prohibiting undisclosed use of AI for assignments but allowing it with proper attribution in certain contexts and encouraging faculty to redesign assessments (Eaton et al., 2024). Meanwhile, Canadian teaching and faculty development networks (e.g. Teaching and Learning Centres) have proliferated resources on adapting to AI (such as how to detect AI-assisted work, or how to incorporate AI in assignments to teach AI literacy).

Another significant voice has been the Canadian Teachers' Federation, which in 2023 called on governments to take urgent action to regulate AI use in K-12 education (CTF/FCE, 2023), reflecting educator concerns about everything from data privacy to the implications of students using AI chatbots for homework. In response, federal and provincial authorities have started work on ethical guidelines. Although Canada does not yet have a single unified policy on AI in education, the **emerging best practice** is collaborative and iterative: cross-provincial sharing of strategies, sector-led guidelines (like U15's principles), and an emphasis on preparing educators and updating curricula. The **gap for Canada** remains turning discussion into formal coordinated policy; however, the steps taken so far demonstrate a commitment to addressing AI proactively. Australia can draw from Canada's experience by fostering pan-sector forums for sharing AI strategies and by encouraging both bottom-up (institution-driven) and top-down policy development in tandem.

# New Zealand

## Introduction

New Zealand has a unified qualifications system underpinned by the New Zealand Qualifications Framework (NZQF), with around 200,000 learners in vocational education. The sector has undergone reform, with the establishment of Te Pūkenga (the New Zealand Institute of Skills and Technology) consolidating 16 Institutes of Technology and Polytechnics (ITPs) into a single national entity. Competency-based assessment is standard, particularly in trades. Trainers are expected to hold both pedagogical and vocational qualifications. Industry is deeply embedded in curriculum development through Workforce Development Councils (WDCs).

## Regulatory Approach

New Zealand's response to AI in the education and training context has been pragmatic, centred on **maintaining academic integrity and updating guidance for assessments**. The New Zealand Qualifications Authority (NZQA), which oversees secondary school and tertiary qualifications, moved quickly in 2023 to issue guidelines on the acceptable use of AI in assessments. Schools that administer NCEA (National Certificate of Educational Achievement) assessments are *required* to have an "authenticity policy" that explicitly covers the use of generative AI by students (NZQA, 2023). The Ministry of Education's guidance for schools reinforces that **valid assessment evidence must be the student's own work**, if a student submits AI-generated material as if it were their work, it renders the assessment invalid (NZ Ministry of Education, 2023). In practice, AI tools are treated similarly to an external "assistant": permitted only to the extent that their use is openly acknowledged and allowed by the assessment rules and strictly disallowed in many formal assessment contexts. For example, NZQA has made it clear that in high-stakes external assessments (exams), the use of generative AI is **not permitted** and for internal assessments, any use of AI must comply with the assessment's conditions and the teacher must be confident the work is the student's own understanding.

In tandem with restrictions, New Zealand is providing **guidance and resources** to help educators manage AI. NZQA has published resources on academic integrity in the age of AI (NZQA, 2023) which advise tertiary providers on updating their academic integrity policies, using tools like plagiarism and AI detectors judiciously and redesigning assessments to include more oral exams, vivas (via voice assessment) and project work where appropriate (similar to approaches being discussed in Australia). The **emphasis is on authenticity and verification**, encouraging assessors to require students to explain their reasoning/process, which makes cheating via AI more difficult. Additionally, the Ministry of Education has highlighted that teachers and schools should incorporate discussions on the role and limits of AI into learning, thereby improving students' digital literacy. Importantly, the guidance suggests that completely banning AI in learning is not realistic; instead, the focus is on teaching students and teachers how to use AI tools ethically and effectively. New Zealand's approach also underscores **policy agility**: the education authorities are monitoring the AI landscape and prepared to refine rules as needed. By mandating that every school and tertiary provider address AI usage in their policies, New Zealand ensures a baseline of awareness and control, which is a key lesson for regulators like ASQA. Flexibility (for institutions to set specific rules) combined with central expectations (e.g. AI not allowed in exams, required academic integrity measures) characterizes New Zealand's stance as a balanced approach to uphold integrity without stifling innovation in using AI for learning.

Japan's strategy can be described as **"limited adoption under supervision."** By only allowing controlled use initially and designating pilot schools, MEXT can develop effective practices and identify pitfalls before scaling up. On the regulatory side beyond schooling, Japan is also considering guidelines for higher education and vocational training. Some Japanese universities have started to issue their own rules on AI usage in coursework, aligned with MEXT's stance.

For Australia, Japan's example underscores the importance of **having clear rules on AI-related misconduct** (cheating) from the beginning and the value of pilot programs to gather evidence. It also highlights a balanced perspective: Japan is neither rushing uncritically into AI nor clinging to blanket bans; instead, it is feeling its way forward with an emphasis on ethics, evaluation and continuous improvement of guidelines. This incremental yet principled approach may be well-suited for adaptation in Australian VET, where a period of experimentation (with ASQA oversight) could inform longer-term standards. However, it also comes with the same risk of failing to act quickly and allowing AI to be implemented inconsistently, cause potential damage and fail to prepare students for the inevitable.

# European Union

## Introduction

Indonesia's vocational education sector serves over 14 million students and is a major focus for national skills development. The system includes vocational high schools (SMKs), community academies, and polytechnics. Competency-based training is embedded, aligned with national occupational standards. However, challenges remain around consistency, infrastructure, and trainer qualifications. Industry involvement is formalised but limited in practice outside urban centres.

## Regulatory Approach

The European Union provides a strong example of a **comprehensive policy framework** for AI in education, combining ethical guidelines with binding regulations. On the soft-law side, the European Commission in 2022 published *Ethical Guidelines on the Use of AI and Data in Teaching and Learning for Educators* (European Commission, 2022). These guidelines are aimed at raising awareness among teachers (especially in primary and secondary education) about AI's potential and pitfalls. They address common misconceptions that can cause anxiety about AI and offer practical advice for educators on how to integrate AI and data-driven tools into their teaching in an ethical, human-centric way. Key themes include ensuring AI is used to support, not replace, teacher judgment, maintaining transparency with students when AI is used (e.g. informing students if an AI system is helping to personalize their learning) and safeguarding privacy by handling student data carefully.

The guidelines also stress the emerging competencies teachers and students need:

-   *AI literacy* (a basic understanding of how AI works and its societal impacts), critical thinking about AI outputs and adherence to principles like fairness and non-discrimination when using AI tools. These EU ethical guidelines serve as a reference for member states and have influenced national policies (Finland's forthcoming guidelines explicitly reference the EU guidelines, for instance).

On the hard-law side, the EU is in the final stages of enacting the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act**, a landmark regulatory framework for AI across all sectors. Under the AI Act's risk-based classification, many **educational AI systems are considered "high-risk"** applications (European Union, 2024).

Specifically, these include:

-   AI systems intended to be used for making decisions about individuals in education or training;

-   AI that grades exams or evaluates students for admission or certification, will be categorized as high-risk.

This means that when the AI Act comes into force (expected in 2025--2026), such systems must meet stringent requirements before they can be deployed.

**These requirements include:**

-   rigorous risk assessments and mitigation plans,

-   high standards of accuracy, robustness and cyber security,

-   human oversight mechanisms,

-   transparency obligations (users must be informed that AI is being used and understand its logic/output); and accountability provisions (including post-market monitoring).

For education providers and EdTech companies in the EU, this will effectively mandate quality assurance on AI tools akin to how medical devices are regulated and it's a significant step to ensure **trustworthy AI** in classrooms. The AI Act also prohibits certain AI practices outright if deemed too harmful (though those tend not to be education-related, focusing more on things like social scoring or mass surveillance).

## Prohibited AI Practices Under the EU AI Act and Implications for Global Social Media Platforms

The European Union\'s Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act), formally adopted in 2024 and entering into force in stages through 2025--2026, establishes a risk-based framework for the regulation of artificial intelligence technologies across member states. Article 5 of the Act outlines a category of "unacceptable risk" AI practices that are prohibited outright, irrespective of sector or origin, due to their potential to violate fundamental rights and democratic values (European Parliament, 2024).

### Key Prohibited Practices

The following practices are explicitly banned under the Act:

1.  **Social Scoring Systems**

> AI systems that assign individuals a trustworthiness or behavioural score based on their personal characteristics, social behaviours, or predicted future actions, particularly when used to restrict access to services or opportunities. This directly targets systems similar to China's social credit model and aims to prevent discriminatory and unjustified treatment (European Commission, 2024a).

2.  **Manipulative and Deceptive Techniques**

> AI systems that use subliminal, manipulative, or exploitative tactics to distort a person's behaviour in ways likely to cause harm. This includes so-called "dark pattern" AI interfaces designed to nudge users unknowingly (ArtificialIntelligenceAct.eu, 2024).

3.  **Exploitation of Vulnerable Groups**

> AI systems that take advantage of vulnerabilities due to age, disability, or social conditions to manipulate or harm users are banned (ArtificialIntelligenceAct.eu, 2024).

4.  **Predictive Policing Based on Profiling**

> AI used to predict criminal behaviour based solely on profiling or behavioural data (without a clear link to past criminal activity) is considered an unacceptable intrusion into individual freedoms (European Parliament, 2024).

5.  **Untargeted Facial Recognition Database Creation**

> The use of AI to harvest biometric data, such as facial images, from the internet or CCTV footage to build facial recognition databases without consent is prohibited (Artificial Intelligence Act.eu, 2024).

6.  **Emotion Recognition in Sensitive Contexts**

> AI systems that attempt to detect emotions in workplaces, schools, or other sensitive contexts (except where needed for health or safety reasons) are banned due to privacy and reliability concerns (Artificial Intelligence Act.eu, 2024).

7.  **Biometric Categorisation Based on Sensitive Attributes**

> AI systems that categorise people using biometric data (e.g. face, voice) to infer race, religion, political orientation, or sexual preference are banned outright due to the high risk of discrimination (European Commission, 2024b).

8.  **Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification in Public Spaces**

> Real-time facial recognition by law enforcement in public spaces is generally prohibited, with narrow exceptions (e.g. for locating missing children or responding to imminent terrorist threats) (European Parliament, 2024).

# Implications for US and Chinese Social Media Platforms

These prohibitions have major implications for foreign social media platforms operating within the EU, particularly large American and Chinese tech companies. The Act applies extraterritorially, if the AI system affects individuals within the EU, it must comply with the regulations, regardless of where the system was developed (WSGR, 2025).

For example, Meta (Facebook) recently updated its privacy policy to permit the use of public posts for training its AI models. In response, EU regulators issued warnings that such usage may violate user consent principles under both the GDPR and the AI Act, especially if data is used without clear notification or opt-out options (Reuters, 2025).

Moreover, any use of AI-powered profiling, behavioural prediction, or content personalisation that nudges user behaviour, particularly if opaque or manipulative, may be scrutinised under the bans on behavioural manipulation and social scoring. While such practices may be permissible under US regulatory frameworks, they are incompatible with the EU's digital rights paradigm.

The AI Act thus sets a precedent: foreign companies wishing to offer AI-driven services in the EU will need to redesign their systems to ensure transparency, human oversight, and non-discrimination. The Act complements other EU legislative efforts such as the Digital Services Act (DSA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), creating a robust defence of digital sovereignty and individual rights.

The EU AI Act reflects a broader strategy to prevent the replication of surveillance-based or socially manipulative AI models within Europe's digital infrastructure. While education-sector applications are not the primary target of the outright bans, the framework signals strong opposition to opaque or exploitative uses of AI, particularly in public, biometric, and social systems. In doing so, it sends a clear message to social media platforms from jurisdictions with looser standards: in Europe, ethics and legality are not afterthoughts they are preconditions.

## The EU's Digital Education Plan (2021-2027)

The EU's Digital Education Action Plan (2021-2027) has initiatives specifically on AI, such as encouraging member states to experiment with AI tutoring systems and developing frameworks for AI-enhanced assessment, all under the umbrella of ethical use. A network of European universities and research institutes is working on AI in education pilots (for example, exploring AI to support teachers in providing feedback), with results feeding into policy recommendations.

For Australia, the EU's experience highlights the value of coupling **immediate guidance with longer-term regulation**. The ethical guidelines help educators adapt practices now, while the AI Act ensures that over time, only safe and transparent AI systems will be allowed in education. Another important aspect is the focus on rights, data protection (via GDPR) and now algorithmic transparency, which ensures that adopting AI in education does not come at the expense of student privacy or equity. ASQA and Australian policymakers could take note of the EU's high standards for educational AI tools, perhaps advocating for similar assurances from any AI system used by RTOs (for example, requiring vendors to explain how their AI-powered learning software meets quality criteria). Aligning with international standards can also future-proof Australia's system in a globalized EdTech market.

# United States

The vocational education and training (VET) system in the United States, commonly referred to as Career and Technical Education (CTE), is a complex and decentralized framework that integrates secondary and postsecondary education with workforce development. This system is designed to equip students with practical skills and competencies aligned with industry needs, thereby facilitating smooth transitions into the labour market.

## Sector Size & Structure

The sector\'s scale is significant, with substantial government investment. For instance, in the 2020/21 school year, the U.S. government allocated approximately \$4.5 billion to technical and vocational education and training (TVET) .

Use of Competency-Based Training (CBT) Competency-Based Training (CBT) is a foundational approach within U.S. vocational education, emphasizing the acquisition of specific, measurable skills aligned with industry standards. This method focuses on learners demonstrating mastery of defined competencies, ensuring they can perform particular tasks to a predetermined level of proficiency (TVET Trainer, 2023).

CBT\'s roots in the U.S. trace back to the 1960s, evolving from behaviorist educational theories that prioritize observable and measurable outcomes. Over time, CBT has been integrated into various vocational programs, particularly in technical fields where practical skills are paramount (ERIC, 2020).

Qualification Requirements (Trainers, Assessors, Managers) Qualifications for vocational educators in the U.S. vary by state and institution but generally include:

-   Trainers/Instructors: Typically required to possess a bachelor\'s degree in education or a related field, along with relevant industry experience. Some states may mandate specific teaching certifications for vocational subjects (Wikipedia, 2023).

-   Assessors: Often need to demonstrate both educational credentials and practical experience in the field they assess. Certification requirements can differ based on state regulations and institutional policies.

-   Program Managers/Administrators: Usually hold advanced degrees in education, administration, or a related discipline, coupled with experience in educational leadership and program management.

It\'s important to note that the decentralized nature of the U.S. education system means that specific qualification requirements can vary significantly across different states and institutions (Wikipedia, 2023).

Curriculum Authority & Design Curriculum development in U.S. vocational education is primarily governed at the state and local levels, allowing for flexibility to meet regional industry needs. However, federal guidelines and funding, particularly through the Carl D. Perkins Career and Technical Education Act, influence curriculum standards and promote consistency across programs (U.S. Department of Education, 2023).

Curricula are often structured around the National Career Clusters Framework, which organizes CTE programs into 16 broad categories, such as Health Science, Information Technology, and Manufacturing. This framework facilitates the alignment of educational programs with workforce demands and provides a coherent structure for curriculum design (UNESCO UNEVOC, 2023).

Industry Involvement Industry engagement is a cornerstone of U.S. vocational education, ensuring that programs remain relevant and responsive to labour market needs. Mechanisms for industry involvement include (West Virginia Department of Education, 2021):

-   Advisory Committees: Comprising industry representatives who provide input on curriculum development, equipment needs, and program evaluation.

-   Work-Based Learning Opportunities: Such as internships, apprenticeships, and cooperative education programs that offer students hands-on experience in real-world settings.

-   Credentialing Partnerships: Collaboration with industry bodies to develop certifications that validate students\' skills and enhance employability.

Key Regulatory Bodies The governance of vocational education in the U.S. involves multiple entities across federal, state, and local levels:

-   Federal Level: The Office of Career, Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) within the U.S. Department of Education oversees national policies and administers funding through the Perkins Act (Wikipedia, 2023).

-   State Level: State Departments of Education develop and implement CTE policies, standards, and funding allocations. They also oversee program approval and quality assurance.

-   Local Level: Local Education Agencies (LEAs) and individual institutions are responsible for program delivery, curriculum implementation, and day-to-day operations.

**Trump's First Term (2017--2021):** In his first administration, Donald Trump launched the *American AI Initiative* via Executive Order 13859 (Feb 2019), signalling a strategy centred on innovation and competition. This initiative emphasized **five pillars** -- increasing AI R&D investment, unleashing federal data and computing resources, setting technical standards, training an AI workforce, and engaging international allies. Indeed, the Trump White House doubled federal AI research funding and established the first national AI research institutes. Regulatory intervention was minimal: the administration issued light-touch guidance on AI (the world's first national AI guidance of its kind) focused on avoiding overregulation. In short, Trump's first-term policy sought to **"maintain American leadership"** in AI primarily by **fuelling innovation** and **removing barriers**, with an explicit view that continued US dominance in AI was vital for economic and national security. Notably, direct federal oversight of AI risks was limited; instead, the administration promoted voluntary frameworks and "AI with American Values," a slogan that in practice prioritized US economic interests and military strength in AI development.

## Biden's Term (2021--2025) 

President Joe Biden's approach to AI marked a shift toward **responsible innovation and risk mitigation**. The Biden administration joined allies in affirming ethical AI principles and proposed a *Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights* to protect privacy, civil rights, and consumers (October 2022). In late 2023, Biden issued **Executive Order 14110 "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of AI"**, which sought to put guardrails around AI deployment. This order and related Biden-era policies encouraged federal agencies to address algorithmic discrimination, AI transparency, and potential labour impacts. For example, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) under Biden released guidance on preventing bias in AI hiring tools, and the White House mandated use of the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** for federal agencies. Biden's policies tried to **balance innovation with safeguards**, reflecting concerns about AI-driven fraud, privacy breaches, and job displacement. Internationally, Biden officials engaged in multilateral efforts (e.g. the Global Partnership on AI and G7/OECD AI principles) to promote **rights-based AI governance** and standards for trustworthy AI, aligning the U.S. more closely with European allies on AI ethics than Trump had in his first term.

## Trump's Second Term (2025--Present)

Upon returning to office in January 2025, Trump rapidly **reversed course** on AI policy, dismantling Biden's nascent AI regulations in favour of an aggressive pro-industry stance. Within his first week back, President Trump **rescinded Biden's October 2023 AI Executive Order** and related guidance, criticizing them as *"unnecessarily burdensome"* rules that *"stifle private sector innovation and threaten American technological leadership"*. In its place, Trump issued **Executive Order 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence,"** declaring it U.S. policy to *"sustain and enhance America's global AI dominance"*. This EO explicitly directs agencies to **eliminate or revise any regulations** that impede U.S. AI development. The Trump White House's fact sheet framed prior AI oversight as ideological meddling, insisting that *"development of AI systems must be free from ideological bias or engineered social agendas"*. In practice, this means halting federal work on AI ethics or fairness constraints. Indeed, Biden-era efforts such as the EEOC's AI hiring guidance and draft rules on algorithmic accountability, were swiftly pulled down or suspended. President Trump instead positioned the government as an unabashed **promoter and customer of AI innovation**, naming prominent tech investor **David Sacks** as the new **"White House AI & Crypto Czar"** to shape policy alongside industry leaders. This represents a fundamental shift: whereas Biden sought to **regulate AI's impacts**, Trump's second-term doctrine is to **deregulate and double-down on U.S. AI supremacy**. The contrast is stark, one former administration official noted that Biden aimed to *"safeguard workers and consumers"* from AI risks, while Trump insists that removing "barriers" will unleash innovation and keep America *"unrivalled"* in the global AI race.

**Trump's second term marks a return to the free-market, techno-nationalist approach of his first term, now taken to new extremes**. Any regulatory restraint introduced under Biden is being rolled back in favour of **laissez-faire AI development and deployment**, justified by the administration as necessary to win an AI arms race against foreign adversaries. By prioritizing industry self-regulation and **rejecting federal "red tape,"** Trump has created an environment where AI adoption can accelerate across sectors, but with minimal centralized oversight on issues of bias, safety, or ethics.

## Deregulation Trends in Tech: AI, Education, Publishing, Crypto, and Defence

A hallmark of Trump's second administration is a sweeping **deregulatory agenda** affecting technology and related sectors. The White House has moved rapidly to loosen or remove regulations in areas ranging from artificial intelligence to education and finance.

## Key trends

### Artificial Intelligence

As described above, Trump's policy is to **lift regulatory constraints on AI** development. Federal agencies have been ordered to *"review and rescind"* rules that could *"impede the nation's role as an AI leader"*. This has halted initiatives that were aimed at AI accountability. The Labor Department has paused enforcement of Biden-era rules on AI-driven hiring bias and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau stepped back efforts to oversee AI in lending decisions. The administration's philosophy is that **market forces and innovation priorities** should drive AI growth, not "pre-emptive" regulation. Trump's EO on AI leadership even emphasizes **"ideological neutrality"** in algorithms, implicitly pushing back against algorithms being modified for fairness or political correctness. In effect, **AI developers face fewer compliance hurdles**: there are no new federal mandates on testing AI for discrimination or security, and agencies have been discouraged from issuing any rules that might slow AI product rollouts. While this hands-off approach is meant to speed up AI adoption, it raises concern that issues like AI-driven bias or privacy violations will rely solely on industry self-policing or after-the-fact state laws. Indeed, several U.S. states (e.g. California, Illinois, New York City) are now pursuing their own AI regulations to fill the vacuum, potentially creating a patchwork of rules in the absence of federal standards.

### Education

The Trump administration's deregulatory zeal extends strongly into education, where it has sought to **dramatically shrink the federal role**. President Trump has long floated eliminating the U.S. Department of Education, and in 2025 he moved aggressively toward that goal, **freezing hiring, cutting programs and even attempting a departmental dismantling via executive order**. With encouragement from Trump-aligned figures like Elon Musk, the White House is targeting what it calls "bureaucratic excess" and "indoctrination" in schools. In practice, this means rescinding many federal education regulations and defunding programs. For example, in February 2025 the Education Department (under Trump's direction) **cut \$600 million in teacher training grants**, claiming these grants promoted *"divisive ideologies"* such as diversity and inclusion. (These cuts were so abrupt that a coalition of eight states sued, though the Supreme Court allowed the funding termination to proceed.) The administration also issued an ultimatum to schools and universities: **eliminate diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives or lose federal funding**. In a memo sent in February, the Department of Education gave institutions **14 days** to end any practice using "racial preferences" in admissions, financial aid, hiring or curriculum, framing such efforts as illegal discrimination. This order is an extension of Trump's executive order banning DEI programs government-wide and has forced colleges to shut down everything from diversity offices to minority mentorship programs. It *"raised the stakes in the fight against 'wokeness'"* in education. More broadly, Trump's budget proposals have sought **deep cuts to education spending**, including reducing Title I funding for low-income schools and scaling back special education grants. He has also revived proposals to promote private school vouchers and **curtail the Education Department's size or merge its functions elsewhere**. The result is a deregulated landscape where federal oversight of school quality, civil rights enforcement, and research funding is being pulled back. Many education services that **millions of students (especially in poor districts) rely on are at risk**, from Title I aid to programs for English-language learners and students with disabilities. Critics warn that these cuts and mandates won't improve learning or save money but will *"take a sledgehammer"* to supports for vulnerable students. In the vacuum, tech solutions including AI-based educational software and privatized online learning programs may rush in to fill gaps. Indeed, Trump's April 2025 executive order on AI education and workforce development hints at relying on **public-private partnerships to provide AI resources in schools**. However, with **little federal guidance on safe or effective use of AI in the classroom**, this deregulation raises the spectre of an unregulated ed-tech boom where products are adopted rapidly despite privacy or bias concerns. States and school districts are left to decide individually how to integrate AI tools into teaching, potentially widening inequalities (well-resourced districts might implement cutting-edge AI tutors, while poorer ones lose human staff and receive only cheap algorithmic substitutes).

### Publishing and Media

In the information sphere, the Trump administration's stance has been to **minimize government intervention in content and platform governance**, which it frames as defending free speech and innovation. Practically, this has meant **non-interference in the rise of AI-generated content** and a rollback of attempts to hold online platforms accountable. For instance, the administration opposes efforts to update Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (the law shielding tech platforms from content liability). Trump instead has focused on accusing social media and publishing platforms of *anti-conservative bias*, threatening them rhetorically but not supporting the kind of robust regulation or co-regulation that some lawmakers (and European regulators) favour. The result is a **deregulated environment for online publishing**: AI tools that generate text, images, or deepfakes face few U.S. legal restrictions. There are **no new federal rules on AI and copyright or misinformation**, despite publishers' concerns about unlicensed AI scraping of their content. In contrast, the EU's upcoming AI Act and Digital Services Act impose transparency and copyright requirements on AI models, conditions U.S. companies find onerous. The Trump administration has made it clear it views EU digital regulations as protectionist "taxes" on American firms. Rather than emulate Europe's rights-based approach (e.g. requiring labels on AI-generated media or audits for algorithms), the U.S. under Trump is doubling down on a hands-off model. This extends to **government communications**: the administration itself has reportedly embraced generative AI for messaging and propaganda, unconstrained by prior norms. Overall, publishers and media outlets in the U.S. must navigate a Wild West of AI-driven content without the support of new federal protections. Traditional publishers (news organizations, book authors, educational content providers) have found little recourse in Washington if AI systems use their material or flood the market with synthetic media. Trump's deregulatory instinct in this domain reflects a calculation that **more regulation would only hamper U.S. tech platforms** while he prefers to combat perceived bias through political pressure and antitrust investigations rather than new rules. The tension between this U.S. approach and more interventionist approaches abroad (EU, Australia) is growing, as discussed below.

### Cryptocurrency and Fintech

After initially dismissing Bitcoin and crypto in his first term, President Trump has surprisingly **pivoted to a crypto-friendly stance** in his second term. The administration's broader economic agenda uses deregulation of crypto as a tool for financial strategy (see Section 6). Early in 2025, Trump **signed an executive order establishing a "Strategic Bitcoin Reserve" and a U.S. Digital Asset Stockpile**. This unprecedented move directs the Federal Reserve and Treasury to **hold cryptocurrencies as reserve assets**, elevating crypto to something akin to gold in the federal portfolio. At the same time, Trump voiced **strong support for dollar-backed stablecoins**, urging Congress to pass laws clarifying their legal status. These actions effectively *reverse* the cautious approach of the previous administration (which had regulators cracking down on crypto exchanges and exploring a U.S. central bank digital currency). Under Trump, agencies like the SEC and OCC have shifted to a lighter-touch approach: enforcement actions against major crypto players have been scaled back, and proposals for a U.S. central bank digital currency were shelved as *"government overreach."* The White House portrays crypto innovation as vital for American financial leadership and has rolled out the welcome mat for blockchain entrepreneurs. In deregulating crypto, Trump aligns with allies in the tech industry (e.g. venture capitalists who see Web3 as the next frontier). **Crypto-related regulations and oversight mechanisms put in place under Biden are being reviewed or revoked**. For example, the Financial Stability Oversight Council's 2022 report calling for stricter stablecoin safeguards is now essentially moot, as Trump explicitly encourages adoption of stablecoins to extend U.S. dollar influence. Similarly, consumer protection efforts (like tighter rules on crypto advertising and safeguards against fraud) have been dialled back. Instead, the administration is exploring **incentives for crypto use** even hinting at tax breaks for repatriating overseas capital via digital assets. This deregulation carries risks: sceptics fear it could invite **volatile speculative bubbles and reduce oversight of illicit finance**. Yet Trump's team argues that integrating crypto into the U.S. economy (rather than fighting it) will shore up American dominance in the global financial system. As discussed later, Trump even envisions using crypto as a geopolitical tool to repatriate dollars and undercut rival currencies.

### Defence and Aerospace Tech

The tech deregulation agenda is perhaps most pronounced and consequential in the **defence sector**, where Trump has removed many peacetime restraints on developing and deploying advanced military technologies. His administration sees **AI dominance in defence as a paramount goal**, overriding prior ethical or multilateral considerations. Trump's new Secretary of Defense (former commentator Pete Hegseth) and other appointees have openly questioned the Pentagon's traditional caution on autonomous weapons. They argue the U.S. must *"contain China at all costs"* in the AI arms race. Consequently, the administration is **loosening internal regulations** that previously required a "human in the loop" for lethal force decisions. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 (which governed autonomy in weapons) is being revised to permit faster adoption of **lethal autonomous weapon systems** (LAWS) without high-level approvals. A new directive encourages service branches to experiment with autonomous drones, vehicles, and combat AI "swarm" systems, accelerating programs like the Pentagon's Project Replicator (aimed at fielding thousands of autonomous systems). Moreover, Trump quickly scrapped Biden-era policies that assessed AI's impact on international law and norms. He withdrew U.S. support from international talks on banning or limiting autonomous weapons, with the administration arguing such agreements *"shackle American hard power"*. The President and Vice President (J.D. Vance) have bluntly stated that *"the AI future will not be won by hand-wringing about safety"*. In line with this, the Pentagon under Trump has forged an unprecedented integration with the tech industry: **major tech companies are invited to directly partner on defence AI projects**. Notably, in late 2024 and early 2025 there was a surge of activity: Meta open-sourced its LLaMA AI model for U.S. government use; Anthropic agreed to work with Palantir and Amazon on selling AI into military channels; Lockheed Martin launched a new AI division; and defence startups like Anduril inked deals (in one case teaming with OpenAI to integrate large language models into autonomous drones). Even Google reversed its earlier pledge and **dropped its ban on using its AI for weapons development** by February 2025. This **"fusion of Silicon Valley and the Pentagon"** is actively encouraged by Trump's deregulation and he has removed bureaucratic procurement barriers and oversight committees that previously slowed adoption of experimental tech. Defence officials have been purging legal advisors who object on ethical or legal grounds, sending a clear signal that rapid deployment is the priority. Billions of dollars are now flowing into AI-driven defence startups; since 2021, defence tech firms have received over \$100 billion in venture funding, a trend likely to grow as the Pentagon opens its coffers further. Overall, the administration's stance is that **"guardrails are being dismantled"** to achieve technological supremacy over adversaries. While this may spur an **explosion of innovation in military AI**, it also raises profound ethical and safety questions. Allies in Europe have expressed alarm at America's go-it-alone approach on military AI norms, and human rights groups warn that the risk of autonomous warfare accidents or abuses will increase. Trump's team, however, views such concerns as secondary, believing that *"soft power"* and normative constraints only hinder America in a confrontational world.

In all these domains, **Trump's second-term deregulation reflects a consistent ideology:** that **removing government "red tape" and fortifying national sovereignty will boost innovation and U.S. dominance**. This approach has unquestionably galvanized U.S. tech industries, but it also puts the onus on states, civil society, and the market to deal with the side effects (whether bias in AI, diminished equity in education, or global instability from unrestrained tech deployment). We next examine how specific individuals allied with Trump influence these trends, particularly in education and federal R&D priorities.

## Trump-Aligned Tech Figures Shaping Policy: Musk, Sacks, and Others

President Trump's agenda has been actively shaped by a cadre of tech magnates and investors who share his vision or have his ear. In his second term, **several Trump-aligned tech figures occupy formal or informal advisory roles**, leveraging their influence to reshape federal priorities in technology and education:

### Elon Musk

The billionaire CEO of Tesla, SpaceX (and owner of X/Twitter) has emerged as an especially vocal ally of Trump's administrative shake-up. Musk was a conspicuous presence at Trump's January 2025 inauguration events, and he has since publicly backed Trump's most radical education policy: **eliminating the Department of Education**. In February 2025, Musk tweeted that Trump *"will succeed"* in abolishing the Department, even as Trump drafted an executive order aiming to dismantle the \$79 billion agency. (Legally, dissolving a Cabinet department requires Congress, but Trump (spurred by Musk) tested those limits by gutting the department's functions via executive action.) Musk and his allies have helped "purge" hundreds of federal employees across agencies, targeting those seen as obstacles to Trump's agenda. At the Education Department, dozens of career staff were suddenly placed on leave in early 2025 under the pretext of enforcing Trump's DEI ban, **even if they didn't work on DEI programs**. This included personnel from units that distribute aid to K-12 schools and those enforcing civil rights laws in education. A union official described it as an ideologically driven purge, and a U.S. Senator noted *"He's just breaking services people rely on"* in schools. Musk's influence here is twofold: he provides **political cover and amplification** for drastic cuts (rallying the base with his statements), and he lends tech-world credibility to Trump's idea that education might be better run without a federal bureaucracy. Musk's own ventures into education (he founded a small, tech-centric school for his children and has criticized traditional schooling as outdated) align with the administration's push for alternative models. Furthermore, Musk's antagonism toward "woke" policies resonates in Trump's eradication of DEI. It's reported that Musk personally encouraged the administration's hard line that DEI initiatives are discriminatory and should be rooted out of not just companies but also schools and colleges. In short, Musk has become a high-profile champion of **defunding public education mandates and promoting a libertarian, tech-driven approach**. He even suggested that AI tutoring and private sector solutions could fill the void of federal retrenchment, reflecting a techno-optimist view that Silicon Valley can solve problems that Washington need not.

### David Sacks

A lesser-known but highly influential figure, Sacks is a venture capitalist (part of the "PayPal mafia") and a close associate of Musk. Trump appointed **David Sacks as the White House "AI & Crypto Czar"** in 2025. In this role Sacks coordinates cross-agency efforts on emerging tech. His mandate is to interface with industry and craft policy that ensures U.S. leadership in AI and blockchain. Sacks, known for his libertarian-leaning views, has been a **vocal critic of Big Tech regulation and of "woke" corporate practices**. He likely had a hand in writing Trump's pro-innovation AI executive order and shaping the narrative that Biden's AI rules were about *"engineered social agendas"*. Insiders note that Sacks has pushed for **greater private sector involvement in education technology** as well. For example, the April 2025 EO on AI education (which set up a task force for AI in education) encourages **public-private partnerships** a model Sacks favours to inject Silicon Valley solutions into public services. Under his influence, we may see initiatives where tech firms provide AI tools to schools (for tutoring, administrative automation, etc.) in exchange for data or future business, effectively reshaping education through a market-driven lens. Sacks is also reportedly working to align federal R&D funding with the interests of tech investors: he advocates redirecting some government research grants toward AI, quantum computing, and crypto technologies, while eliminating programs he deems ideologically biased or low impact. His presence in the White House underscores how **Trump's tech policy is being co-written by venture capital**, prioritizing deregulation, entrepreneurial freedom, and the fusion of government needs with start-up innovation.

### Other Allies and Influencers

Beyond Musk and Sacks, several other tech-aligned figures are impacting policy. **Peter Thiel**, who supported Trump's campaigns, continues to influence tech and defence policy behind the scenes. Thiel's data analytics firm Palantir benefited from Trump's first term with large government contracts, and now Palantir is deeply involved in military AI and border technology. Thiel has long opposed diversity initiatives and favoured a more confrontational stance with China, themes mirrored in Trump's actions. **Palmer Luckey**, founder of Oculus and the defence startup Anduril, is another notable ally; Anduril's cutting-edge drone and surveillance tech aligns well with Trump's border security and defence AI push. Luckey has publicly praised the administration's removal of what he called *"bureaucratic shackles"* on defence innovation. The Vice President, **J.D. Vance**, though a government official, also belongs to this circle as a venture capitalist backed by Thiel, Vance amplifies Silicon Valley's anti-establishment streak within the administration. He has been explicit that the U.S. should not let **"hand-wringing"** over AI safety slow it down, and he casts regulatory efforts (especially by Europe) as attempts to hamstring American tech firms. All these figures share a **philosophy of techno-accelerationism**: they believe the U.S. must move fast and break things (be it outdated policies or global norms) to win the tech competition of the 21st century. This has resulted in tangible policy outcomes: *Cutting federal science and education bureaucracies, axing DEI programs, privileging defence and industry in AI funding decisions, and sidelining ethicists and social scientists in advisory roles.* For instance, soon after inauguration, Trump replaced members of the White House OSTP and advisory councils who had expertise in AI ethics, bringing in more business-oriented advisors.

The **removal of DEI programs** is a signature example of how these tech-aligned advisors have reshaped priorities. On Day 1, Trump's EO 14151 directed all agencies to terminate "illegal" DEI mandates and training and EO 14173 went further to **rescind Executive Order 11246** (in place since the 1960s to enforce affirmative action by federal contractors). This effectively rolled back decades of diversity policy in government contracting, a move cheered by certain business leaders. The **justification given** was that any consideration of race or gender is discriminatory, an echo of arguments Musk, Thiel and others have made that meritocracy is undermined by affirmative action. Additionally, Trump's April 2025 order ending the use of "disparate impact" analysis in enforcing civil rights law came straight from conservative think-tank wish lists and was applauded by some corporate executives who felt burdened by compliance. It's clear that **ideological alignment with Trump's base (anti-"woke", pro-business) among tech elites has translated into concrete policy**. While these moves are touted as promoting "equality under the law" (in Trump's words) and efficiency, civil rights advocates warn they will reverse progress for marginalized groups.

In sum, **Trump's second-term policymaking has a strong infusion of Silicon Valley's most contrarian voices**. Billionaires like Musk have lent their clout to slash government spending on traditional education and to disrupt bureaucratic norms, while investors like Sacks have directly steered the administration's tech strategy from within. This synergy accelerates the implementation of Trump's agenda, melding political power with tech industry know-how, but it also means public policy is being driven by individuals with significant financial stakes and ideological leanings. The education sector is feeling the shock: **funding cuts, loss of diversity programs, and a turn toward privatized, AI-enabled solutions** all bear the fingerprints of these Trump-aligned tech figures.

## Global Impact: Clashes with EU AI Rules and Australia's Safety Regulations

Trump's domestic shifts in AI and tech policy are reverberating globally, often putting the United States at odds with allies who are pursuing a more regulatory or rights-focused approach. Two revealing case studies are the **European Union's rights-based AI regulation** and **Australia's child online safety regulations**, both of which have created friction with the U.S. tech sector and the Trump administration.

### EU's Rights-Based AI Regulation vs. U.S. Deregulation

The European Union has been advancing a comprehensive regulatory regime for AI, centred on fundamental rights, transparency, and risk mitigation. The flagship **EU AI Act**, set to take effect in phases starting 2025, adopts a **"risk-based" framework**, banning certain high-risk AI practices and imposing strict requirements (such as transparency disclosures and human oversight) on AI systems used in sensitive domains. For example, as of February 2025 the EU has *prohibited* AI systems with "unacceptable risk" (like social scoring and real-time biometric surveillance), and over the coming year the Act will mandate that higher-risk AI (e.g. in education, employment, law enforcement) undergo conformity assessments and documentation. These rules apply to any AI product or service operating in Europe, including those from U.S. tech companies. In parallel, the EU has updated digital services laws (DSA/DMA) and pursued an AI Liability Directive (though the latter was recently withdrawn to fine-tune the balance between innovation and accountability).

This European approach **prioritizes trust, safety, and fundamental rights**, aiming to prevent AI from infringing on privacy or discriminating. EU leaders stress that the rules *"apply equally to all actors, American, Chinese, or European"*, rejecting the notion of singling out U.S. firms. From Brussels' perspective, the regulations create a level playing field and protect citizens from harm. However, from Washington -- especially under Trump these moves look very different. President Trump and his officials see the EU's digital regulations as **economically motivated barriers** targeting U.S. tech dominance. Trump has derided EU fines and rules as *"a form of taxation"* on American companies. Vice President Vance similarly accuses *"foreign governments"* of *"tightening the screws"* on Silicon Valley, warning that overregulation abroad could *"stifle a transformative industry"*. Congressional Republicans echo this, arguing that EU laws like the DSA and AI Act disproportionally burden U.S. platforms and serve European protectionist interests.

The result is a growing **transatlantic tension** over AI governance. The Trump administration has taken a confrontational diplomatic stance: at international forums such as the **Paris AI Action Summit (Feb 2025)**, the U.S. delegation pointedly refused to join EU-backed declarations on "Inclusive and Sustainable AI". American negotiators cited scepticism of Europe's regulatory approach, emphasizing instead **"innovation and sovereignty"**. The U.S. is also reportedly lobbying friendly nations to adopt **American-style AI frameworks** (lighter regulation, focus on AI for economic growth) as a counterweight to EU influence. For instance, Britain post-Brexit has been leaning more toward a flexible AI strategy in line with the U.S. and Trump's team has applauded this divergence from Brussels. Meanwhile, the **Council of Europe's draft AI Treaty**, an effort among about 20+ countries to create binding international AI rules has seen U.S. officials participate only as observers and sceptics. Both the U.S. and EU are vying to set global standards: one through hard law and rights-centric treaties, the other through market power and bilateral deals. A commentator described it as a contest **"to shape the rules, values, and market structures"** of next-generation tech.

This friction has practical implications for companies and users. U.S. tech companies now must either comply with the EU's stringent requirements incurring significant compliance costs and sometimes redesigning their AI systems or face hefty fines (the AI Act threatens penalties up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover for violations). Some companies have voiced concern that EU rules will slow AI deployment or force them to limit services in Europe. Indeed, in 2023 some AI firms (notably OpenAI's CEO) even mused about potentially withdrawing from the EU if regulation became too onerous, although that hasn't happened so far. Instead, firms are investing in compliance: e.g., providing EU-specific transparency tools and data governance measures. The Trump administration, however, has not been sympathetic to these compliance efforts, it has *not* pressured U.S. companies to follow EU standards and has even hinted that it would defend American firms in disputes (seeing them as unfairly targeted). We could foresee, for example, the Trump DOJ or USTR challenging aspects of EU digital regulation as trade barriers in the WTO or in future U.S.-EU trade talks. All of this creates a **regulatory schism**: Europe's *rights-first* approach versus America's *innovation-first* approach. Third countries may be pulled between these models. Already, we see countries like **India** and **Brazil** studying the EU model, while others like **Singapore** lean toward a lighter touch, choices that could align them with one bloc or the other. If U.S. and EU tensions intensify, it could lead to a fragmented tech ecosystem where AI products have regional variants (to meet local laws) or where data flows are restricted (e.g., the ongoing EU-U.S. wrangles over data privacy impacting AI training data). In essence, **global AI governance is now contested territory**, with Trump's policies exacerbating the divide by explicitly rejecting the multilateral, rights-oriented consensus that Europe is trying to build.

### Australia's Child Safety Tech Regulations

Another arena of friction is the domain of **online child safety**, where U.S. tech companies are facing stringent new rules in countries like Australia that contrast with the deregulated U.S. stance. In late 2024, Australia passed one of the world's toughest laws to protect minors online, effectively a **social media ban for children under 16**. This law, which takes full effect by the end of 2025, **requires social media platforms to verify users' ages and prohibit anyone under 16 from holding an account**. Platforms like Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, X (Twitter) and others must implement robust age verification or face fines up to A\$50 million for non-compliance. The Australian public strongly backed this move (surveys showed 77% support) amid growing concern over social media's mental health impacts on youth. Child safety groups in many countries have debated similar measures, but Australia's is the first comprehensive under-16 ban passed in a liberal democracy, making it a **test case for regulating Big Tech in the name of child welfare**.

The law puts U.S. based platforms in a difficult position. To enforce it, they must deploy technologies like **age verification**, possibly involving AI estimation of ages via facial analysis, government ID checks, or other invasive methods, which raise privacy issues and technical challenges. Companies have been wary: too lax an implementation and they incur huge fines; too strict and they risk losing teenage user bases or breaching user privacy. The Trump administration's approach to this has been hands-off internally (the U.S. has no federal age-gating law and is unlikely to pursue one under Trump, who generally opposes new internet regulations), but confrontational externally. Administration officials privately criticized Australia's law as heavy-handed and **"censorial."** They pointed out that in the U.S., attempts to impose similar age restrictions (like certain U.S. states requiring parental consent for minors' social media use) have faced constitutional challenges on First Amendment grounds. Indeed, a Florida law that banned social media for under-18 was struck down in courts as violating free speech; Australian law has no such constitutional hurdle, illustrating a values gap between the countries on this issue.

Australian regulators, notably the **eSafety Commissioner**, have also been pushing for tech companies to **proactively combat child sexual abuse material (CSAM)** online, even if it means undermining encryption. Australia's 2021 Online Safety Act gave the government power to issue **"basic online safety expectations"** notices to platforms and to require periodic transparency reports on how they handle child abuse content. There's also discussion in Australia (similar to the UK's debates) about whether end-to-end encrypted services should be required to have some scanning capability for CSAM. U.S. companies like Meta have resisted such moves, arguing that it would compromise user privacy and security. Under the Biden administration, there was at least diplomatic dialogue on these tricky issues, but under Trump, there is less inclination to cooperate with foreign regulators. If anything, Trump's DOJ has historically favoured strong law enforcement powers and might sympathize with surveillance, but Trump's political stance against Big Tech "censorship" complicates openly supporting something like scanning private messages for content. The outcome is that U.S. companies often find themselves caught **complying with foreign safety mandates that go beyond U.S. law**. In Australia's case, come 2025, platforms will likely implement some form of age verification (the government is trailing various technologies, including blockchain-based age ID and AI facial analysis). This creates a patchwork where, for example, a 15-year-old might legally use Instagram in the U.S., but not in Australia.

*From the global viewpoint, such divergent approaches pose operational and ethical dilemmas.*

Companies may choose to **geo-fence features or apply stricter standards in one country than another**, leading to inequities in user experience. It also raises enforcement questions.

### *Will Australia's law be enforceable if teens use VPNs or if foreign platforms only partially comply?*

If U.S. companies flout the rules, it could strain diplomatic relations; if they comply rigorously, it could embolden other countries to demand similar concessions. The Trump administration, true to its America First ethos, has signalled it will not let foreign governments unilaterally dictate how U.S. tech firms operate globally without pushback. Already, there's talk of using trade agreements or forums to argue that some requirements (like handing over encryption backdoors or proprietary algorithms) constitute unfair practices. Yet, at the same time, Trump has been inconsistent on issues like encryption. In his first term, he often sided with law enforcement in urging tech companies to allow access to encrypted devices. So the U.S. stance might vary depending on the issue: supportive of some child-protection aims (when framed as crime-fighting), but opposed to broad platform liability or drastic measures like age bans that curb business interests.

In essence, **global tech governance is splitting into multiple regimes**: a U.S. model that is relatively laissez-faire and industry-led and others (EU, Australia, and beyond) that are more interventionist in the name of rights and safety. This is leading to friction as companies navigate conflicting laws. European and Australian officials privately express dismay that Washington is not just lagging on issues like AI ethics and child safety, but sometimes actively undermining international coordination. For instance, at the **Seoul AI Summit (May 2024)** and **Paris Summit (2025)**, high-level principles on AI safety were agreed by many nations, but the U.S. often added footnotes or abstained on commitments to regulation. One concrete example: **61 countries** in late 2024 endorsed a voluntary commitment not to develop certain autonomous weapons (a kind of moratorium), but the U.S. under Trump pointedly did not join, preferring to keep its options open. Similarly, on online harms, the U.S. has been absent from initiatives like the Christchurch Call (to combat extremist content) or has sent low-level delegates, signalling a step back from multinational pledges made under the prior administration.

The **global impact of Trump's shifts** thus includes a deepening rift with Europe over AI and data governance, and situational tensions with close allies like Australia when their domestic tech regulations clash with Silicon Valley's modus operandi. Over time, this could spur localization of technology e.g. European users might see more Europe-based AI services arise, touting compliance and privacy as selling points (since American services may be viewed as too data-extractive or opaque). American platforms, for their part, could decide to comply minimally or even exit smaller markets if regulations there are deemed too strict relative to the profit (something not unthinkable if the patchwork grows). **In the long run, if no common ground is found, the world may experience a kind of "digital decoupling" between the U.S. and allied democracies, not just the oft-discussed U.S. and China decoupling.** That would have profound implications for global AI governance, as discussed in the final section.

### Booming U.S. AI Startups and New Business Models under Trump

One immediate consequence of Trump's deregulation and pro-industry policies has been an **explosion of AI-focused startups and novel business models in the United States**. By removing regulatory hurdles and actively championing tech entrepreneurship, the administration has created a climate of exuberance in the AI sector:

### Investment Surge

Venture capital and private equity are pouring money into AI ventures at record levels. Since 2021, and accelerating through 2024--25, U.S. defence and dual-use AI startups alone have attracted over **\$100 billion in VC funding**. Companies developing everything from generative AI software to autonomous vehicles and biotech AI are seeing skyrocketing valuations. Investors cite the **friendly policy environment** with no looming threat of heavy regulation or antitrust breakup as a key reason the U.S. is the premier destination for AI investment. Trump's massive corporate tax cuts (from his first term) remain in effect, and he has hinted at further tax incentives specifically for tech investments. Moreover, the administration's rhetoric that *"America's AI industry \[will\] be fully unleashed"* has created bullish sentiment: entrepreneurs feel that, unlike in the EU or even China (which has tight state controls), they have free rein to innovate in the U.S. This has led to a virtuous cycle (or a bubble, depending on who you ask) of more startups forming to chase the abundant capital, especially in **frontier areas like large language models, AI chips, automation software, and crypto applications**.

### New Business Models in AI

With government policies encouraging deployment, many startups are rushing to integrate AI into traditionally regulated sectors that are now opening up. For example, in **education technology**, companies are introducing AI tutors, grading systems, and adaptive learning platforms to fill the gap left by reduced public funding. Some are partnering directly with states or school districts in pilot programs (facilitated by Trump's push for public-private partnerships) to replace or augment teachers with AI assistants. These models offering "AI-as-a-service" to schools, likely wouldn't have advanced so quickly if the federal government were more cautious or had privacy regulations in place. Similarly, in **healthcare AI**, the FDA under Trump has eased pathways for AI-driven diagnostic tools, leading to startups launching AI medical triage apps and imaging analysis services without extensive clinical trial requirements that might have been demanded previously. In **finance**, with the embrace of crypto and fintech, startups are developing new crypto trading platforms, stablecoin-based payment systems, and AI-driven investment advisors, relatively free from stringent oversight. The administration's clear signalling that it won't quickly regulate areas like algorithmic trading or "robo-advisors" has emboldened these firms to innovate new products for consumers (though not without risk).

### Defence and Security Startups

Perhaps the most pronounced boom is in defence tech startups, a **direct result of Trump's procurement shifts**. Companies like **Anduril, Palantir, SpaceX's defence wing, Shield AI, Rebellion Defence** and dozens of newer entrants are landing significant Pentagon contracts to provide AI solutions for surveillance, logistics and combat systems. Trump's Pentagon has launched fast-track funding programs (akin to the previous DIU and AFWERX initiatives but supercharged) to scale up small tech firms that can deliver AI capabilities. For example, **Scale AI**, originally a Silicon Valley data-labelling startup, secured a contract in 2025 to supply "artificial agents" for military planning exercises. Meanwhile, legacy defence contractors are spinning off internal startups (as Lockheed Martin did with its new AI subsidiary) to remain competitive. The government is also using **challenge competitions and prize incentives** to crowdsource AI solutions from startups, further fuelling the sector. The presence of figures like Palmer Luckey (Anduril) and Trey Stephens (Founders Fund, involved in defence startups) in Trump's circle means these new companies have advocates pushing their tech into real-world use quickly. The net effect is that the U.S. defence procurement is no longer the slow, bureaucratic process of old, it's becoming more **venture-capital-like, accepting prototypes from 20-something startup founders** and deploying them in months. This dynamic has positioned the U.S. as **the place to be for defence AI entrepreneurs**, likely outpacing allied countries who have more cumbersome approval processes.

### Open-Source and Collaboration Models

Another interesting development is the embrace of *open-source AI models* by U.S. businesses, with tacit government approval. In late 2024, as noted, **Meta released its LLaMA AI model to government and researchers**, an open approach quite unlike the proprietary stance of OpenAI's GPT. This open-source surge (Meta's model, StabilityAI's image generators, etc.) has catalysed countless startups building on these models without needing to reinvent the core algorithms. The Trump administration's lack of concern about potential misuse of open-source AI (contrasted with some EU voices who worry about open models enabling disinformation or cybercrime) means the U.S. has not tried to restrict or watermark these models. Companies are free to take an open-source large model and fine-tune it for niche uses -- a common business model now. For instance, startups are customizing open AI models for specific industries (legal AI assistants, educational chatbots, etc.) and scaling up quickly, since no regulation compels them to filter outputs or audit for bias. This environment is fostering a **vibrant developer community and rapid commercialization**. One could argue that by not imposing rules like requiring AI models to reveal copyrighted training data or meet certain transparency standards (provisions Europe is leaning toward), the U.S. is giving its AI firms a competitive advantage in agility. However, if those startups later want to export to regulated markets, they might hit barriers.

### Cryptocurrency & Fintech Startups

With crypto legitimized by Trump's policies, a wave of **crypto startups and fintech innovators** has also emerged. New stablecoin issuers, blockchain infrastructure firms, and crypto investment platforms have set up shop, many in places like Miami and Austin where local governments are crypto-friendly. The promise of regulatory certainty, Trump endorsing Congress's move to legalize and regulate stablecoins has drawn not only domestic entrepreneurs but also foreign crypto companies to relocate to the U.S. (in contrast, some were fleeing the U.S. during the prior regulatory crackdowns). This influx is solidifying the U.S. as a global hub for blockchain innovation once again. We see business models that integrate AI and blockchain too: e.g., startups offering AI-driven smart contract auditing, or using blockchain to verify deepfake media authenticity, all leveraging the synergy of two industries now favoured by policy. It's worth noting that Trump's personal interest in crypto (including reports of him and his wife profiting from issuing collectible "meme coins" or NFTs just before inauguration) has also made crypto entrepreneurship somewhat glamorous in the political sphere, adding to the hype.

Overall, **the policy environment under Trump has turbocharged entrepreneurial activity**. The U.S. is witnessing what might be called an *AI Gold Rush*: talent and capital are converging to launch new ventures at a dizzying pace. Many of these startups explicitly cite the lack of restrictive rules as enabling rapid iteration for instance, developers of an AI-powered recruiting tool don't currently have to worry about a federal algorithmic accountability law (one had been proposed under the prior Congress, but is now shelved). Similarly, a startup offering facial recognition services faces fewer federal constraints (though some states like Illinois have biometric privacy laws, there's no national equivalent to the EU's ban on real-time biometric ID systems). This freedom allows **fast go-to-market strategies** and the testing of business models that might be outright illegal elsewhere.

However, this booming landscape isn't without **concerns and potential pitfalls**. Some analysts caution that **lack of regulation can lead to short-term booms but long-term busts or crises**. For example, if many enterprises deploy unvetted AI and there are high-profile failures or harms (say, a biased AI causing a major scandal, or an autonomous vehicle crash due to lax rules), there could be a public backlash that forces abrupt regulation later, potentially harming well-intentioned startups alongside bad actors. There's also the question of **monopolies**: ironically, deregulation can sometimes entrench big players, as they are free to acquire or outcompete startups without antitrust intervention. The Trump FTC and DOJ have been less aggressive on tech antitrust than their predecessors, meaning Big Tech firms (Amazon, Google, Meta, etc.) can scoop up promising AI startups with fewer hurdles. This could eventually dampen the competitive environment that the policies initially foster. Nonetheless, as of now in 2025, the **narrative is overwhelmingly positive for U.S. AI entrepreneurs** -- they are "unleashed" and racing ahead, with Washington cheering them on to "innovate faster, break the rules of the old world, and keep America #1."

### AI and Crypto in Trump's Economic and Geopolitical Strategy

Trump's embracement of AI and cryptocurrency isn't just about technology for its own sake, it's deeply entwined with his broader **economic nationalism and geopolitical strategy**. In his second term, Trump has been leveraging these tools as part of a vision to reorder global economic relations in America's favour. Key elements of this strategy include **tariff-based diplomacy, dollar repatriation, and strategic use of digital assets**, often drawing on unorthodox economic theories. Here we analyse this strategy, with insights from economists like **Yanis Varoufakis** who have examined Trump's approach:

### Tariff-Based Diplomacy and "America First" Economics

Trump has doubled down on using tariffs and trade pressures as leverage to achieve economic goals. His view, as interpreted by Varoufakis, is that tariffs are not merely about protecting industries, they are a **weapon to realign global capital flows**. In essence, Trump's team is attempting a two-pronged macroeconomic strategy: **devalue the U.S. dollar** (to boost exports and domestic manufacturing competitiveness) *while simultaneously* **maintaining the dollar's dominance as the world's reserve currency** (to keep financing costs low for U.S. debt). Normally, these goals conflict -- a weaker dollar might prompt investors to flee to other currencies, undermining dollar hegemony. But Trump's approach tries to square the circle by using **tariffs and negotiations to pressure allies and adversaries to hold more U.S. debt and assets even as the dollar declines in value**. For example, Trump has reimposed or threatened high tariffs on countries with large dollar reserves (like China, Japan, South Korea, Germany), effectively telling them: *accept a reduced trade surplus with the U.S. and reinvest your dollars in American assets, or face trade pain.* The administration believes that by forcing other countries to buy U.S. exports and especially U.S. Treasury bonds, it can have its cake and eat it too -- a cheaper dollar helping U.S. factories, and foreign money still funding Washington's deficits cheaply. As Varoufakis explains, *"Tariffs, in this context, are the chief weapon"* to coerce nations to *"unload their dollar holdings"* in ways favourable to the U.S.. Indeed, we've seen Trump engage in hardball negotiations: with **Japan and Korea**, he's linked tariff exemptions to those countries investing in U.S. infrastructure bonds and tech projects. With **China**, he's hinted tariffs will persist unless China not only buys more U.S. goods (as in the Phase One trade deal redux) but also refrains from dumping U.S. Treasury securities. This strategy has a transactional logic consistent with Trump's style -- treating global trade like a series of deals where the U.S. leverages its import market and financial clout for concessions.

### Crypto as a Tool for Dollar Repatriation

The most novel aspect is Trump's use of **cryptocurrency in international finance**, something no previous U.S. administration entertained. After calling Bitcoin a "scam" years ago, Trump surprised many by **establishing a federal crypto reserve** and encouraging the Fed to accumulate crypto assets. Varoufakis interprets this as part of Trump's plan to **soak up excess dollars globally without strengthening rival currencies**.

**Here's how it works:**

Many U.S. trading partners hold vast dollar reserves (e.g. over \$1 trillion by Japan alone). If those dollars are unloaded, they usually would go into euros or yuan, boosting those currencies' stature. Trump's goal is to coax or coerce these countries to instead convert a chunk of their dollar holdings into **cryptocurrencies or U.S.-issued digital assets**. Since crypto (like Bitcoin) isn't tied to any one country's economy, if Japan swaps, say, \$100 billion into Bitcoin, the dollar's value might fall (achieving the devaluation aim) but Japan hasn't switched to using euros or yuan (so the dollar stays dominant as the benchmark). Moreover, by having the U.S. government itself hold a strategic stash of Bitcoin, **the U.S. can exert some influence over the crypto market** or at least benefit if global demand for crypto rises. The March 6, 2025 executive order that Trump signed, instructing the Fed to never sell but only hold crypto, is essentially taking those assets out of circulation, possibly to drive up scarcity/value. It's a startling development: the U.S. treating Bitcoin and other cryptos as strategic reserves akin to oil or gold.

### Stablecoins and Digital Dollar Diplomacy

Even more intriguingly, Trump voiced strong support on March 10, 2025 for **U.S. dollar-backed stablecoins**, signalling that the U.S. might integrate these into its financial diplomacy. Stablecoins (like USDC or Tether) are crypto tokens pegged 1:1 to the dollar. Trump's endorsement, as Varoufakis notes, *"added a new dimension"* to forcing foreign investors into moves that serve the U.S. plan. By promoting regulated U.S. stablecoins, Trump's administration is effectively saying: rather than hold your dollars in bank accounts or convert to foreign currency, buy U.S.-issued stablecoins. Those stablecoins still represent dollars (maintaining dollar hegemony), but since they circulate in crypto markets, they can absorb liquidity outside traditional forex channels. In negotiations, U.S. officials have subtly pushed this idea: for instance, if a country is accumulating too many dollars from trade, the U.S. might suggest they purchase U.S. stablecoins or digital treasury tokens with those dollars (sometimes even dangling tariff relief as an incentive). By doing so, the dollars are essentially locked in a digital form that the U.S. can monitor or influence. Another aspect is **digital trade deals**: the U.S. is exploring bilateral agreements where, say, an ally like **India** or **Israel** (named as priorities in replacing Biden's export rule) gets access to U.S. AI or defence tech, and in return they hold more U.S. digital assets or adopt U.S. tech standards. Stablecoins could be part of such deals e.g., encouraging partner countries' central banks or companies to use U.S. stablecoins for cross-border transactions, thereby extending dollar dominance into the crypto realm. All of this underscores what Varoufakis describes as Trump's *"broader transactionalism and deal-making"* approach, using every lever (tariffs, tech access, currency, crypto) to extract concessions.

### Domestic Capital Repatriation and Investment

On the home front, Trump has continued efforts to **repatriate capital** and stimulate domestic investment, this time using both traditional incentives and new crypto channels. Building on the 2017 tax reform (which brought back some offshore cash by lowering tax rates), Trump in 2025 proposed additional tax breaks for companies that invest in U.S. AI and manufacturing. There's talk of a targeted "**Repatriation 2.0**" policy where profits parked abroad can return tax-free if funnelled into certain industries (like semiconductor fabs, AI research centres, or infrastructure). In parallel, by embracing crypto, Trump has tacitly given a green light for U.S. dollars stored in foreign crypto exchanges or shadow banking to flow back into American markets via digital asset channels. For instance, wealthy individuals or entities overseas holding significant cryptocurrency (or dollars in offshore accounts) might convert them to the new U.S. Digital Asset Stockpile bonds or Fed-issued digital tokens if they believe Trump's policies will favour crypto value and stability. The administration is effectively creating **new mechanisms for capital inflow**: one could convert foreign-held dollars into a U.S. government-sanctioned stablecoin or digital bond and be part of this strategic reserve system. Unlike traditional treasury bonds, these might be marketed with a tech twist -- potentially even offering yields in crypto terms. While these details are still emerging, the clear goal is to capture global liquidity and direct it into funding U.S. priorities (be it the deficit, infrastructure, or tech innovation).

### Economic Analysis and Risks (Varoufakis' Perspective)

Yanis Varoufakis, the economist and former Greek finance minister, provides a critical lens on Trump's gambit. He notes the **inherent contradictions and potential pitfalls**. For one, forcing others to buy U.S. bonds or stablecoins via tariffs is a form of economic coercion that could breed resentment and retaliation. Countries might comply in the short term but seek to diversify away from the dollar over the longer term, especially if they feel "blackmailed." Also, the strategy to devalue the dollar while selling more U.S. debt relies on delicate timing, if miscalculated, it could spook markets and cause either a dollar crash or a spike in U.S. interest rates. Varoufakis points out the odd logic of the crypto reserve: *"if you own the printing presses of the world's reserve currency, what's the point of hoarding crypto that you can't print?"*. In other words, the U.S. can always print dollars (and indeed has that power which underpins its influence), so accumulating Bitcoin (which is finite and not yield-bearing) is puzzling unless one sees it as a geopolitical signal or a way to manipulate perceptions. He suggests part of it is **"self-serving bluster and trolling"** e.g., Trump profited from hyping crypto (pocketing tens of millions from a coin issuance) and enjoyed thumbing his nose at the establishment by elevating a currency favoured by "libertarians, cranks and criminals" to federal status. But Varoufakis concedes there is *"one part strategy"* in it: that strategy being to *weaponize* crypto to reinforce a dollar-centric system under new terms. For example, by dominating the stablecoin space, the U.S. might maintain the dollar as the anchor of global value even if trust in U.S. Treasury bonds wavers, people might trust a well-regulated digital token redeemable in dollars instead, which is still essentially the dollar.

Varoufakis also describes how, during negotiations with other governments, U.S. officials might drop *"subtle hints"* that if those countries' investors buy into U.S.-supported stablecoins or digital assets, it would please President Trump and perhaps lead to tariff leniency. This almost mafioso style *quid pro quo* ("buy our coins or face our tariffs") is unprecedented in international finance. If it succeeds, it could indeed expand U.S. influence: for instance, say **Saudi Arabia** is hinted to keep oil prices and trade in dollars, and in return they are encouraged to invest surplus petrodollars into a U.S. infrastructure coin or Bitcoin reserve rather than Chinese projects, that keeps the loop closed in the dollar sphere. But the **risks** are manifold: unstable crypto markets could introduce volatility; other major powers (like the EU, China) might accelerate their own digital currency alternatives to avoid being drawn into Trump's scheme (the EU could push the digital euro, China its CBDC, as counters). Additionally, domestic use of tariffs as a constant threat can disrupt global supply chains and raise costs for American consumers, potentially hurting the economy Trump seeks to boost.

### Tariffs and AI Geopolitics

It's worth noting how AI itself factors into the tariff diplomacy. The Biden administration in 2023 had introduced an **"AI diffusion rule"**, export controls on advanced AI chips to places like China and broad categories of countries. Trump's team has **signalled it will replace that rule with a "simpler" framework**, likely meaning a more unilateral, deal-by-deal approach rather than blanket restrictions. Analysts suspect Trump will use high-end chip exports as a bargaining chip: for example, allowing certain countries access to American AI hardware or software if they align with U.S. interests (such as banning Huawei/Chinese tech, or supporting U.S. positions in international forums). Conversely, nations not toeing the line could find themselves cut off from crucial AI technology. This is an AI-era extension of tariff diplomacy, using **technology access as leverage**, which in some cases might complement tariffs or replace them. For instance, with India, Trump may relax restrictions and co-develop AI defence tech (as hinted by a joint statement in Feb 2025 committing the U.S. and India to co-develop new defence technologies) to pull India closer, forming a bloc against China's AI rise. With European allies, however, such tactics have limits, hence friction remains over rules rather than trade war, because slapping tariffs on EU goods while also needing their cooperation is a tricky balance.

# Future Scenarios: Global AI Governance and Education in 2030 if Trends Continue

Projecting current trajectories into the future, we can imagine a transformed landscape for global AI governance and education by the end of this decade. If the trends seen under Trump's second administration persist (or intensify), the world of 2030 might be characterized by **greater divergence in tech governance, a reordered global AI power structure, and significant consequences for education systems worldwide**. Below is a forward-looking scenario based on continuation of these trends:

### Fragmented Global AI Governance

By 2030, the world has not settled on a single cohesive approach to governing AI instead, distinct blocs have hardened:

The **United States** leads a bloc of largely laissez-faire AI adopters. Domestically, the U.S. still lacks comprehensive AI regulation; voluntary industry standards and state laws patch certain gaps (like California's algorithmic transparency law, or New York's hiring AI bias audit rule), but at the federal level innovation remains prioritized over regulation. The U.S. government positions itself as the **champion of free-market AI** and continues to resist binding international agreements that might constrain its tech sector or military uses of AI. Allies such as the UK, Israel, and some parts of Southeast Asia gravitate towards this model, attracted by its promise of rapid innovation and economic benefits. These countries cooperate loosely on AI R&D and perhaps form a tech alliance (an "Innovation Coalition") focused on countering the Chinese sphere and outpacing the EU in AI development. They share AI resources among themselves with minimal restriction, freely exchanging AI research and possibly aligning on lower data protection standards to allow large-scale data sharing for AI training. The guiding belief in this bloc is that **AI progress equals geopolitical strength** and thus any self-imposed constraint could mean falling behind. By 2030, this has resulted in **breakneck advancements**: AI is deeply embedded in these societies (from autonomous vehicles on roads to AI assistants in government services), but also a series of *scandals and crises* (some predictable, like privacy breaches or algorithmic discrimination and some unforeseen, like an autonomous system malfunction causing harm). These crises, however, have not yet dissuaded the bloc's leadership from their path; solutions are sought in *more tech* (e.g. better AI to fix problems caused by earlier AI) rather than regulation.

The **European Union** in 2030 stands as the alternative model: a heavily regulated, rights-focused AI regime. After rolling out the AI Act in the mid-2020s, the EU doubled down with additional legislation like an AI Liability Act (making it easier to sue for AI harms), strong enforcement of transparency and fairness rules, and even a *moratorium on certain uses* (like autonomous lethal weapons or social scoring, which the EU tried to push globally but only some countries adopted). Europe's approach is often dubbed **"Ethical AI"** governance. By 2030, European consumers enjoy robust protections: AI systems interacting with them must disclose AI involvement, face recognition in public is largely banned, and strict data privacy is the norm. This has somewhat slowed deployment of AI in Europe, many AI products undergo lengthy conformity checks or were adapted to comply with EU rules (for instance, generative AI models in Europe have filters and training data disclosures). European tech companies sometimes complain they are at a competitive disadvantage, but the EU points to higher public trust in AI as a strength (European AI systems have fewer high-profile failures or lawsuits, because many risky systems never reach the market). The EU continues to push its standards internationally, finding some uptake in like-minded governments (perhaps Canada, Japan to an extent, and parts of Latin America) who sign onto EU-led AI governance partnerships. However, the **transatlantic gap remains wide**: the U.S. and EU frequently clash in trade forums over digital standards, each arguing the other's approach harms the global economy. Some multinational companies have effectively split their AI operations: one division making "EU-compliant" versions of products, another making faster-evolving versions for the U.S./rest-of-world, reflecting a **bifurcated AI market**.

### China and Authoritarian States

Meanwhile, China has pursued its own path, emphasizing AI for state control and economic growth, with strict government oversight but not in the interest of individual rights, rather in the interest of regime stability. By 2030 China leads in certain AI areas (mass surveillance, smart cities, AI-driven manufacturing) and exports its model to allied or client states. A number of developing countries in Asia and Africa use Chinese AI systems for public security or censorship. The world thus effectively has **three major AI governance spheres**: U.S.-led (liberal-market, innovation-centric), EU-led (democratic-regulatory, rights-centric), and China-led (authoritarian, state-centric). These spheres struggle to find common ground in international bodies. Attempts at a **global AI governance framework** under the UN or G20 remain stymied. Meetings produce generic principle documents but no binding treaty. The Council of Europe's AI Treaty, for example, did come into existence by late 2020s, but the U.S., China, and even some of CoE's own members (like the UK) refused to join, limiting its reach. The "Bletchley Process" (from the 2023 summit in the UK) continued annually but turned into more of a venue for airing differences than resolving them.

### AI Arms Race and Geopolitical Consequences

If Trump's policies persist through the decade (or successors maintain a similar stance), the **military AI arms race** will likely escalate. By 2030, the U.S. military has deployed swarms of autonomous drones, AI-assisted cyber defences, and perhaps **space-based AI surveillance**. Russia and China, not to be outdone, have fielded their own autonomous weapon systems, with China especially narrowing some technology gaps. The lack of arms control agreements on AI is a casualty of U.S. withdrawal from talks, has led to a precarious situation reminiscent of the early Cold War nuclear arms race, but in the AI context. **Accidents or near-misses** may have already occurred: e.g., an American lethal drone making an unplanned strike due to a sensor glitch, or an AI-driven Chinese defence system misidentifying an aircraft, with both incidents fortunately contained, yet ringing alarm bells. Without mutual confidence-building measures, each side's AI missteps are viewed with suspicion by the other, fuelling **further military AI build-up**. One positive note: by 2030 there might be an informal norm, pushed by concerned scientists and some diplomats, that AI systems shouldn't control nuclear arsenals -- major powers likely keep human oversight on strategic weapons (out of fear of catastrophic error). But conventional warfare AI is fair game, creating a constant risk of inadvertent escalation if AI systems misinterpret actions. Smaller nations too could get a hold of advanced AI weapons (especially if the tech proliferates via open-source or black market), potentially destabilizing regional balances.

## Education Systems and Inequality

In education, the consequences of Trump's sustained policies (and those following his template) are coming into full view by 2030, particularly in the U.S. **American education has undergone a privatization and technologization wave.** With the federal Department of Education effectively hollowed out or abolished, control over education is almost entirely state and local. Many states increased school choice programs; public school funding in some areas dwindled without federal supplements, leading to more school closures or consolidations in low-income and rural areas. To fill the gap, **AI-powered education technology** rushed in. By 2030, it's common in the U.S. for students, especially in under-resourced districts, to learn from AI tutoring programs on tablets or VR headsets, with far fewer human teachers per student than a decade prior. Some states have contracts with ed-tech companies where an "AI teacher" is provided for certain subjects (say, a virtual math tutor across all middle schools). The quality of these AI educators varies. The best ones, often developed in partnership with top universities and constantly updated, can provide personalized learning and have improved standardized test performance in some districts. However, **without strong guidelines**, other systems turned out to reinforce biases (for instance, early on some AI tutors steered girls away from advanced math due to biased training data, until that was caught) or simply lacked the socio-emotional element of teaching. Wealthier families increasingly opt for private human schooling or premium AI-human hybrid tutoring (the equivalent of hiring an expensive tutor who uses AI tools to supercharge instruction). Thus, a serious concern is that **educational inequality deepened**: affluent students get the best of both worlds, cutting-edge AI tools plus rich human mentorship, while poorer students might be left with an AI as their primary instructor in overcrowded classrooms or fully virtual schools.

## Social and Workforce Impacts

The generation growing up in this 2025-2030 period experiences education and entry into the workforce in profoundly different ways. On one hand, they are quite tech-savvy and comfortable interacting with AI from a young age (AI bots as reading buddies, etc.). On the other hand, there are reported deficits in interpersonal skills and critical thinking for some students who had heavy AI usage but limited real teacher guidance. The lack of DEI programs (eliminated under Trump) also means that schools largely stopped proactive inclusion efforts. By 2030, college campuses and high schools may see **a resurgence of social tensions**, some students feel that curricula have been "whitewashed" or key perspectives omitted due to the chilling effect of anti-DEI policies. Indeed, as early as 2025-2026, lawsuits and student protests emerged over the banning of diverse literature or ethnic studies programs (which were curtailed under the broad sweep of Trump's orders against anything that could be seen as affirmative action). # Australian State Approaches to Mobile Phone and AI Use in Schools: A National Policy Landscape Review

In this section we explore Australian state approach to regulating student mobile phone use and integrating artificial intelligence (AI) tools in school education. It considers both public and private sectors and includes commentary on emerging federal legislation around social media access for children. South Australia\'s leadership on digital safety policy is highlighted, with a comparison to the slower response of the Vocational Education and Training (VET) sector.

New South Wales (NSW) NSW implemented a phased approach to banning mobile phones. Public primary schools enforced a ban as early as 2018, while public high schools followed suit from Term 4 2023. Catholic schools in Sydney have aligned with these bans. Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT were initially blocked in public schools due to concerns around plagiarism and data security. However, by late 2023, NSW had aligned with the national framework allowing AI in schools under ethical guidance.

Victoria (VIC) Victoria led the country with a full public school phone ban in 2020. It was the first state to remove restrictions on ChatGPT, allowing its use from Term 2 2023 under broad ethical use guidelines. Schools are now expected to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum.

Queensland (QLD) QLD adopted a state-wide phone ban from Term 1 2024, following school-level variations previously. Initially cautious about AI, QLD has since launched state-wide use of adaptive learning AI tools and endorsed the national AI framework. Schools now receive guidance to use generative AI in classrooms with oversight.

Western Australia (WA) WA implemented a phone ban in 2020, requiring primary students to surrender devices and secondary students to keep them out of sight. WA is now exploring AI tools to support teacher workload, including lesson planning and marking automation, while developing a cautious approach to generative AI use.

South Australia (SA) SA enforced a public-school phone ban in Term 3 2023. Unlike other states, SA never banned ChatGPT and developed its own AI tool, EdChat, which was piloted in schools with privacy safeguards. SA has been at the forefront of AI integration in education. Premier Peter Malinauskas also led the national push for restricting under-16s from social media, influencing new federal legislation.

Tasmania (TAS) TAS implemented a public school phone ban in 2020. Initially blocking ChatGPT, the state is now preparing to introduce AI under the national framework with professional development and updated teaching resources.

Australian Capital Territory (ACT) ACT banned phones for students up to Year 10 starting Term 1 2024, with Year 11--12 students allowed limited use. ACT took a cautious approach to AI, emphasizing teacher training and ethical integration.

Northern Territory (NT) NT banned mobile phones in public schools from Term 1 2023. Details on AI policy are less visible, but the territory is expected to follow the national framework and ensure access in remote areas.

Comparison with VET Sector Unlike K--12 schools, the VET sector lacks a uniform mobile phone policy. VET learners are considered adults, and phone use is governed by individual codes of conduct. While some TAFEs have issued AI policies (e.g., TAFE NSW), most of the sector lags behind the school system in adopting a coordinated approach to AI literacy and integration.

# Australia's Current Response: ASQA and TEQSA on AI

## ASQA's Efforts to Address AI in VET

As the national regulator for VET, ASQA has begun to respond to AI-related issues, but its approach to date has been cautious and primarily focused on the **academic integrity risks** posed by AI. In its recent public communications, ASQA has acknowledged that the use of generative AI by students (for example, using tools like ChatGPT to write assignments) represents a major challenge to assessment authenticity. Indeed, **"academic cheating"** involving AI has been identified as a regulatory risk priority for 2024--25, signalling ASQA's intent to scrutinize how providers maintain assessment integrity in the face of AI tools (ASQA, 2024). In newsletters and guidance to Registered Training Organisations (RTOs), ASQA has warned that easier access to AI technology and contract cheating services could **normalize academic dishonesty** if not addressed, potentially damaging the reputation of the VET sector (ASQA, 2024). ASQA expects RTOs to have robust systems to ensure authenticity of student work, echoing the existing requirement in the Standards for RTOs that assessment submissions are valid and the student's own. In practical terms, ASQA has advised providers to consider **redesigning assessments** (where possible) to make cheating via AI more difficult, for instance by incorporating more verbal presentations, practical demonstrations, or supervised assessments instead of sole reliance on take-home written tasks. This aligns with steps seen overseas, though ASQA's guidance so far has been advisory rather than mandatory.

In April 2025, ASQA released one of its most detailed pieces of guidance on AI to date in an edition of *ASQA IQ* (the regulator's integrity and quality bulletin). This edition discussed "how providers can manage artificial intelligence (AI) use to safeguard quality and integrity in the VET sector" and also **the positive uses of AI** that can benefit students and providers (ASQA, 2025). This marks a notable evolution in tone, moving from treating AI purely as a threat to also recognizing it as an opportunity. ASQA's guidance in 2025 suggests RTOs adopt a two-pronged approach: **mitigate the risks** of AI misuse and **embrace the benefits** of AI for improving training delivery. On the risk mitigation side, ASQA emphasizes principles of fairness, flexibility, and validity in assessment when AI is in the picture. Concretely, ASQA advises that if AI tools are used in training or assessment, all students should have equal access to them to avoid disadvantaging those with lower digital literacy or resources (principle of *Fairness*) -- and providers should have clear policies about reassessment or consequences if integrity issues involving AI arise (ASQA, 2025). To uphold *Validity*, ASQA reiterates that competency decisions must be based on evidence of the learner's own performance, meaning an AI-generated answer cannot be accepted as demonstration of competence without further verification (ASQA, 2025). Regarding *Flexibility*, ASQA echoes the idea of varied assessment methods (practical, oral, observational) to capture skills that AI cannot fabricate and to accommodate different learning styles in an AI-influenced environment.

On the positive side, ASQA acknowledges that AI, when used appropriately, can **enhance VET delivery**. The April 2025 guidance notes that AI can support personalized learning and help optimize theoretical training components (e.g. adaptive learning platforms), automate routine administrative tasks (freeing up trainer time), and even identify at-risk students through learning analytics (ASQA, 2025). It encourages RTOs to consider how AI tools might increase accessibility for disadvantaged learners, for instance, AI-driven translation or text-to-speech tools can assist students with low literacy or those for whom English is a second language (ASQA, 2025). ASQA also points out that since many industries are adopting AI, there is an **emerging expectation** that vocational training will incorporate relevant AI technologies so that graduates are work-ready (ASQA, 2025). Therefore, completely avoiding AI in training may not be desirable; instead, the focus should be on *responsible integration*.

Despite these efforts, **gaps remain in ASQA's response**. To date, ASQA has not issued binding guidelines or specific regulatory requirements for RTOs regarding AI (e.g. there is no equivalent to TEQSA's directive for universities to submit AI action plans, discussed below). ASQA's Standards for RTOs (2015) do not yet explicitly mention AI, leaving a potential grey area in how providers should update their academic integrity policies. Compliance audits by ASQA have just begun to include questions about AI (such as how providers verify authenticity in an age of AI), but practices likely vary widely among RTOs. Moreover, ASQA's focus has been primarily on the assessment side; there is less evidence of guidance on, say, data privacy issues if RTOs use AI platforms, or on minimum quality standards for any AI-based training tools used by providers. In short, ASQA's approach, while increasingly aware and informative, **lacks a formalized framework or set of expectations** compared to some international counterparts. This reactive stance could leave Australian VET lagging in consistency and preparedness.

It is worth noting that ASQA itself, as an agency, is exploring the use of AI internally to improve its regulatory operations. ASQA published an "AI Transparency Statement" in 2023 committing to ethical and responsible use of AI in its own processes. ASQA plans to leverage AI for tasks like document summarization and data analysis to enhance its efficiency, but with a human-in-the-loop for all decisions (ASQA, 2023). This internal adoption signals that ASQA acknowledges AI's importance and it aligns with the Australian Government's broader AI use policy. However, internal use is separate from sector guidance, the latter is where Australian VET could benefit from clearer regulatory direction.

## TEQSA's Approach in Higher Education

The Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA), which regulates Australia's higher education sector (universities and other degree-awarding institutions), has been relatively **proactive and strategic** in addressing AI. TEQSA's approach can be characterized by early recognition of the academic integrity threat posed by generative AI and a coordinated effort to prompt institutions into action while sharing best practices across the sector.

By late 2022 and early 2023, as tools like ChatGPT emerged, TEQSA publicly highlighted the evolving risk of generative AI to academic integrity. In March 2023, TEQSA's Chief Commissioner wrote to all higher education providers emphasizing the need to uphold academic standards in the face of AI, and TEQSA published guidance materials acknowledging that a "wait-and-see approach will not suffice" given the serious and immediate risk of AI-facilitated cheating (TEQSA, 2023). TEQSA's messaging stressed that technological tools to detect AI-written text were uncertain, so universities should invest effort in **redesigning assessment and strengthening integrity controls**, rather than relying purely on detection.

A cornerstone of TEQSA's strategy was the issuance of a **sector-wide Request for Information (RFI)** in June 2024. TEQSA formally required all registered higher education providers to submit by July 2024 a "credible institutional action plan" detailing how they are addressing the risks that generative AI poses to academic integrity and assessment (TEQSA, 2024). This RFI, effectively a regulatory directive, ensured that every university and higher education college had to engage with the issue at the governing level. TEQSA specified that these action plans should be overseen by appropriate governance bodies within institutions (e.g. Academic Boards) and should cover measures to guard "award integrity" -- meaning that degrees should continue to reflect students' own learning. By early July 2024, all institutions had submitted their plans (TEQSA, 2024). TEQSA then used these responses to gauge the sector's readiness and identify areas needing more support or scrutiny. Notably, this approach by TEQSA is **more directive and encompassing** than any step taken by ASQA so far in VET.

In addition to compliance measures, TEQSA has functioned as a knowledge hub for best practices on AI in education. It set up a "generative AI" resource hub on its website, curating and disseminating guidance developed by both TEQSA and other bodies. TEQSA's resources include: a briefing paper on immediate steps to mitigate AI cheating (with suggestions like reviewing assessment design, updating academic integrity policies to mention AI, training staff to recognize AI outputs, and educating students on acceptable use); a detailed report *"Assessment Reform for the Age of Artificial Intelligence"* to inspire long-term shifts toward authentic assessment; and an array of links to external good-practice examples (for instance, guidelines from the University of Melbourne on adapting assessment in Semester 1 2023, and the Australian Academic Integrity Network's guidelines on generative AI use for students). TEQSA also hosted webinars and workshops, bringing together experts and practitioners to discuss responses to AI (TEQSA, 2024). All this signals TEQSA's role not just as an enforcer but as a facilitator of sector-wide learning and adaptation.

TEQSA's stance on AI is embedded within the existing Higher Education Standards Framework, which requires providers to maintain academic integrity and effective assessment design. While the standards did not originally contemplate AI, TEQSA has interpreted them in this new context, for example, by viewing undisclosed AI plagiarism as a breach of academic integrity policies that providers must police and considering whether assessment formats still validly measure learning outcomes when AI could be involved. If providers were found to be doing nothing about rampant AI cheating, TEQSA could conceivably find them non-compliant with the standards on assessment or integrity. Thus, TEQSA has implicitly raised expectations: doing nothing is not an option.

In terms of **outcomes**, TEQSA's pressure has led many universities to react quickly. By early 2023, a number of Australian universities modified their academic integrity policies to explicitly forbid using AI to write assignments unless permitted by the instructor. Several universities convened internal taskforces on AI in teaching and learning. Following the RFI in 2024, most institutions have either developed or are developing comprehensive AI governance policies, covering not only cheating, but also positive use of AI in teaching, support for staff to experiment with AI edtech and guidelines for research use of AI. TEQSA's sectoral overview will allow it to identify if any institutions are lagging and offer targeted guidance or regulatory intervention as needed.

TEQSA's approach contrasts with ASQA's in its **proactivity, breadth and formality**. TEQSA quickly set clear expectations that institutions *must actively respond* to generative AI, essentially mainstreaming the issue within quality assurance processes. It also sought to balance enforcement with empowerment, giving providers resources to craft solutions. This balanced, firm approach has positioned Australia's higher education sector to better cope with AI, though challenges remain and TEQSA continues to monitor developments (such as the effectiveness of the action plans and whether further regulatory changes are needed).

# Comparison: ASQA vs TEQSA and the Gaps in Australia's AI Response

The differing approaches of ASQA and TEQSA reveal a gap in Australia's overall response to AI in education. While TEQSA and the higher education sector have moved relatively quickly to set expectations and share practices regarding AI, the VET sector under ASQA has not yet seen an equivalent concerted effort. Key points of comparison include:

## Proactivity and Formal Requirements

TEQSA took an active stance by mandating action plans from universities on a set timeline, essentially forcing every institution to think through its AI strategy (TEQSA, 2024)[teqsa.gov.au](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/higher-education-good-practice-hub/artificial-intelligence/request-information-addressing-risk-artificial-intelligence#:~:text=To%20ensure%20higher%20education%20institutions,plan%E2%80%99%20in%20the%20Provider%20Portal). ASQA, in contrast, has so far issued guidance but *not* requirements. There is no evidence that ASQA has asked all RTOs to submit their plans or policies on AI, or that ASQA has audited RTOs specifically on AI readiness. The absence of a formal directive in VET could mean many RTOs remain complacent or unsure about how to proceed, whereas the higher education institutions have had to accelerate their efforts.

## Scope of Issues Addressed

TEQSA's communications and resources address a spectrum of concerns -- primarily academic integrity, but also the need for assessment reform, staff and student awareness, and even opportunities for using AI in teaching. ASQA's focus until recently was almost exclusively on academic cheating risks. Only in April 2025 did ASQA publicly discuss positive uses of AI and benefits to providers (ASQA, 2025)[app4.vision6.com.au](https://app4.vision6.com.au/em/message/email/view.php?id=1839440&a=41710&k=EYm4GI4-HrPJBZ7kFYCCW595dXEVYZtVuQ1Y0tHRR1g#:~:text=The%20April%202025%20edition%20of,bring%20to%20students%20and%20providers). This suggests the VET discourse is slightly behind in acknowledging that AI is not just a threat to police but also a wave of innovation to manage and leverage. Consequently, Australian universities might be piloting new AI-driven pedagogies or efficiency tools, while many RTOs may still be in a defensive mindset (concerned mostly with catching cheating, potentially by banning AI outright in assessments). Such a gap could lead to uneven adoption of AI innovation across tertiary education, with VET students missing out on learning with AI tools that university students might be exposed to.

## Guidance and Support

Both regulators have provided guidance, but TEQSA developed a more structured repository of good practices (the "Gen AI good practice hub") and worked with expert networks like the Australian Academic Integrity Network. ASQA's guidance has been more limited (a few newsletter articles and web pages). There is currently no dedicated ASQA toolkit for AI in VET akin to TEQSA's resources for higher education. Moreover, TEQSA's guidance has benefitted from input by academics and specialists in digital assessment (e.g. the CRADLE group at Deakin University collaborated on the *Assessment Reform for AI* resource). ASQA may not yet have similarly engaged VET pedagogy experts or EdTech experts to tailor advice for competency-based training environments. This could result in VET-specific challenges (for instance, verifying competency in workplace simulators that use AI) not being adequately addressed.

## Collaboration and Consistency

TEQSA's actions have spurred universities to share strategies among themselves (for example, the Group of Eight universities issued a joint statement on AI principles shortly after TEQSA's push, and universities are aligning on common approaches to citation of AI, etc.). In VET, where there are thousands of RTOs of varying size, the sector is more fragmented. Without strong prompts from ASQA or industry bodies, RTOs may develop inconsistent AI policies, some might ignore AI and some might ban it outright, while others might embrace it unsupported. This inconsistency was exactly the scenario Ireland noted and sought to avoid by top-down guidance. Australia's VET sector currently risks a patchwork of responses. A cohesive approach championed by the regulator is lacking, which is a gap considering ASQA's role is to ensure quality and consistency across providers.

## Regulatory Standards and Enforcement

The Higher Education Standards (which TEQSA enforces) are outcome-based and mention academic integrity and assessment validity, which TEQSA could directly tie to AI issues. The VET Quality Framework and RTO Standards also cover assessment validity and integrity, but enforcement in VET tends to be more compliance checklist-driven. It's unclear whether ASQA's auditors have been systematically checking if RTOs updated their academic misconduct policies to mention AI or if they have processes to detect AI usage. If not, then the risk is that some RTOs might be complacent, and students could be obtaining qualifications with significant undisclosed AI assistance -- undermining confidence in VET outcomes. ASQA has identified this risk, but the question is whether it will use its regulatory powers (e.g. specific compliance audits or directives) to ensure RTOs take action.

In essence, **Australia's overall response is uneven**: comparatively strong in universities, weaker in vocational training. This matters because employers and society expect that regardless of whether a student went through a university or an RTO, the credentials they earn reflect genuine competency. If one sector is perceived as addressing AI challenges more rigorously than the other, a disparity in trust could emerge. There is also efficiency to be gained by aligning efforts, many of the issues (academic cheating, need for teacher upskilling, data security) are common across sectors, so solutions can be shared.

The current gap presents an opportunity: ASQA can learn from TEQSA's head start, avoiding reinventing the wheel. Likewise, TEQSA could benefit from collaborating with ASQA since VET has its own insights (for instance, VET's experience with competency-based assessment might offer creative ideas for authentic assessment in higher ed, and vice versa). The next section outlines recommendations for how ASQA and TEQSA can work together and what steps they should consider to strengthen Australia's regulatory posture on AI in education.

# Recommendations for ASQA and TEQSA Collaboration on AI

Given the analysis, it is recommended that ASQA and TEQSA undertake a coordinated and urgent response to AI in tertiary education. Specific recommendations include:

## Joint AI Taskforce

Establish a formal ASQA-TEQSA taskforce or working group on AI in education. This inter-agency team would facilitate information sharing between the VET and higher education sectors and develop unified guidance. It should include representatives from both regulators, as well as experts from the sectors (e.g. TAFE directors, university academic integrity officers, educational technologists). A joint taskforce can ensure consistent principles across sectors seeking agreement on definitions of what constitutes AI-assisted plagiarism, or best practices for authentic assessment, while still respecting the differences in sectoral context. This body could also engage with state-based regulators (for schools) to share knowledge nationally.

## Unified Guidelines and Principles

Collaborate on creating a **set of national guidelines or principles for the ethical use of AI in education and training**, applicable to both VET and higher education. These could be modelled on international best practice like the UK Russell Group principles and Ireland's AI principles but adapted to Australian conditions. Topics should include: data privacy (e.g. advising providers not to feed sensitive student data into public AI tools without consent, echoing UK guidance); academic integrity (a clear stance that submitting AI-generated work as one's own is misconduct, in line with Japan's and others' approaches); transparency (educators and students should disclose when AI is used and to what extent); equity (ensuring all students have equal access to AI tools if they are used in learning); and human oversight (AI should augment, not replace, teacher and assessor roles in decision-making). Having a common set of expectations endorsed by both ASQA and TEQSA would send a strong signal to all education providers and could be published as a joint guidelines document or code of practice.

## Coordinated Academic Integrity Policies

Encourage or require that both universities and RTOs update their academic integrity and assessment policies to address AI in a consistent manner. ASQA can mirror TEQSA's RFI approach by asking RTOs to submit their policies or plans on managing AI risks (cheating and otherwise). Collaboration could involve developing a **template or toolkit** for AI policy that RTOs (especially smaller ones with less capacity) can adapt. This toolkit might include sample honour code statements about AI, examples of assessment redesign in various disciplines, and protocols for investigating AI-related misconduct. TEQSA's repository of examples from universities can be leveraged to help RTOs, and vice versa -- some TAFEs may have come up with innovations that universities could learn from (for instance, practical assessments in trades that inherently mitigate AI misuse).

## Shared Monitoring and Intelligence

The two regulators should share data and insights on AI-related compliance issues. For instance, TEQSA's analysis of university tip-offs or misconduct cases involving AI could inform ASQA's monitoring of VET (and ASQA has its own "tip-off" line for reporting cheating in RTOs). By pooling intelligence on how AI is being misused (or used) by students and providers, the regulators can identify common patterns, such as emerging "contract cheating" services that cater to both university and VET students using AI. A shared database of such risks would help both agencies act faster. This collaboration could extend to jointly developing detection or verification methods: for example, exploring technology solutions (acknowledging their limitations) or creating cross-sector peer review mechanisms for suspicious assessment submissions.

## Professional Development Initiatives

Facilitate joint training and professional development for educators in both sectors on AI. ASQA and TEQSA could co-sponsor workshops, webinars, or communities of practice for teachers, trainers, and assessors focused on effective use of AI in pedagogy and assessment. This might involve bringing in experts from countries like Finland or Singapore (who are leaders in teacher AI training) to share approaches. The regulators could also collaborate with bodies like TAFE Directors Australia, Universities Australia, and professional teacher associations to embed AI modules into teacher training and VET workforce development programs. By coordinating efforts, economies of scale can be achieved, for instance, an online course on "Ethical and Effective Use of AI in Education" could be developed once and offered broadly to both VET and higher ed instructors, rather than duplicate efforts.

## Assessment Innovation and Sandbox Trials

Both regulators should encourage a culture of assessment innovation in an AI age, possibly through "regulatory sandboxes" or pilot projects. In a collaborative approach, ASQA and TEQSA could jointly invite a group of volunteering institutions (universities, TAFEs, private RTOs) to experiment with new assessment models that incorporate AI (for example, allowing students to use AI in an assessment with certain rules, and then evaluating learning outcomes). Regulators can provide a safe environment for these trials by agreeing not to penalize any compliance variation for the duration of the pilot. The results could inform wider policy. This would mirror Japan's approach of pilot schools and aligns with the idea that guidelines should be "live documents" updated with learnings (as Ireland suggested).

## Policy Alignment and Standard Changes

In the medium term, ASQA and TEQSA should work with government to update formal standards to reflect AI. TEQSA is already interpreting the Higher Education Standards in light of AI; ASQA can start reviewing the Standards for RTOs (which are currently under revision for 2024+) with AI in mind. A joint recommendation could be made to include explicit references to digital tools or AI in the next iteration of VET quality standards. Adding a clause that providers must have and implement policies for the ethical use of emerging technologies (including AI) in assessment and training. This would future proof the regulatory framework. By presenting a united front, the regulators can more convincingly advocate for these changes to the Education Ministers and COAG Education Council.

## Communication and Consistency

Ensure consistent messaging to stakeholders. Government policymakers and the public should hear a coherent story from both ASQA and TEQSA about Australia's approach to AI in education: that we are treating it seriously, acting urgently but prudently, and aiming to harness benefits while protecting integrity. A collaborative approach might include joint press releases or joint appearances at conferences (for example, an ASQA and TEQSA representative co-presenting on AI at a national education forum). Consistent messaging will build confidence among employers and communities that qualifications, whether vocational or academic, remain credible in the AI era.

By taking these steps, ASQA and TEQSA can help close the gap between VET and higher education responses to AI and elevate Australia's overall readiness. Collaboration ensures that neither sector is left behind and that resources are efficiently used. It also sends a signal internationally that Australia is moving beyond a piecemeal approach and aligning with global best practice in a unified way.

# Future Directions and Conclusion

Artificial intelligence will only become more entrenched in education and training, and the pace of change is accelerating. Australia's regulators and policymakers must respond with urgency and foresight to avoid falling further behind international peers. The comparisons in this report illustrate that many jurisdictions have moved decisively: the UK and Ireland are promulgating principles and guidelines; Finland is mobilizing a national framework; Singapore and China are embedding AI into curricula; and the EU is even legislating standards for trustworthy AI. These actions recognize that while AI poses risks (especially to academic integrity), it also offers transformative potential for improving learning and administrative efficiency. The **challenge for Australia** is to strike the same balance, enabling innovation and adoption of AI in the VET sector, while safeguarding the quality and integrity that underpin qualifications.

ASQA's current efforts, focused on mitigating cheating, are a necessary start but not sufficient. A shift to a more comprehensive strategy is needed. This includes widening the lens to also facilitate positive uses of AI in vocational training (so Australian VET graduates are AI-capable and competitive) and addressing policy areas like data protection and equity. It also means moving from reactive advice to a more structured framework or requirements, as stakeholders will respond more strongly to clear standards. The involvement of TEQSA, with its proactive approach, is a valuable asset. By collaborating, ASQA can accelerate its learning curve, and both regulators can promote consistency.

Time is of the essence. Every academic term that passes without robust AI policies in place is a period in which students might gain qualifications through unethical means, or conversely, miss opportunities to learn with new tools. It also leaves educators anxious or unsure about what they are permitted or expected to do with AI. The urgency expressed by Ireland's advisory group, that the importance and urgency "cannot be overstated", rings true in Australia as well. We do not have the luxury of lengthy deliberations; policies must be iterative and adaptive, starting now and evolving as we gather experience.

Australia's VET sector stands at a crossroads with respect to AI. Will we lag behind, attempting to patch holes (chasing cheating incidents, for example) while other countries leap ahead in innovation? Or will we embrace a forward-looking, comprehensive approach that both **protects and advances** our education system in an AI-driven world? The recommendations in this report chart a path toward the latter -- a path of collaborative regulation, informed by global best practices, and attuned to the unique needs of our VET sector. By implementing these recommendations, ASQA, in partnership with TEQSA and other stakeholders, can ensure that Australia's regulatory response to AI is not only robust and rigorous but also enabling and future focused. This will maintain the confidence of employers and the community in VET qualifications and ensure Australian learners and training providers can safely reap the benefits of the AI revolution. The time to act is now, with urgency and unity of purpose.
