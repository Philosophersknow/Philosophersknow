## A2 — Day 3

**Subject:** The most common reason students get marked NYC

Hi {{first_name}},

Most students who receive a Not Yet Competent result knew the material.

That sounds like a contradiction, so here's what actually happens.

The assessment asks something specific:

> *"A client tells you she doesn't want a male support worker. How do you respond?"*

The student writes an accurate paragraph about person-centred practice, cultural respect and dignity. Every sentence is correct.

It contains no reference to **this client**, **this preference**, or **what they would actually do**.

Not Yet Competent.

The student didn't fail because they didn't know. They failed because they answered the general question instead of the specific one.

**The fix is a checklist you run over your answer before you submit:**

1. Have I named the person or situation in the question, or have I written about the topic in general?
2. Have I said what I would **do**, in order — not just what I'd consider?
3. Have I cited something specific? Not "relevant legislation" — the actual Act
4. Have I said who I'd tell, and when?
5. Have I said what I'd write down?

Items 4 and 5 are omitted more often than anything else, across every qualification I've worked with. Escalation and documentation. If your answer doesn't have them, add them.

That's not a study technique. It's answering the question that was asked.

## A3 — Day 7

**Subject:** What your assessor is working from

Hi {{first_name}},

Your assessor isn't marking on impression. They're working from a marking guide with specific criteria, and it's not a secret document — you just don't get handed it.

Here's what's consistently in it.

**Knowledge evidence.** Specific things you must demonstrate you know. Often a number: "at least four", "minimum of six". If the criterion says four and you give three, that's a gap regardless of how good the three are.

**Performance evidence.** What you must be able to do, usually demonstrated or described in a scenario.

**Application.** Whether you connected the knowledge to the situation. This is where most NYCs happen.

**Two things the guide almost always includes and students almost always miss:**

- **Escalation** — who you'd report to and when. Assessors look for this specifically because in real workplaces, failing to escalate is how harm happens
- **Documentation** — what you'd record, in what form. "I'd document it" is weak. "I'd record the time, my exact observations, the client's words verbatim, and who I notified" is strong

**One thing worth knowing:** on ethical questions, presenting both sides and stopping is not balance. It reads as avoidance and it fails.

If a scenario involves a landlord asking you to discriminate, a supervisor directing unsafe work, or a vendor asking you to conceal a material fact — **take a position**. The law does, and your assessor is checking whether you know that.

## A4 — Day 14

**Subject:** Is reading worked examples cheating?

Hi {{first_name}},

I get asked this, so I'll answer it directly.

**No — and here's the line.**

Reading a worked example to understand what a competent response looks like is exactly what it's for. That's how every professional field teaches judgement. Law students read judgments. Medical students read case presentations. Apprentice carpenters watch someone else set out a roof before they set out their own.

**Copying a worked example into your assessment is a different thing entirely**, and I'd ask you not to.

Not primarily because you'll get caught, though RTOs run similarity checks and assessors notice work that doesn't match a student's demonstrated ability.

The real reason: you'll arrive at a job without the competency your certificate says you have.

In the areas we cover, that gap isn't academic. It's the difference between a support worker who recognises a client deteriorating and one who doesn't. A carpenter who spots an unbraced roof frame. A cook who understands why reheating won't make that soup safe. A mechanic who reads the fuel trims instead of replacing the part named in the fault code.

Someone eventually depends on you knowing the thing.

**So use the examples this way:** read the scenario, write your own answer, compare, find the gap, understand why it matters. Then write your own answer for your actual assessment.

That's the whole method, and it works.

## B2 — Day 3

**Subject:** Three gaps that come up repeatedly

Hi {{first_name}},

Three patterns that show up consistently in training and assessment tools, in rough order of how often.

**1. Assessment tools that don't cover the full unit.**

The tool assesses the elements and performance criteria and misses part of the knowledge evidence, or covers knowledge evidence and under-assesses performance. Usually because the tool was built from the elements rather than from the full unit of competency including the assessment requirements.

**The check:** map every assessment task back to the assessment requirements document, not just the unit. If something in the knowledge evidence isn't assessed anywhere, that's a gap.

**2. Insufficient evidence of application.**

The tool asks knowledge questions and calls it done. The unit requires the student to *do* something, or demonstrate they could, and there's no task that gets at it.

This is particularly common in units where practical demonstration is awkward to arrange, which is exactly where it matters most.

**3. Version control and currency.**

The training package was revised. The resources weren't. Or they were, but there's no record of when, by whom, or what changed — so at validation you can't demonstrate the process.

**The check that catches this fastest:** pick three resources at random and try to establish, from the documentation alone, when each was last reviewed and against what. If you can't, an auditor can't either.

None of these are exotic. They're what happens when resources are built once by someone competent and then not maintained, which describes most resources in most RTOs.

## B3 — Day 7

**Subject:** What a scope addition actually involves

Hi {{first_name}},

Scope additions are more work than most providers plan for, and the surprise is rarely the application itself.

**What you're demonstrating:**

- **Trainers and assessors** with the required vocational competency, current industry skills, and the training and assessment credential — with evidence, not assertion
- **Training and assessment strategies** for each qualification, covering the target group, delivery mode, duration, assessment approach and pathways
- **Assessment tools** that validly assess the full unit
- **Facilities and equipment** appropriate to the qualification. This is where practical qualifications get expensive
- **Industry consultation** evidencing that your strategy reflects current industry practice — dated, documented, and specific

**Where providers actually get caught:**

**Trainer currency evidence.** Having a qualified trainer isn't the issue. Being able to *demonstrate* their industry currency with dated evidence is where files fall apart.

**Industry consultation that's too thin.** A single email exchange from two years ago doesn't establish that your strategy reflects current practice.

**Assessment tools built for a superseded version** of the unit.

**Timeframe:** allow considerably longer than the nominal processing period, because the clock effectively restarts each time you're asked for more information.

The domestic delivery requirement — that a provider must be delivering to domestic students before CRICOS registration — has been formally codified, and the framework now includes provisions dealing with providers who register and don't subsequently deliver.

**What that means practically:**

CRICOS isn't a fast pivot. If it's in your plan, the domestic delivery clock is something to start deliberately rather than incidentally — and the evidence of that delivery needs to be real and documented, not nominal.

**The things providers underestimate:**

**Financial viability scrutiny** is deeper than domestic registration. Providers are regularly surprised.

**Student support infrastructure** — welfare, accommodation, under-18 arrangements where applicable — is genuine organisational change, not a policy document.

**Ongoing compliance load.** CRICOS is a continuing obligation with reporting requirements, not a one-off approval.

The useful question isn't "can we get registered." It's "can we sustain it once we are" — and a provider that answers the second honestly is in a much better position when they come to the first.

## C2 — Day 3

**Subject:** What Jisc found

Hi {{first_name}},

Jisc's framework for leading AI in colleges organises the problem into three pillars: **skills and knowledge, technology, and governance.**

The ordering matters, and I think they've got it right.

**Skills and knowledge first**, because the constraint in almost every college I've looked at is staff confidence rather than tool availability. The tools are largely free. The capability to use them well, and the confidence to try, is not.

**Technology second**, because a procurement decision made before you understand what you need produces an expensive tool nobody uses.

**Governance throughout**, not as a final step. Colleges that write their AI policy after an incident write it under pressure, defensively, and usually too restrictively.

**Three things worth checking in your own college:**

1. Do staff know what they're **permitted** to use? Uncertainty produces either avoidance or undisclosed use, and the second is worse
2. Is there anywhere **low-stakes** for staff to experiment? Adoption requires trying things that don't work
3. Is your position on **assessment integrity** written down and communicated to students?

If the answer to any is no, that's a more productive place to spend the next term than a procurement conversation.

## C3 — Day 7

**Subject:** The Ofsted signals

Hi {{first_name}},

Ofsted's early-adopter study on AI in FE put it directly: the biggest risk is doing nothing.

That's an unusual position for an inspectorate, and it's worth taking seriously.

**The reasoning:** students are already using these tools. Employers are already using them. A provider with no position hasn't chosen neutrality — it has devolved the decision to individual staff and students without guidance, consistency or safeguards.

**What I'd take from it, practically:**

**Have a position and be able to articulate it.** Not necessarily a permissive one. A considered restrictive position is defensible; an absent one isn't.

**Be able to show it's operating.** A policy nobody has read isn't a position, and the difference is visible.

**Know where your assessment integrity risk actually sits.** It's not evenly distributed. Practical and observed assessment is largely unaffected. Written work produced off-site is where the exposure is.

**Don't build your approach on detection.** Detection tools have real false positive rates and accusing a student incorrectly is a serious matter. Assessment design is a more robust answer than surveillance.

## C4 — Day 14

**Subject:** One thing to do this week

Hi {{first_name}},

A specific, small suggestion rather than a strategy.

**Appoint an AI lead.**

Not a new role, not a recruitment exercise. Give the responsibility to someone who already works for you, name them, and give them a modest amount of protected time.

**Why this is the highest-return single action:**

**It creates a destination for questions.** At the moment, staff with an AI question have nobody to ask, so they either guess or avoid it.

**It makes the work someone's job.** Everything that is everyone's responsibility is nobody's. AI governance is currently sitting across quality, MIS, curriculum and safeguarding, and falling between them.

**It gives you a position to point at.** When an inspector, a governor, or an employer asks who's leading this, you have an answer.

**It's reversible and cheap.** If it doesn't work you've lost very little.

**Who to pick:** someone curious rather than someone senior. The capability required is willingness to learn and credibility with colleagues. A respected teacher with genuine interest will outperform a director who's been handed it.

**What to give them:** a few hours a week, a clear remit, and permission to say "I don't know yet."
