## The problem

You've read the learner guide. You understand the topic. You write what you think is a solid answer.

It comes back Not Yet Competent.

The feedback says something like *"needs more detail"* or *"apply to the scenario"* — which is accurate and completely unactionable if you don't already know what that means.

Here's what it usually means:

| What you wrote | What the assessor wanted |
|---|---|
| A correct explanation of the principle | The principle **applied to the person in the question** |
| "I would follow relevant legislation" | The **name of the Act** and what it actually requires |
| The right conclusion | The **reasoning that gets there** |
| What you'd consider | What you'd **do**, in order |
| — | **Who you'd tell, and when** |
| — | **What you'd write down** |

Those last two are missing from more failed assessments than anything else.

---

## What we do about it

Every unit guide shows you a **full worked example** — a realistic assessment scenario, a response that would be marked Competent, and an examiner annotation explaining exactly why it works.

Where it helps, we also include a response that **isn't** good enough. Not a strawman — a plausible answer, the kind students actually write, with an explanation of the specific gap that produced the Not Yet Competent.

That comparison is the thing. Reading a good answer tells you what good looks like. Reading a good answer next to a nearly-good one tells you where the line is.

## Is this cheating?

No — and here's the line.

**Reading a worked example to understand what a competent response looks like** is exactly what it's for. Law students read judgments. Medical students read case presentations. Apprentices watch someone else do it first.

**Copying one into your assessment** is different, and we'd ask you not to.

Not mainly because you'd get caught, though RTOs run similarity checks and assessors notice work that doesn't match a student's demonstrated ability.

The real reason: you'd arrive at a job without the competency your certificate says you have. In these fields that isn't academic. It's the support worker who doesn't recognise a client deteriorating. The carpenter who doesn't see an unbraced frame. The cook who thinks reheating makes it safe.

Someone eventually depends on you knowing.

**Use them to learn the reasoning. Then write your own answer.**

## The hidden cost of building it yourself

Building in-house is legitimate and plenty of RTOs do it well. But the honest cost is higher than the line item.

**Currency maintenance.** Training packages get revised. Every resource you build is a resource you maintain, indefinitely.

**Consistency.** Resources built by four trainers over three years read like resources built by four trainers over three years. Validation exposes this.

**Opportunity cost.** Trainer hours formatting a workbook are hours not spent training, assessing, or maintaining industry currency.

**Audit exposure.** Resources built without a documented process are harder to defend than resources with clear provenance.

## The position

Higher education has the OfS. Schools have a reasonably clear DfE steer.

**FE sits between them, and on AI the guidance is thinner than either.**

The consequence: every college is developing its position independently. Fifty colleges producing fifty AI policies is fifty times the effort for a fraction of the quality any one of them could reach with a shared framework — and the risk sits locally, with a principal making a judgement personally.

---

## What the report covers

**Comparative analysis — twelve jurisdictions.** What's working, what isn't, and the pattern that separates them.

**The UK regulatory position.** Ofsted, DfE, Skills England and OfS — what each has said, what none has, and where the gap leaves colleges.

**Provider-level recommendations.** What an individual college can do without waiting for sector guidance.

**Sector-level recommendations.** What actually requires collective or policy action, separated honestly from what doesn't.

---

## The finding that changes what you do

The jurisdictions making genuine progress are **not** the ones with the best technology.

They're the ones that invested in **staff capability before student-facing deployment**.

That inverts how most AI initiatives are sequenced — tools first, training later, governance after an incident. And it means the constraint in most colleges isn't procurement. It's three things:

- Staff don't know what they're **permitted** to use, so they avoid it or use it undisclosed
- Experienced teachers are being asked to adopt tools their students already use fluently, which is uncomfortable and rarely acknowledged
- There's nowhere **low-stakes** to experiment, so nobody tries anything

A framework addressing those is worth more than a procurement decision.

## Where it actually stands

The domestic delivery requirement — that a provider must be delivering to domestic students before applying for CRICOS registration — has been **formally codified**, and the framework now includes provisions dealing with providers who register and subsequently don't deliver.

The position is **tighter** than before, not looser.

*[VERIFY: confirm current CRICOS registration requirements and the operation of the domestic delivery rule with ASQA before this page goes live. This is the single highest-risk claim on the page.]*

**What that means:** CRICOS is not a fast pivot. If it's in the plan, the domestic delivery clock starts deliberately, now — and the evidence needs to be genuine and documented, not nominal.

---

## Where applications actually fail

Providers anticipate the application. The application is substantial, but it's not where most failures happen.

**Evidence of domestic delivery.** Not registration — demonstrable, documented delivery of sufficient quality and duration.

**Financial viability.** Scrutiny is materially deeper than domestic registration. This surprises providers more than anything else.

**Student support infrastructure.** Welfare, accommodation, under-18 arrangements where relevant. This is genuine organisational change, not a policy document.

**Sustaining it.** CRICOS is a continuing obligation with ongoing reporting and consequences for lapses.

**The useful question isn't "can we get registered." It's "can we sustain it once we are."**
