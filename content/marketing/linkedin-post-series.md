# Series A — DRMC and Local Government (10 posts)

**Audience:** council officers, elected members, public administration academics, local government consultants
**Objective:** establish authority for the DRMC framework and the civic intelligence positioning

---

### A1 — What DRMC benchmarking actually is

Local government is where democracy leaves the press conference and becomes a footpath.

It is the only tier of government most people touch directly and repeatedly. The bin gets collected or it doesn't. The playground is maintained or it isn't. The development application is handled transparently or it isn't.

And yet the way we measure council performance is largely inherited from a corporate reporting tradition that was never designed for it. Rates collected. Kilometres resealed. Complaints closed within target.

Those are inputs and throughputs. They tell you a council is functioning. They tell you almost nothing about whether it is *governing well*.

The Dynamic Relational Model of Consciousness is a 26-domain framework for assessing local government across what actually constitutes good governance — including the things that resist easy measurement. Trust. Ethical coherence. Relational accountability. Intergenerational responsibility.

Not because those are nice ideas. Because they are the conditions under which the measurable things either work or don't.

I'll be unpacking the domains over the coming weeks.

---

### A2 — Reporting vs listening

There is a meaningful difference between a council that reports to its community and a council that listens to it.

A council that reports produces an annual report, a quarterly performance dashboard, and a community satisfaction survey with a response rate it doesn't publish prominently.

A council that listens changes something as a result.

The test is not whether consultation happened. It is whether the outcome would have been different without it. If the answer is no, what occurred was not consultation — it was notification with a comment period.

This distinction matters because it is measurable. You can trace decisions back through the record and ask: what changed, and why?

Most councils have never been asked that question systematically. It is one of the 26 domains in the DRMC framework, and in my experience it is among the most uncomfortable to assess honestly.

---

### A3 — The 26 domains

Roads, rates and rubbish is where local government accountability begins. Somewhere along the way it became where it ends.

The three Rs are genuinely important — a council that cannot maintain its roads has failed at something fundamental. But a council can deliver all three competently while:

- Making decisions no resident can trace or understand
- Excluding the communities most affected by those decisions
- Holding values in its strategic plan that its actual conduct contradicts
- Deferring costs onto residents who are not yet born

None of that shows up in a service delivery dashboard.

The DRMC framework assesses 26 domains. Some are conventional. Some — place identity, ethical coherence, relational accountability, intergenerational responsibility — are not measured by anyone at present, which is precisely why they degrade unnoticed.

You get what you measure. Which means you also lose what you don't.

---

### A4 — Community experience as evidence

There is a persistent assumption in public administration that community experience is *feedback* — soft, subjective, something to be managed rather than measured.

I'd put it differently. Community experience is evidence.

If residents consistently report that they cannot navigate a council's planning process, that is not a communications problem to be solved with a better brochure. It is data about how the process actually works, gathered by the people who use it.

The reason this gets discounted is that it is inconvenient. Service delivery metrics are produced by the organisation about itself. Community experience is produced by people outside it, and it frequently disagrees.

Treating the disagreement as a perception gap rather than an information source is one of the most reliable ways a council convinces itself it is performing well while its community concludes otherwise.

---

### A5 — Trust as a governance condition

Trust is routinely described as a soft metric. It isn't a metric at all.

It's a governance condition — the thing that determines whether everything else functions.

A council with high community trust can consult meaningfully, because people participate. It can make difficult decisions, because it is granted the benefit of the doubt. It can implement change, because compliance is voluntary rather than enforced.

A council with low trust finds every one of those things harder and more expensive. Consultation attracts only the aggrieved. Difficult decisions trigger disproportionate resistance. Enforcement costs rise because voluntary compliance falls.

Trust is not the reward for good governance. It is the operating condition that makes good governance possible.

Which is why measuring it — properly, not through a satisfaction score — belongs in any serious assessment of council performance.

---

### A6 — Relational accountability

Accountability in local government is usually vertical. Council is accountable to the minister, to the auditor, to the regulator. The lines run upward.

Relational accountability runs sideways — to the community, to affected parties, to the people who live with the decision.

The two are not the same and they are not substitutes.

A council can be fully compliant with every reporting obligation to the state and entirely unaccountable to the residents of a suburb whose character has been altered by a decision they could not meaningfully influence.

What relational accountability looks like in practice:
- Decisions traceable by someone without a planning degree
- Reasons given, including the reasons that were rejected
- Affected parties identified before the decision, not after
- A genuine mechanism for being told you got it wrong

None of that is exotic. Most of it is simply harder than the alternative.

---

### A7 — Place is not location

This is the insight that changed how I built the DRMC framework, and it came from Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander conceptions of Country.

Place is not location.

Location is coordinates, a land parcel, a zoning designation, a rateable property. Place is relationship — accumulated meaning, memory, belonging and obligation attached to somewhere specific.

Local government administers location almost exclusively. It is structurally set up to. Planning schemes, asset registers, rate valuations and service boundaries are all location instruments.

But residents experience place. Which is why a decision that is entirely defensible in location terms — this parcel, this zone, this permitted use — can be received as an act of destruction. The council answered a question nobody asked.

Assessing place identity as a governance domain is uncomfortable, because it means acknowledging that the administrative frame is not the only legitimate one.

---

### A8 — Ethical coherence

A council without ethical coherence publishes beautiful values statements as laminated fiction.

Ethical coherence is the alignment between what an organisation says it values, what it rewards, and what it actually does when the two conflict.

Every council has a values statement. Integrity, respect, accountability, community. They are almost interchangeable between organisations, which is itself informative.

The assessment question is not whether the values exist. It is what happens when they collide with something else:

- When transparency is inconvenient to a commercial negotiation
- When community consultation would delay a project with a funding deadline
- When accountability would require naming an officer

Ethical coherence is measured in those moments, not in the strategic plan. And staff read the outcome accurately every time — which is how organisational culture is actually set, regardless of what the poster in the foyer says.

---

### A9 — Intergenerational responsibility

Councils routinely make decisions with consequences measured in decades, assessed against budget cycles measured in years, by representatives with terms measured in a single term.

The incentive structure is not subtle.

Deferred asset renewal is the clearest example. Not renewing a road, a bridge or a stormwater network this year produces an immediate saving and a delayed, larger cost. The saving accrues to the current council. The cost accrues to a future one — and to residents who had no say.

Most councils now report an asset renewal ratio, which is genuine progress. But intergenerational responsibility extends further:

- Land use decisions that foreclose future options
- Climate exposure accepted rather than mitigated
- Debt structures that shift cost forward
- Institutional knowledge not preserved

The domain asks a straightforward question: what are we handing on, and did we decide that deliberately?

---

### A10 — From compliance reporting to civic intelligence

Most council performance reporting is compliance reporting. It exists because a regulator requires it, it is produced to satisfy that requirement, and its primary audience is not the community.

You can tell because of what happens to it. It is tabled, published, and rarely referenced again.

Civic intelligence is different in purpose. It exists to help a council understand itself well enough to govern better — which means it has to be capable of delivering unwelcome findings, and it has to be used.

The shift involves three changes:

**From compliance to insight.** Reporting what a regulator asks for, versus asking what you actually need to know.

**From outputs to conditions.** Measuring what was delivered, versus measuring whether the conditions for good governance are present.

**From audience to instrument.** A document produced for someone else, versus a tool the organisation uses.

That shift is what the DRMC framework is for. It is a considerably more demanding thing to do than compliance reporting, which is the main reason it is uncommon.

# Series B — RTO and VET Training (10 posts)

**Audience:** VET students, RTO managers, trainers and assessors, compliance managers
**Objective:** drive resource sales and subscriptions; establish credibility in VET assessment

---

### B1 — Why students fail assessments they should pass

A significant proportion of VET students who receive a Not Yet Competent result knew the material.

That sounds like a contradiction. It isn't.

The most common reason for an NYC is not that a student didn't know the answer. It's that they answered the **general** question instead of the **specific** one.

The assessment asks: *"A client tells you she doesn't want a male support worker. How do you respond?"*

The student writes an accurate paragraph about person-centred practice and cultural respect. Everything in it is correct. It contains no reference to this client, this preference, or what the student would actually do on Tuesday.

NYC.

Assessors are not looking for evidence you have read the material. They are looking for evidence you can **apply** it to a situation, in order, with reasons.

That gap is learnable, and it is the single highest-return thing a struggling student can fix.

---

### B2 — What your assessor is actually looking for

Your assessor is working from a marking guide. It is not a secret document, but nobody hands it to you either.

Broadly, across every training package I've worked with, competent responses share the same features:

**They apply, they don't describe.** Naming the principle is the start of the answer, not the answer.

**They cite the specific thing.** "Relevant legislation" is worth nothing. "The Residential Tenancies Act 2010 (NSW) requires seven days' written notice" is worth a lot.

**They show reasoning.** The right conclusion with no working shown reads as a guess, because sometimes it is.

**They say what they'd do, in order.** Identifying a problem is half an answer.

**They escalate and document.** These two are omitted more often than anything else, and their absence is what an assessor circles.

**They take a position.** On ethical questions, presenting both sides and stopping is not balance. It's avoidance, and it fails.

None of that requires more study. It requires answering the question that was asked.

---

### B3 — The AI course coach

We're building an AI course coach for VET students. Her name is Iris.

Here's what she does and — more importantly — what she deliberately doesn't.

**What she does:**
- Answers questions about any unit in the qualifications we cover
- Explains what an assessment task is actually asking for
- Walks you through the reasoning on a scenario
- Points you to the relevant legislation or standard
- Asks you questions back, so you arrive at the answer

**What she won't do:**
- Write your assessment
- Give you a response to submit
- Complete a task on your behalf

That's a deliberate design constraint, not a limitation we're apologising for. A student who submits AI-written work arrives in a workplace without the competency the qualification says they have — and in community services, construction, food safety or automotive work, that gap has consequences that aren't academic.

Iris scaffolds. She doesn't substitute.

---

### B4 — The hidden cost of building resources from scratch

If you're an RTO developing supporting resources in-house, the honest cost is higher than the line item.

The visible cost is trainer time. The hidden costs:

**Currency maintenance.** Training packages get revised. Unit codes change. Legislation is amended. Every resource you build is a resource you now maintain, indefinitely.

**Consistency.** Resources built by four different trainers over three years read like resources built by four different trainers over three years. Validation exposes this.

**Opportunity cost.** Trainer hours spent formatting a workbook are trainer hours not spent training, assessing, or on industry currency.

**Audit exposure.** Resources developed without a documented process are harder to defend at audit than resources with a clear provenance and review trail.

Building in-house is entirely legitimate — plenty of RTOs do it well. But it should be a decision made with the full cost visible, rather than a default because the materials are "free."

### B5 — CRICOS and the two-year rule

If you're an Australian RTO planning to move into international student delivery, the rules tightened on 5 December 2025.

The domestic delivery requirement — that a provider must have been delivering to domestic students before applying for CRICOS registration — has been formally codified, and the framework is now tighter than the previous position rather than looser.

There are also provisions around providers that register and then fail to deliver, with consequences for dormant registrations.

**[VERIFY: Confirm the current CRICOS registration requirements and the operation of the domestic delivery rule with ASQA before relying on this. Requirements have changed recently and continue to be reviewed.]**

What this means practically: CRICOS is not a fast pivot. If international delivery is part of your three-year plan, the domestic delivery clock is something to start now rather than later, and your evidence of that delivery needs to be genuine and documented.

Happy to talk through where you sit if you're weighing it up.

### B6 — Reflective practice

Reflective practice is the most consistently underused tool in VET, and it's underused for an understandable reason: most students experience it as a box to tick.

"Reflect on your practice" produces "I learned a lot and I would do it the same way next time." That is not reflection. It's compliance with a word count.

Genuine reflection has a structure:

- What actually happened?
- What did I think at the time?
- **What was I assuming?**
- What did I not notice until afterwards?
- What would I do differently, specifically?

The third question is the one that does the work. Most of what shapes a practitioner's decisions is assumption, and assumptions are invisible until you go looking for them deliberately.

Reflection isn't a soft add-on to competency. In community services particularly, it's the mechanism by which a worker notices they've started doing things *for* people rather than *with* them.

### B7 — Nine training packages, one subscription

We've been building assessment support resources across nine training packages:

CHC Community Services · BSB Business · CPP Property Services · CPC Construction · SIT Hospitality · AUR Automotive · RII Civil Construction · CUA Screen and Media · ICT Information Technology

For a student, what that actually means:

If you're doing a Certificate III in Individual Support and later move into business administration, or you're a carpenter who picks up a civil construction unit, or you're a hospitality worker adding an IT qualification — you're not starting again with a new provider and a new subscription.

VET students move between packages far more often than the sector's structure assumes. People retrain, add scope, change industries, and pick up units for a specific job.

One library covering all of it is a small thing, but it removes a friction that exists for no good reason.

---

### B8 — Assessment integrity and AI

Every RTO is currently working out its position on AI in assessment. A few observations from building resources in this environment.

**Detection is not a strategy.** AI detection tools have significant false positive rates, and accusing a student incorrectly is a serious matter. Building a policy on detection is building on sand.

**Assessment design is a strategy.** Tasks that require the student's own workplace context, their own observed practice, or a supervisor's verification are substantially more robust than tasks that can be answered from general knowledge.

**Practical demonstration is largely unaffected.** A student cannot get an AI to weld a joint, plate a dish, hang a door or take a client's blood pressure.

**The honest framing for students works better than the punitive one.** "This will be detected" is arguable. "You will arrive at a job unable to do what your certificate says you can" is not, and it's the argument that actually lands.

The resources we build are deliberately designed to scaffold reasoning rather than supply answers, for the same reason.

---

### B9 — Why writing about your practice makes you better at it

We're building a reflective journal product called Mirror. Here's the reasoning behind it.

There's a well-established gap between having an experience and learning from it. Experience alone doesn't produce competence — plenty of people do the same job badly for twenty years. What converts experience into competence is *processing* it.

Writing does that in a way thinking doesn't, for a straightforward reason: writing forces sequence and specificity. You cannot write a vague sentence without noticing it's vague. You can think vague thoughts indefinitely.

For VET students there's a second benefit that's purely practical: a reflective journal maintained during placement becomes **evidence**. Most community services and health qualifications require reflective evidence, and a student who has been writing weekly has it. A student who hasn't is reconstructing three months of placement from memory the night before it's due.

Mirror tags entries by domain so patterns become visible over time. You notice what you keep coming back to.

### B10 — From local RTO to international provider

The CRICOS pathway is genuinely difficult, and most of the difficulty isn't where providers expect it.

Providers usually anticipate the application. The application is the visible part, and it's substantial — but it's not where most applications fail.

**Where it actually gets hard:**

**Evidence of domestic delivery.** Not just registration. Demonstrable, documented delivery to domestic students, of sufficient quality and duration.

**Financial viability.** CRICOS registration involves scrutiny of your financial position that domestic registration doesn't. Providers are frequently surprised by the depth of this.

**Student support infrastructure.** International students have specific support obligations — welfare, accommodation, under-18 arrangements where relevant — that domestic delivery doesn't require. Building that is a genuine organisational change, not a policy document.

**Ongoing compliance load.** CRICOS is not a one-off approval. It's a continuing obligation with reporting requirements and consequences for lapses.

If you're considering it, the useful question isn't "can we get registered." It's "can we sustain it once we are."

# Series C — UK Further Education (5 posts)

### C1 — "The biggest risk is doing nothing"

Ofsted's early-adopter study on AI in further education put it plainly: the biggest risk is doing nothing.

That's a striking position for an inspectorate to take, and it deserves attention. Inspectorates are not usually in the business of encouraging providers toward the unfamiliar.

The reasoning is straightforward. Students are already using AI. Employers are already using AI. A provider that has no position is not therefore neutral — it has simply devolved the decision to individual staff and students, without guidance, consistency or safeguards.

That's not caution. It's an absence of governance, and it produces exactly the risks the caution was meant to avoid: inconsistent practice, unmanaged data exposure, and students entering employment either unprepared or having relied on tools they don't understand.

Doing nothing is a decision. It's just an unexamined one.

### C2 — Twelve countries, one lesson

I've spent a considerable amount of time comparing how twelve jurisdictions are approaching AI in vocational and further education.

The pattern that emerges is not about technology.

The countries making genuine progress — and the list includes some smaller systems that get less attention than they deserve — share three things:

**A national position that providers can align to**, rather than each institution developing its own from scratch.

**Investment in staff capability before student-facing deployment.** Confident, informed teachers producing considered practice, rather than tools arriving ahead of the people expected to use them.

**Governance built in at the start**, not retrofitted after an incident.

The systems struggling share the inverse: no national steer, tools ahead of training, and governance written in response to something that already went wrong.

UK FE currently sits in an awkward position — more devolved than schools, less regulated than higher education, and carrying more of the burden locally as a result.

---

### C3 — The regulatory gap

Higher education providers in the UK have the OfS. Schools have the DfE and a relatively clear steer.

FE colleges sit between the two, and on AI specifically the guidance is thinner than either.

That gap has practical consequences:

**Each college is developing policy independently.** Fifty colleges producing fifty AI policies is fifty times the effort for a fraction of the quality any one of them could achieve with a shared framework.

**Inconsistency across the sector** creates problems for employers, awarding organisations and students moving between providers.

**Risk sits locally.** Without a sector position to align to, a principal carries the judgement personally.

I'm not arguing for heavier regulation. I'm arguing that a governance framework colleges could adopt and adapt — covering acceptable use, data handling, assessment integrity, staff development and student guidance — would save the sector an enormous amount of duplicated effort.

That's what we've built. Happy to share it with anyone who'd find it useful.

---

### C4 — Skills England's AI apprenticeship units

Skills England published AI leadership apprenticeship units in April 2026, and the implications for providers deserve more attention than they've had.

Two things follow for FE providers:

**A delivery question.** If you intend to offer these, you need staff capable of teaching AI leadership — which is a genuinely scarce capability and not something a two-hour CPD session produces.

**A credibility question, and this is the sharper one.** A provider delivering AI leadership content while having no coherent internal AI governance is in an awkward position. Students notice. Employers notice. Inspectors notice.

The practical sequencing is: get your own house in order first. A college with a functioning AI governance framework, staff who use these tools competently, and a clear position on assessment integrity is in a position to teach it.

One without those things is teaching from a textbook.

### C5 — Staff confidence is the problem

Almost every conversation I have about AI in FE starts with technology and should have started with people.

The constraint is not tool availability. The tools are largely free or cheap and require no procurement decision.

The constraint is staff confidence — and specifically, three things:

**Uncertainty about what's permitted.** Staff who don't know whether they're allowed to use a tool will either avoid it or use it without telling anyone. Both are bad outcomes and the second is worse.

**Fear of looking incompetent.** Experienced teachers are being asked to adopt tools their students already use fluently. That's an uncomfortable position and it's rarely acknowledged directly.

**No safe space to experiment.** Adoption requires trying things and having some of them not work. If the only visible AI activity is high-stakes and student-facing, nobody will try anything.

A framework that addresses those three is worth considerably more than a procurement decision.

Jisc's three-pillar approach — skills and knowledge, technology, governance — puts capability first for exactly this reason, and I think they're right.
